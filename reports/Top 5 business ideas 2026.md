# Five business ideas survive, insiders win

*Final report of the business idea search. Written 24 October 2026 for Asher. Every plan date below assumes a Monday 26 October 2026 start and slides if you start later.*

After about 2,100 agent runs and about 1,770 ideas, five survived, and none is an easy win. Each is a real, fundable business with a typical outcome of roughly **$5-45M of revenue** and a rare **$100-200M upside**. None is an uncrowded, high-margin, venture-scale slam dunk, and that is the honest finding of the whole search. Every war-game ended with the same condition: start only with a co-founder who already is the insider, whether that is a nuclear QA lead, an enterprise IT-finance veteran, a former health-plan operations VP, a laundry operator or a German Meister. So the edge in late 2026 is the founder's insider position more than the idea. Each idea also has a cheap test that can kill it in two to four weeks, and no test has been run yet.

## 1. The answer in ten lines

1. **Signed-Off v2 (C10).** Buy a small, already-approved nuclear engineering firm and run it cheaper with AI-assisted, human-verified work. Median about $25M revenue, upside $110M. Kill test: a $10k bake-off that ends 13 November.
2. **Run-Base Shrinker v2 (C13).** Fund the shutdown of unused enterprise software, hosting and support, and get paid only when a vendor invoice line drops. Expect a $5-20M firm. Kill test: 14 days, about $0 cash.
3. **HCBS Operations Utility (C03).** Fixed-price 24/7 home-care operations for Medicaid plans. The war-game's verdict: narrow yes, big no. Median about $18M. Kill test: 12 calls by 6 November.
4. **Regional Partner Group (C16).** Buy family-owned uniform laundries while the families roll equity. Best evidenced of the five, but a median return of only about 8% IRR. Kill test: 25 owner calls by 20 November.
5. **Stafette (C09).** Meister-led hubs in Germany that absorb the customers and crews of closing electrical and plumbing firms. $108M of revenue would be only about EUR 6M of profit. It needs a German Meister co-founder.
6. **The one conclusion:** the edge is the founder's insider position, not the idea.
7. **No slam dunk survived.** No idea was uncrowded, high-margin and venture-scale. All five finished "wounded", with deep-validation scores of 3.0 to 3.67 out of 10.
8. **For you at 18:** none can be run solo. You can start the bake-off, the 14-day test or the call sprint. You cannot run what follows without the insider.
9. **How to use this report:** treat it as five options, run the cheapest test first, and stop on a fail. Do not shrink a failed idea into a boutique.
10. **Nothing here has met a customer.** All of it is desk research by AI agents.

### 1. Signed-Off v2: buy a nuclear engineering boutique and run it cheaper

**The pitch.** Buy a small nuclear engineering firm that is already on owners' approved-supplier lists. Keep its quality manual and customers. Then sell finished, independently checked calculation revisions and design-change packages at a fixed price per deliverable, with a discount tied to hours actually saved. AI drafts. Every number is recomputed in qualified tools, and a second qualified engineer verifies and signs. This is not an AI company and not a licensing moat. It is a roll-up with one operating twist, bought at "orphan" prices with no auctions.

**Why now.** The restart and uprate wave has money behind it. DOE announced a conditional commitment of up to $4.2B on 5 October 2026 for Vistra uprates ([POWER](https://www.powermag.com/vistra-in-line-for-doe-loan-package-to-uprate-nuclear-plants-in-ohio-and-pennsylvania/)), and a $1.9B loan closed in September for the Duane Arnold restart ([Dakota News Now](https://www.dakotanewsnow.com/2026/09/08/duane-arnold-energy-center-restart-under-19b-federal-loan/)). Operating-plant engineers are projected to grow from 6,900-7,400 FTE in 2025 to 9,200-10,400 by 2030 ([NCEA/USC](https://energyanalytics.org/nuclear-workforce/)), and on 7 August the industry told the NRC it faces a shortage of NQA-1 qualified suppliers ([NEI filing](https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML2621/ML26219A186.pdf)). Two cautions: NEI's 2025 capital spend of $7.59B is a rebound from a trough ([NEI](https://www.nei.org/getContentAsset/47fa8caa-9b0d-4029-932c-07f902e82f4f/8d8ff8d6-b2ae-401b-a63c-f6b108e809d2/2024-Costs-in-Context-final.pdf?language=en-US)), and the AI drafting speedup is public, so any rival can use it.

**Evidence of demand.** Owners do outsource. The pool is an agent estimate of **$0.7-1.2B a year**, because no published figure exists. USA Inc., the buying co-op for nine owners at ten sites, self-reported a 37:1 return on its 2025 savings program ([USA Inc.](https://usainc.org/about-us/)). But no owner has posted an RFP for fixed-price, AI-produced deliverables. Fixed-price task orders for nuclear design are normal at DOE's Oak Ridge lab, so the pricing itself is not novel ([ORNL](https://smallbusiness.ornl.gov/sites/default/files/2026-01/Architect-Engineering%20Design%20Support%20Opportunities.pdf)).

**Strongest rival.** ENERCON, owned by Arlington Capital and merged with Pond in April 2026, has 2,700+ staff, says it supports about 90% of US plants, and plans more add-ons ([Arlington](https://arlingtoncap.com/news/arlington-capital-partners-acquires-enercon-and-completes-merger-with-its-portfolio-company-pond/)). Pelican Energy Partners (GSE, EXCEL and an AI-licensing vendor) is the closest clone of the thesis ([Pelican](https://www.pelicanenergypartners.com/news/excel-services-partnership)). The war-game expects ENERCON to buy your target first, to offer the same bounded scope at a fixed price inside contracts it already holds (the most damaging move), and to bid up the scarce verifiers. Your edge is cost per accepted deliverable and surge capacity, and the war-game would not claim a structural defense. Also new: a standard engineers' liability form excludes nuclear-facility losses ([policy form](https://www.victorinsurance.com/content/dam/victor/victor2/documents/victor-us/architects-engineers/policy-forms/US-architects-engineers-policy-form-south-dakota.pdf)), and AI exclusions are being layered on top ([Independent Agent](https://www.independentagent.com/vu_resource/verisk-to-roll-out-new-general-liability-exclusions-for-generative-ai-exposures/)).

### 2. Run-Base Shrinker v2: get paid only when a vendor invoice falls

**The pitch.** A vendor-neutral company that funds and runs the retirement of unused application cost: apps, licences, hosting, third-party support and outsourced maintenance lines. It is paid a share only when a third-party invoice line actually falls, on a ledger the client's Internal Audit signs. One rule: no invoice line drops, no fee. It starts with a paid "Estate X-ray" at $75-150k, credited against later fees. Shares are 30% of verified annual reduction for 36 months where you execute, 20% on negotiation-only lines, 15% where the client co-funds, then a 10% tail through month 84.

**Why now.** Outsourced IT is repricing around savings nobody can verify. ISG's first-half 2026 index shows managed-services growth of 2.7% and the Americas down 6%, and says neither providers nor enterprises can see how AI savings will arrive ([ISG](https://isg-one.com/articles/index-insider---1h26-isg-index-recap)). The wedge is dated events: acquisitions closed 6-18 months ago, sponsor exits within 12-24 months, and end-of-support dates such as SQL Server 2016 on 14 July 2026 ([Techielass](https://www.techielass.com/sql-server-2016-end-of-support-esu-dates-pricing-and-what-to-do-next/)) and Windows Server 2016 in January 2027 ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/lifecycle/products/windows-server-2016)). The second-round critic noted that end-of-support is a price-up event: retiring a server avoids new spend and may drop no invoice, so the fee there can be near zero.

**Evidence of demand.** Buyers have the pain and weak follow-through. The City of Austin found 1,184 applications and $49-142M a year of potential savings, yet has verified only **$8.1M** ([memo](https://services.austintexas.gov/edims/document.cfm?id=472443); [committee deck](https://services.austintexas.gov/edims/document.cfm?id=471312)). A health-system CIO panel targeted 20% and realized about 5-6% ([Health System CIO](https://healthsystemcio.com/2026/07/16/application-rationalization-juice-worth-squeeze/)). Savings-share pricing exists elsewhere: Redress charges 25% of savings and nothing if none ([Redress](https://redresscompliance.com/pay-from-savings)). No RFP was found for third-party, share-of-savings application-cost reduction.

**Strongest rival.** Rimini Street has 3,132 clients and $412.8M of annual recurring revenue, and already sells the fastest invoice-line cut, lower support fees ([Rimini Q2 2026](https://www.riministreet.com/press-releases/rimini-street-announces-q2-2026-financial-operating-results/)). Its US revenue fell 1.6%, so it wants cost-cutting offers, and the war-game expects a shared-savings rider on its renewals within two quarters. Flexera, owned by Thoma Bravo, bought ProsperOps and says clients need execution, not dashboards ([ITAM](https://itassetmanagement.net/2026/01/07/flexera-buys-prosperops-and-chaos-genius-from-dashboards-to-execution/)). Your counter is to stay off lines Rimini touches and aim at the long tail, SaaS, hosting and post-merger duplicates. Your edge is funded execution to an invoice drop, which is not hard to copy.

### 3. HCBS Operations Utility: sell Medicaid plans an exceptions desk on a deadline

**The pitch.** Fixed-price, 24/7 operations for a Medicaid plan's highest-need long-term home-care members (LTSS): missed-visit response, hours-delivered reconciliation, back-office operations, and later licensed coordinators. It replaces cost the plan already carries, from administrative budget. No health plan owns it, and it takes no downstream risk. The ladder is R1 an exceptions desk at about $300 per member-year, R2 an operations stack at about $600, R3 coordinator capacity priced per seat, and R4 shared savings only later. | Month 12 after first close | Retained revenue under 70%, contribution under 15% after the 3.5% fee, or price paid above 0.4x |
| Any time | Three consecutive deals lost to ROM, HomeServe or GREENOX (a small rival buyer) at above 0.5x; stop bidding in that cluster |

**Who must be on the team.** A German-speaking electrical or heating-plumbing Meister who has run a firm of 10+ staff, has a local guild and chamber network, and can be a hub's registered managing director. A deal operator who can handle asset purchases, staff-transfer law, earn-outs and escrow. A solo founder with no German and no Meister partner cannot execute this. The war-game calls the capital side, a first-time operator raising no-exit equity at a capped 6-8% yield, the likeliest cause of death even with the right team.

**Honest verdict.** Ranked #5. Real deal flow, thin margins that depend on utilization, and strong strategic rivals. The median is about **$27.6M of revenue at roughly 3.5% EBIT**. The $108M upside is only about EUR 6M of EBIT. The author put the upside at 3-5% and the second-round critic at 1-2%. Start it only as a 12-week, under-EUR 40k deal-flow test led by a German Meister.

## 4. Three contrarian bets, clearly less validated

These are high-variance ideas that did not get the same testing. None has a war-game, a 90-day plan or named customers. Treat them as options to study.

**Anchor-Funded Independent Foundry (round 2, C05).** An independent foundry platform in the metal-castings pool, funded by anchor customers. It is the **biggest prize in round 2**: a $55M median, a $200M upside, and the best defensibility score of any contender (a 4), though its overall score was 3.0. It lost the head-to-head (8th, 9 wins of 22) because it needs **$130-175M of equity** and a proven casting operator. That is out of reach for you alone, and the first job would be finding the operator.

**Certified Cell, "Hadrian for nuclear" (round 2, near-miss from the same nuclear pool as #1).** An automated nuclear-grade manufacturing cell. It is venture-shaped, with an $18M median and a $100M upside. The evidence gate rated it weak and nobody deep-validated it. It matters as a product business next to the services business ranked first.

**Evac Cash (round 1, I0175).** Parametric cover that pays out when the policyholder is ordered to evacuate. It was the most divisive idea of round 1: the VC and contrarian judges scored it 0.91 and 0.96, and the operator ranked it last at 0.0. It was never deep-validated. Nobody has checked whether it is legal to sell, who would reinsure it, or whether the trigger can be defined cleanly. Those three questions are the whole test.

## 5. What the search learned about finding ideas

**Round 1 failed because the judges could not see market size.** The 12 round-1 finalists scored 3.3 out of 10 at best, with ceilings of $0.1-2M a year. Insider-persona ideas were 15% of all ideas but 55% of the top 100, about 3.7 times their share, while domain-research agents made 48% of ideas and only 15% of the top 100. Insider ideas read as specific, credible and feasible, and that specificity tends to come with a thin market. The early competitor check was too lenient: of 104 ideas it labeled 83 "gap" and 21 "crowded", and agents flagged a kill on only 2 of the 21. Deeper research later found an incumbent or free substitute in 11 of the 12 finalists. The rewrite step made it worse, because narrowing the wedge to survive a critique lowered the upside.

**Breeding helped, crossbreeding did not.** 75 of 120 offspring outscored their parent, and 20 of the 25 finalists were offspring. The best mutation fixed the named flaw and dodged the named competitor. None of the 12 hybrids reached the finals.

**The gate had to be calibrated once.** Round 2's first gate asked a skeptic for one "realistic" revenue number. On 80 ideas it gave 33 kills, 46 weak, 1 strong and 1 pick. The log's diagnosis: one number squeezes everything toward the median, while startup outcomes are skewed. The second gate asked for a median case and an upside case. On the next 40 ideas it gave 19 kills and 2 picks, and only 3 non-killed ideas showed a $100M+ upside, so the fix helped but did not open the floodgates. Two more lessons: pool rank predicted little (pools ranked 16-35 produced 5 picks where the top 10 produced 1), and pattern-copying ideas died most often (13 of 20 in the pilot), because someone had usually already done it.

**Operations-heavy businesses dominate because capital, licences and labor are the scarce inputs.** The log's read is that high-margin, venture-shaped openings are crowded, with funded AI-native startups and private-equity roll-ups in nearly every niche the agents found. What is uncrowded is services, pass-through revenue and balance-sheet or labor businesses in regulated money flows: large by revenue, but thin-margin and capital-hungry. My interpretation, not a tested claim: money has already flowed to margin-rich shapes, and what is left needs licences, people and balance sheet, which software investors avoid. The head-to-head found the same from the other side. Judges favored ideas whose **median case is already a real, cash-flowing business** and whose risky bet only drives the upside, which is why a nuclear boutique and a laundry roll-up beat ideas that need a customer to behave in a new way. The top scorer was the one with AI leverage on regulated, high-value work, a margin-expanding shape.

## 6. Limitations

**Adversarial-agent bias.** The critics are built to find reasons an idea fails, and the same kind of agents killed all of round 1. The log notes that great companies often look bad on paper at seed, and that judges reading three critiques plus a "wounded" verdict lean negative. The round-2 deep scores are bunched between 2.7 and 3.67 out of 10, so they cannot separate the contenders alone. A process this hostile favors boring, cash-flowing shapes and may undervalue a real venture bet, which is why section 4 exists.

**LLM judges.** Claude models did everything: Sonnet 5.5 workers and an Opus 5.5 orchestrator who made the final review alone. Such judges bunch scores near the middle and reward polished pitches, and pitch-level tournaments judge the claimed market, not the real one. In the head-to-head the two order-swapped judgments agreed 52 of 66 times (79%), and the idea read first won 59% of the time. Places 3-5 were separated by margin points alone, and one model family may share blind spots (my inference).

**Desk research only.** The war-games ran about 460 searches. Many facts come from search summaries, not full pages, and I marked those. The NRC enforcement site and several trade-association pages returned errors, so some items rest on snippets. Much of the arithmetic is agent estimation: the nuclear engineering pool, C13's 700 reachable payers, the 15% laundry margin, Stafette's 19% contribution. The upside probabilities are judgment, and the critics put them well below the authors' (1-3% against 5-8% for C10). Some contact titles are inferred, and several named executives are unconfirmed.

**No customer calls yet.** Not one named customer has been contacted. No nuclear owner has asked for fixed-price AI deliverables. No health plan has said it will pay a fixed fee. None of the ten laundries has said it wants to sell. No CFO has agreed to a share of savings. Every dated event (the 11 December merger date, the 5 January Arizona award) is scheduled and can slip.

## Conclusion

The search changed the question. It started as "which idea is best" and ended as "which insider can you recruit." Start with the cheapest tests and let them kill ideas for you: the 14-day C13 test costs about $0 in cash, the C03 call sprint $0-3k, and the C10 bake-off under $10k.

The less comfortable result is that people matter more than rankings. The war-games say a young or outside founder can enter C10 only as the capital-and-AI-operations partner behind a credentialed nuclear operator, and can do C13 only beside an enterprise insider. If a test fails, stop. The quit rules in this report are written to end an idea, not to shrink it into a smaller one.
