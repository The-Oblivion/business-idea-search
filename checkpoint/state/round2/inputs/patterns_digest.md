# Breakout patterns digest (round 2)

Mechanisms that built big companies in 2024-2026, mined from evidence by 5 research agents. Full sources: round2/patterns/<miner>.md.

## ai_breakouts

**Outcome-priced replacement of a high-volume, measurable labor function**: The vendor owns finishing the work (e.g., a resolved customer conversation) and is paid per completed outcome instead of per seat. Revenue scales with the customer's volume of work, and the budget line is operations labor or outsourced BPO rather than IT. Foundation Capital's implied target criteria: repetitive/rule-based, large existing spend, chronic understaffing, measurable outcome. *Examples:* Sierra (customer-service agents); Decagon; Zendesk / Intercom Fin (incumbent responses). *Shift:* LLM agents became able to resolve tickets end to end (authenticate a patient, process a return, reissue a card), and a countable unit of finished work (a resolution) made per-outcome pricing possible. Budget moved from IT seat licences to operations/CX labor and BPO lines (Foundation Capital sizes outsourced business-process services at ~$303B by 2027 and IT services at $1.5T in 2024). *Still open?* CX is crowded (Sierra, Decagon, Intercom, Zendesk, voice-agent vendors). The mechanism is open in other functions, but healthcare RCM/prior-auth shows incumbents buying rather than losing (R1 acquiring Humata Health Aug 2026; Waystar bought Digitize.AI). *Transfer targets:* Inference, not sourced: functions with a clear unit of finished work and large in-house or outsourced headcount, such as insurance claims intake/first notice of loss, prior-authorization and denial appeals, accounts-payable exception handling, collections, KYC/onboarding case handling, recruiting coordination. Each needs a named signer and a bottom-up path to $100M under the round-2 rules.

**Developer-salary capture via bottom-up seats that expand into the enterprise**: A product that visibly multiplies a scarce, expensive worker (software engineers, ~$1T/year global salaries per Foundation Capital). Individuals adopt on a credit card, employers buy seats once it is embedded, then agent/usage-based features raise spend per seat. *Examples:* Cursor (Anysphere); Lovable; Replit. *Shift:* Models crossed the threshold where agents write and debug real code. Menlo (Dec 2025) credits startups' shipping speed, model-agnostic design, and product-led growth; coding grew from $550M (2024) to $4.0B (2025), 55% of departmental AI spend, and startups captured 71% of product/engineering application spend. *Still open?* Fully crowded: frontier labs ship their own coding agents (general knowledge), Bessemer notes Supernova gross margins around 25% and sometimes negative, and low-price tiers retain poorly. Do not plan a new company against it. *Transfer targets:* Inference: other scarce, expensive technical workforces working in a digital artifact an agent can edit and verify, such as chip/hardware design, mechanical/CAE engineering, bioinformatics and scientific computing, quantitative research, security engineering.

**Seat price anchored to a professional's billable hour, with forward-deployed ex-professionals**: Sell to organizations whose product is professional time (law firms, in-house legal). Price per seat as a small fraction of the hourly rate, require annual commitments and seat minimums, and staff customer success with former members of the profession. Seats double within the account as usage proves out. *Examples:* Harvey (legal); Legora; EvenUp (personal-injury demand letters). *Shift:* Language-heavy professional work became automatable at acceptable quality. Bessemer: traditional SaaS 'failed to solve high-value vertical-specific tasks' that were language-heavy or multimodal; vertical AI competes for labor budgets (business/professional services = 13% of US GDP, ~10x the software market). *Still open?* Legal is crowded and the leader holds a large share of the pool. The mechanism is open in other high-rate professions. Unresolved caveat: in hourly-billing firms, efficiency can cut the firm's own revenue, so the buyer needs a reason (fixed fees, competition, in-house demand). *Transfer targets:* Inference: accounting and audit, management consulting/research, regulatory and compliance consulting, patent prosecution, actuarial work, architecture/engineering permitting. Test whether the buyer is paid by the hour (headwind) or by fixed fee/in-house budget (tailwind).

**Ambient capture of conversations to eliminate documentation labor (the voice wedge)**: Professionals spend large fractions of time documenting what they just said aloud. Record the conversation, produce the structured record the system of record needs, and sell to the organization on a measurable saving (clinician retention, billing capture, sales coaching). Bessemer calls the wedge 'often voice/audio' and cites Abridge as the safer 'tertiary workflow' entry. *Examples:* Abridge (clinical scribe); Rilla (in-home sales conversation recording for HVAC/plumbing/roofing); Epic / Microsoft DAX (incumbent bundling). *Shift:* Speech recognition plus LLM summarization reached clinical-grade reliability, and clinician burnout/retention gave health systems a budget line. Menlo shows healthcare as the largest vertical AI line at ~$1.5B in 2025. *Still open?* Clinical scribes are crowded (Abridge, Ambience, Nabla, DAX, Epic, Freed). The conversation-to-record mechanism is open in non-clinical fields where the system of record is weaker or fragmented. All ARR figures are third-party estimates. *Transfer targets:* Inference: insurance field adjusters and home inspectors, home-health and social-work case management, public-safety report writing, construction site logs, mortgage and wealth-advisory meetings. Screen each for who owns the record and how soon they could bundle.

**Free tool for a profession, paid for by the vendors who want that profession's attention**: Make a tool so useful that most of a profession uses it daily at no charge, then sell the moment of attention (the seconds while an answer generates) to suppliers whose marketing budget targets exactly that audience. Gross margin is very high because the user pays nothing. *Examples:* OpenEvidence (physician answer engine); Doximity and UpToDate (comparison incumbents); Pool-size check (eMarketer). *Shift:* LLM answer engines beat keyword search for clinical questions and the generation wait created a premium ad slot; pharma is shifting physician outreach from sales reps to digital. *Still open?* Medicine is being attacked by OpenAI (ChatGPT for Clinicians, Apr 2026) and others, revenue is concentrated in one regulated buyer category (pharma), and the digital HCP ad pool is only ~$2-3B. Pattern only works where supplier marketing budgets aimed at a defined professional audience are large. *Transfer targets:* Inference, pool sizing NOT done: professions whose suppliers spend heavily to reach them, such as trades/contractors (building-product manufacturers), farmers and agronomists (ag-input firms), dentists and veterinarians (dental and animal-health suppliers), pharmacists, technicians. Medicine is unusually rich because pharma earns very high return per physician reached, so size the supplier-marketing pool before believing it transfers.

**Selling scarce credentialed human expertise to a new concentrated buyer class (the AI labs)**: Frontier labs became buyers of a new input: expert human judgment for post-training and evaluation. A marketplace that already controls a supply of credentialed people resells their time at a markup to a handful of buyers with multibillion-dollar budgets. Revenue is gross of contractor pay (60-70% of topline), so net is much smaller than headline. *Examples:* Mercor; Handshake (former campus job board); Surge AI. *Shift:* Reinforcement-learning-based post-training needs expert-graded data, and Meta's 49% stake in Scale AI made rival labs pull work from Scale, redistributing share to Mercor, Handshake, Surge and Turing. *Still open?* Crowded at the top (four-plus firms at $0.5-2B gross) and fragile: ~90% revenue from model firms, labs considering in-house labeling, low switching costs, thin margins, and a Mercor data breach in late March 2026. *Transfer targets:* The transferable core is 'an owned network of scarce credentialed or physical-world workers plus a new buyer class with huge budgets.' Inference-only candidates: robotics/embodied-AI demonstration and teleoperation data, expert evaluation in regulated fields. Neither was researched, and lab insourcing risk applies equally.

**Best-in-modality product, self-serve first, enterprise second, priced high enough to retain**: Win on quality in one modality or task (voice, answer/search, code), launch self-serve and usage-based, and let enterprise demand arrive from internal champions. Revenue per account grows with usage. The decisive variable is price per account because retention rises steeply with price. *Examples:* ElevenLabs (voice); Perplexity; ChartMogul retention data (cautionary). *Shift:* Model-quality jumps in voice and agents plus self-serve payment let one product reach millions of users without a sales team; Menlo credits product-led growth for startups' 63% share of application spend (up from 36%). *Still open?* Horizontal consumer/prosumer AI is crowded and low-retention below $50 a month. Plan for per-account revenue above ~$250/month or contracts, not $20 subscriptions. *Transfer targets:* Inference: a function-specific tool for professionals with expense authority (recruiters, procurement, analysts, clinicians) priced above $250/month with workflow embedding, rather than another horizontal assistant.

**Buy the services firm, then use AI to rebuild its margin (the balance-sheet path)**: Instead of selling software to skeptical, fragmented service providers, acquire the providers (clients, staff, revenue) with permanent or large capital and install AI to cut delivery cost. Revenue arrives through acquisition; the bet is margin expansion, not organic growth. *Examples:* Crete Professionals Alliance (now Current), Thrive Holdings; Shield Technology Partners / Thrive Holdings; Exit-risk evidence. *Shift:* Patient private capital willing to hold services businesses long term, plus AI making cost-of-delivery gains plausible. Bessemer also notes the line between software and service 'blurs' as AI tools resemble intelligent service providers. *Still open?* Accounting and IT services are crowded with capital (Thrive, General Catalyst, PE). Exits are unproven and the revenue is bought, so it is not comparable to organic ARR. *Transfer targets:* Inference: fragmented recurring-revenue service lines with repetitive cognitive work, such as insurance agencies/brokerages, title and escrow, medical billing, property-management back offices, court reporting/legal support, claims adjusting. This is the only pattern here where a small team with outside capital can credibly reach $100M of revenue without a model-quality lead, but it needs hundreds of millions of capital and real integration skill.

**Concentrated-operator vertical platform that compounds, then widens into a system of record**: Sell a communications/workflow agent (leasing calls, maintenance requests, renewals, patient scheduling) to the largest operators in a fragmented industry, where the same labor-heavy function repeats across thousands of sites. Win a few very large accounts, then widen into adjacent workflows until the product becomes the record. Growth is slower than 'Supernovas' but retention and margins are healthier (Bessemer 'Shooting Star' shape). *Examples:* EliseAI (housing and healthcare); Rillet (AI-native ERP) - weak evidence; Bessemer Shooting Star benchmark. *Shift:* Voice and text agents became good enough for routine resident/patient interactions, giving operators a headcount line to cut or avoid; large owner-operators have central procurement. *Still open?* Housing is contested. The structure (a short list of very large operators controlling many locations) can recur elsewhere and offers the strongest durability signal in this study, but compounding is sales-heavy and slow. *Transfer targets:* Inference, pool sizing needed: industries where a few dozen operators control a large share of locations and run a repetitive customer-communication function, such as self-storage, senior living, dental and veterinary groups, auto dealer groups, hotel management companies, school districts and university administration.

**Race the bundle: land through the incumbent's system of record, then widen before it ships a good-enough native version**: Fast AI wedges often sit on top of an incumbent system of record (Epic, ServiceTitan, a helpdesk, a legal-research platform). The startup integrates, grows quickly, and must become a multi-product platform before the record's owner ships a native feature. Incumbents respond in four ways: bundle, reprice, acquire, or ally. *Examples:* Epic vs Abridge; ServiceTitan vs Rilla (bundle); Zendesk vs Sierra/Decagon (reprice); R1/Humata Health and Waystar/Digitize.AI (acquire); LexisNexis/Harvey (ally). *Shift:* Foundation models make a good-enough competing feature cheap for the incumbent to build once the category is proven. Bessemer's open question is whether vertical AI integrates with or replaces legacy systems of record. *Still open?* Not a market; it is a risk filter that applies to every idea. For each: who owns the record, how long before they bundle, and what second product exists by then? *Transfer targets:* Apply as a screen rather than a target. Strongest candidates are products that become the record themselves (Pattern 9) or that sit across several incumbents' records so no single owner can bundle them.

## global_arbitrage

**Mass-market digital bank that wins thin-file credit**: A zero-fee account is acquired at near-zero cost in a market where a few banks charge high fees and refuse thin-file customers. The account generates transaction data, credit cards, personal loans and payroll loans are underwritten on that data, and cheap deposits fund the book. Revolut's variant leads with FX/travel, then monetizes subscriptions, cards, interest and wealth. *Examples:* Nubank (Brazil/Mexico/Colombia); Revolut (UK/EU); Plata (Mexico). *Shift:* Instant payment rails (Pix), regulator-granted non-bank licenses (Mexico's CNBV licenses, Brazil's institution licenses), smartphone penetration, and open-finance data. The licensing detail is partly prior knowledge; Plata's license is sourced. *Still open?* Crowded, and the winners are going global. Nubank has conditional OCC approval for a US bank (Jan 2026); in Mexico Nu (14M customers), Plata, Klar and Mercado Pago are all funded. No gap found in the home markets. *Transfer targets:* No untouched US segment found. The US mechanism is already ported (Chime, Cash App, SoFi, Dave; prior knowledge) and Nubank itself is arriving. The transfer is geographic: countries with concentrated banks and high card APRs, which is not an obvious US-founder wedge. Unverified hypothesis: ITIN or immigrant thin-file credit in the US.

**Marketplace-owned payments and credit flywheel**: The marketplace owns logistics, payments and credit. Commerce data lets it underwrite buyers and merchants more cheaply than any bank, float funds the credit book, and free shipping drives purchase frequency. Owned logistics is the moat. *Examples:* Mercado Libre (LatAm); Coupang (Korea), logistics-only variant. *Shift:* Weak postal systems forced owned logistics, low card penetration pushed wallets and credit, and Pix cut payment costs (the Pix point is partly prior knowledge). *Still open?* Closed at national scale: the moat is logistics plus capital, with no room for a me-too. Open only in verticals. *Transfer targets:* Not the horizontal version (Amazon is the US incumbent). The transferable idea is a vertical B2B marketplace where small buyers carry 30-90 day payables and the marketplace finances the gap. Unverified hypotheses: independent-restaurant supply, independent-pharmacy wholesale, auto-repair parts. Assume someone already does each (Faire, Toast Capital, Amazon Business, Sysco/US Foods, FBN; prior knowledge).

**Control the repayment channel (payroll deduction or wallet sweep)**: The lender is repaid first, from payroll or wallet inflows, so losses and rates fall far below revolving credit. Borrowers refinance and volume explodes. The state or a telco builds the channel, and lenders compete on top of it. *Examples:* Brazil Crédito do Trabalhador (private payroll loans, launched 21 Mar 2025); Safaricom Fuliza (Kenya M-Pesa overdraft). *Shift:* In Brazil the state built an employer-agnostic platform on its payroll data (prior knowledge: private payroll loans previously needed employer-by-employer agreements; the cited articles do not describe the design). In Kenya the shift was M-Pesa's wallet float plus partner-bank licenses. *Still open?* Brazil is already crowding (Banco do Brasil and others), average ticket is falling and credit-quality alarms are sounding. In the US the category is open but legally gated. *Transfer targets:* Best mechanism found, hardest to port. The Brazilian breakout came from the state removing the distribution bottleneck, not from a product innovation, and the US has no equivalent rail. Nearest rail owners are payroll processors and large employers. Legal gate: CFPB Dec 2025 advisory opinion treats employer-offered payroll-settled no-recourse EWA as not credit; H.R. 9330 advanced 29-22 on 30 Jun 2026 and is not law; about a dozen state EWA laws, with CA/CT/MD treating EWA as credit. Installment loans repaid by payroll deduction (state small-loan and wage-deduction law) were NOT researched. No employer budget line exists, only a retention motive. US pool size not sourced. Fuliza-style auto-overdraft is already ported (Cash App Borrow, Chime MyPay, Dave, EarnIn).

**Remote-lock collateral with mobile-money collections and local-currency securitization (PAYG)**: If the customer stops paying, the product stops working, and that lockout is the collateral. Daily micropayments flow through mobile money. Each customer cohort becomes an asset-backed security funded in local currency, avoiding FX mismatch. *Examples:* M-KOPA (Kenya and 4 other markets); Sun King (Kenya, Nigeria, Tanzania). *Shift:* Mobile money ubiquity, cheap GSM lock modules, smartphones as a second product, and a Kenyan securitization market now willing to buy micro-loan cash flows. *Still open?* Two scaled players in Africa. The securitization is the proof the cash flows are bankable. No evidence found of an open gap. *Transfer targets:* Weak. US incumbents are lease-to-own and rent-to-own (Progressive, Katapult, Upbound; prior knowledge), and state lease and repossession law differs. Speculative targets only: e-bikes for delivery workers, laptops, prepaid phones. No evidence of a US-scale gap was found.

**Merchant-terminal or cash-agent network as distribution and underwriting data**: Where payments are free or thin-margin, the terminal or agent relationship supplies cash-flow data, the right to sweep settlement to repay loans, and a base to cross-sell accounts, devices and insurance. The payment itself is not the product. Lesson: dominating the rail does not make money. *Examples:* Moniepoint (Nigeria); PhonePe (India). *Shift:* UPI's zero merchant fee and its scale in India; in Nigeria a push to cashless payments (prior knowledge). *Still open?* Crowded in both home markets (OPay and PalmPay in Nigeria; Google Pay and Paytm in India). Already ported and saturated in the US (Square, Toast, Clover, Shopify, Stripe). *Transfer targets:* Avoid as a US idea. Volume does not equal revenue, since the profit is in credit and software on top of the terminal. Only possible angle: cash-heavy US segments with no software layer, which I did not size or verify.

**Dense dark-store quick commerce**: Small warehouses sit inside dense neighborhoods. Delivery labor is cheap, order frequency is high and ads help. Profit needs very high throughput per store: Zepto does ~2,140 orders a store a day against ~3,000 to break even. *Examples:* Blinkit/Zepto/Instamart (India); Getir/Gorillas/Flink (Europe), the negative proof; Talabat (UAE/Gulf), the working variant. *Shift:* UPI-based payments, very low rider cost and kirana (corner-store) substitution (the last is prior knowledge). *Still open?* India is a three-way discount war (Blinkit ~45-49% share, estimates conflict). Europe is dead. Gulf is served by Talabat and others. *Transfer targets:* Do not port to the US: labor cost and density kill the unit economics, as Getir and Gorillas showed in rich-world cities. Only the ad-monetization and private-label pieces transfer.

**Flip which side pays the marketplace fee**: Charge the side that values protection or convenience (the buyer, via a protection fee), or charge supply a small flat subscription instead of a commission. Supply keeps its margin, floods in, and gives the marketplace liquidity. *Examples:* Vinted (Lithuania, now US); Rapido (India). *Shift:* Rapido: driver anger over commissions plus a tax-ruling gap in India. Vinted: European parcel-locker infrastructure and consumer-protection norms. Both are partly inference. *Still open?* Vinted is in the US now, so a US clone is closed, and its profit fell 19% while investing. Rapido is still loss-making, and a flat fee caps revenue per ride. *Transfer targets:* Vinted's US launch is the live experiment (its CEO says next year's profit depends partly on it). Unverified hypothesis for other categories: supply-scarce service marketplaces where pros pay per lead, such as home-services lead generation, trades and per-diem healthcare staffing. The gate is contractor classification (AB5, Prop 22; prior knowledge, legal premise unverified).

**Own the supply chain, win on price**: Own the factory, ingredients or logistics, price 30-50% below incumbents to win traffic, and make money on the supply layer rather than on royalties. *Examples:* Mixue (China-origin; big in Indonesia and Vietnam); Lenskart (India); Mobile World / Bach Hoa Xanh (Vietnam). *Shift:* Manufacturing scale and automation (prior knowledge). *Still open?* Supply-margin franchising is old in the US (Domino's; prior knowledge). Mixue's own overseas retreat shows the price point does not survive higher local costs. Vertical-integration incumbents exist (EssilorLuxottica, Warby Parker, Zenni; prior knowledge). *Transfer targets:* Weak. The price-point arbitrage fails on US labor and rent. What transfers is the screening question: find categories where the US retail layer is the cost, then own the supply chain behind it. Candidate categories (eyewear, hearing aids) already have incumbents, and none were sized here.

**Instant-pay spot-labor marketplace in a shortage economy**: Remove the friction of staffing (no interview, no contract, short shifts, instant pay) so idle labor becomes just-in-time capacity for employers that cannot hire. *Examples:* Timee (Japan, TSE 215A). *Shift:* Japan's labor shortage and side-job liberalization (prior knowledge). *Still open?* Crowded in the US (Instawork, Wonolo, ShiftKey, Clipboard Health, Snagajob; prior knowledge), and Timee's own growth is decelerating. *Transfer targets:* Low. Gates are W-2 versus 1099 classification, state staffing law and workers' comp. Per-diem nursing is the biggest sub-pool and already contested. No clearly untouched US pool found.

**Mandate-created compliance rails**: A regulator mandates structured B2B invoice exchange through certified intermediaries. Every VAT-registered company must buy, on a legal deadline, and revenue is recurring per document or per seat. Accountants are the distribution channel. The payer (CFO or tax director) has a forced budget line. *Examples:* EU e-invoicing mandates (Belgium, Poland, France, Germany); Pagero (acquired by Thomson Reuters); Pennylane (France). *Shift:* Governments closing VAT gaps through real-time reporting, now an EU-wide program (prior knowledge for the motive; the mandate dates are sourced). *Still open?* Consolidating fast at the enterprise tier (Thomson Reuters/Pagero, Vertex's bid). The SME tier still has years of mandate calendar (France SMEs 2027, Germany send-side 2027-28). No reliable revenue found for Sovos, Avalara or Basware. *Transfer targets:* There is no US VAT or e-invoicing mandate, so a direct port has no mandated spend. The mechanism ports to US rules that force data exchange. Unverified hypotheses from prior knowledge, with every date to be checked: CMS prior-authorization API rule (CMS-0057-F), FDA DSCSA drug serialization, and FSMA 204 food traceability (deadline extended to 2028). Round 1 died on why-now windows that expired or arrived after 2028, so check deadlines first.

**Stablecoin and alternative rails for FX-constrained corridors**: Stablecoin settlement plus a licensed local off-ramp replaces correspondent banking. Value is captured through FX spread and float, and the fee pool compresses as volume moves onto the new rail. *Examples:* Bitso (Mexico/Argentina). *Shift:* FX-access limits in Mexico and Argentina (per Bitso executives). On the US side the GENIUS Act, signed July 2025, is NOT yet effective: it takes effect by 18 Jan 2027 at the latest, and final rules were still pending in Aug 2026 (OCC targets November). *Still open?* Crowded with Remitly, Wise, Western Union, Circle and Coinbase (prior knowledge). The pool is large but price-compressing. *Transfer targets:* $100M test, my arithmetic: if sender cost is 2-4% of the US$64.7B Mexico corridor (my assumption, not sourced), the fee pool is ~US$1.3-2.6B a year, so US$100M of revenue needs roughly 4-8% of it. US rules landing in 2027 give a timing edge to anyone building the US-side license stack. Prior knowledge to verify: a 1% US excise tax on cash-funded remittances began 1 Jan 2026, pushing senders to account-funded rails.

**Curated retail and fast brand incubation, exported to the US**: A hyper-competitive home retail market (Olive Young's curation and rotation) works as a fast feedback loop. Surviving brands export through TikTok, Amazon and Sephora to US buyers who pay higher prices. *Examples:* Korean cosmetics export boom; Olive Young (Korea). *Shift:* Social commerce and Amazon fulfillment (prior knowledge). *Still open?* Crowded and being absorbed: Olive Young is taking the retail layer itself, so intermediaries are being squeezed out. *Transfer targets:* Already a reverse port in progress. Remaining space looks brand-level (tens of millions, not $100M+) unless it becomes a retail or incubation platform. Do not call it large without company revenue numbers. Other fast-iteration Korean or Japanese categories are untested here.

## non_ai_growth

**Regulatory-gap cash-pay telehealth, then manufacturer re-entry (GLP-1)**: A drug-shortage declaration let compounders legally copy branded semaglutide/tirzepatide at cash prices far below list; a telehealth front door plus paid acquisition and subscription billing turned that into a consumer subscription business (Hims revenue +59% to $2.35B). Then Lilly and Novo built their own cash-pay channels ($149-$449/mo) and the FDA/Novo squeezed compounding, so the loophole closed in roughly 24 months; what survived was the subscriber base and marketing engine, at lower margin. *Examples:* Hims & Hers Health; Eli Lilly (LillyDirect self-pay Zepbound vials); Novo Nordisk (Wegovy cash channel; Ro as a telehealth partner). *Shift:* FDA shortage-list status for semaglutide/tirzepatide (compounding exemptions), telehealth prescribing flexibility, branded list prices near $1,000+/mo with patchy insurance coverage, and mass consumer demand. Reversed by the end of the shortage, Novo litigation, FDA threats of action on active ingredients, and manufacturers' own cash channels. *Still open?* The compounder loophole is closed and the field is crowded (Hims, Ro, LifeMD, Noom, GoodRx, Costco, Amazon, the manufacturers themselves). Still open: being the front door (prescribing, adherence, fulfilment, coaching) for manufacturer cash-pay programs and for the next drug category. Lesson from round 1 applies: the loophole was the 'why now' and it is used up; the business is the customer relationship, not the gap. *Transfer targets:* Hypotheses only, pool sizes not researched here: therapeutic areas where branded price is falling toward a cash price and insurance coverage is weak or hostile (menopause/hormone therapy, testosterone, hair loss, fertility drugs, migraine), and 'front door as a service' for pharma direct-to-consumer programs. Each needs a legal check on the exact premise before it is believed.

**Capex-supercycle pass-through to scarce physical trades**: Buyers with huge capex budgets (hyperscalers, utilities) need electrical, mechanical and civil work faster than craft labor and equipment can supply it. Contractors with labor, prefab capacity and purchasing power book multi-year backlogs, win pricing power and expand margins. The stated constraint is labor and transformers, not demand. *Examples:* Comfort Systems USA; Quanta Services; IES Holdings. *Shift:* Data-center and electrification load growth after ~15 years of flat US electricity demand, utility rate-base capex, and a thinned-out skilled trades pipeline. Demand is partly AI-capex derived, so this is AI-light supply with AI-dependent demand. *Still open?* Public contractors dominate and are buying capacity. The open space is the constraint itself: craft labor supply and training, specialty subcontracting, testing and commissioning, prefabrication, transformers and switchgear. Risk: a hyperscaler capex pullback. *Transfer targets:* Hypotheses: other funded programs with the same constraint, namely grid transmission and distribution (utility rate base pays), onshored factories, water systems, and defense-industrial construction. A founder enters through the scarce input (electrician placement and training, testing/commissioning, prefab), selling to a contractor or owner with a funded backlog. A $100M path means a few percent of a multi-billion backlog, which must be bottom-up sized before it is believed.

**Neo-primes and the defense supply-base rebuild**: A demand shock (munitions depletion, war), a budget shock and procurement reform let builders that make products to a price at volume (often vertically integrated, software-defined hardware) take share from cost-plus primes, while money also flows to a capacity-constrained sub-tier of suppliers. Government is the payer and the budget line exists. *Examples:* Anduril; Karman Holdings (public sub-tier supplier); Hadrian (automated precision-machining factories). *Shift:* Munitions depletion and the 2026 conflict context, a roughly $1T+ budget request with multi-year munitions procurement, drone/autonomy priorities and explicit support for non-traditional suppliers. Note the comptroller said the request predates the Iran conflict and excludes war costs, and the request is not an appropriation. *Still open?* The top tier is crowded and needs billions (Anduril, Hadrian, Shield AI class). The sub-tier (castings, forgings, energetics, precision machining, electronics, rocket motors) remains the bottleneck and is where a small supplier with prime pull can grow; contracts follow prime qualification, so one customer concentration risk is built in. *Transfer targets:* Hypotheses: allied rearmament budgets, critical-minerals processing (explicitly in the FY2027 request), shipbuilding sub-tiers, munitions precursors. A wedge needs a named prime that already buys the item and a qualification path, since the payer signs through the prime.

**Float and spread on payment rails without credit risk**: Hold or route money for many customers and keep a small spread (interest on reserves, interchange, FX, software on top) with little credit risk. Growth comes from a regulatory clarity event (GENIUS Act) or from software that wins the CFO; the spread is shared with whoever owns distribution. Global payroll/EOR (Deel) is the same idea with compliance as the moat. *Examples:* Circle (USDC); Tether; Ramp (and Deel as comparator). *Shift:* Stablecoin legislation (GENIUS Act, 2025) and high short-term rates for reserve income; remote-work-driven cross-border employment for EOR; corporate demand for spend controls. *Still open?* Stablecoin issuance is winner-take-most with heavy distribution costs, and spend management is crowded (Ramp, Brex, Amex, Navan). Open space is vertical flows where payers want control and compliance and nobody has built rails; revenue from float falls if rates fall. *Transfer targets:* Hypotheses, unsized: construction pay-apps and retainage, healthcare provider payments, escrow-type flows, freight pay. The test for each is the take rate net of whoever controls distribution, and who signs the contract.

**A federal licence that preempts a state-by-state patchwork**: One federal registration (a CFTC-designated exchange) lets a product that states license and tax individually launch nationally within months. Product re-labelling (event contracts, parlays) plus brokerage and app distribution produce hypergrowth; the risk is the legal premise. *Examples:* Kalshi; Polymarket (US entry). *Shift:* A pro-innovation CFTC posture in 2025-26, lawsuits against states and favourable appellate rulings (Third Circuit, Apr 2026), plus sports and election event demand. The status is split by circuit and could reverse. *Still open?* Crowded and legally unsettled. One source puts Kalshi at ~83% of CFTC-regulated volume; Polymarket is now in the US. Revenue is concentrated in sports, which is also the legal target. A founder should assume incumbents and well-funded entrants are already in. *Transfer targets:* The structural asset is preemption: federal charters or registrations that replace many state licences (payments and money transmission, trust or banking charters, certain commodity/swap products). This fails round 2's 'check the legal premise' rule until a specific statute and its litigation record are read; none is verified here.

**Legal-shock claims pool (finance and filing layer)**: A court ruling creates a large, easily quantified receivable owned by many small, process-poor claimants. A service layer files claims and a finance layer buys them at a discount (20-30 cents on the dollar was reported), taking a cut for speed and certainty. *Examples:* IEEPA tariff refund pool; Tariff-refund secondary market (hedge funds; Cantor reported). *Shift:* A Supreme Court holding that removed the legal basis for ~$170B of duties, plus a court order (CIT, Atmus Filtration, Mar 4 2026) that all importers of record benefit, combined with a government claims system that is phased and non-automatic. *Still open?* Mostly closing: it is a one-time pool, large players and brokers are active, and payment is expected within months of valid claims. Round 1's refund-exchange ideas already died to hidden incumbents, so assume competition. The reusable asset is a claims-processing factory plus a financing partner, ready before the next ruling. *Transfer targets:* Hypotheses, not researched here: other mass-claim pools with low take-up (antitrust and class settlements, interchange litigation, unclaimed property, out-of-network dispute arbitration under the No Surprises Act). Each needs a count of claimants, average claim size and an incumbent check.

**Roll-up multiple arbitrage in fragmented, recurring local services**: Buy owner-operated firms at roughly 4-12x EBITDA, add procurement, back-office and cross-sell, then sell or recapitalize the platform at 15-18x. Retiring owners without successors supply the targets; debt and PE equity supply the capital. Growth is largely acquired, not organic. *Examples:* Hub International (insurance brokerage platform); Public insurance-broker valuations (Brown & Brown, peers); PE-backed HVAC and home-services platforms. *Shift:* Cheap leverage through 2021, an aging owner generation, recurring service revenue that lenders like, and PE capital seeking yield. Multiple expansion at exit is now stalling. *Still open?* Late-cycle. Multiples are compressing, the pool of buyers is thinning (most large PE firms already own broker assets), and organic growth slowed. Entry for a small team is a search fund or independent sponsor, not a startup. A $100M-revenue path exists but needs hundreds of acquisitions or a sponsor's balance sheet. *Transfer targets:* Hypotheses, unverified: recurring, compliance-mandated services that are not yet picked over, such as fire and life-safety inspection, elevator maintenance, backflow/water testing, specialty insurance agencies and MGAs. Each needs a bottom-up count of reachable firms and an incumbent-platform check before it is believed.

**Category reinvention against legacy-brand health overhang, ending in a strategic exit**: In a category dominated by two or three giants whose core brands cannot credibly turn 'better for you', a challenger uses creator and social-proof trial, wins retail velocity at Walmart/Target/Costco, makes through co-packers and is bought by the incumbent at ~3-4x revenue. *Examples:* Poppi; Olipop; Feastables (MrBeast). *Shift:* Health-conscious consumers, social/creator distribution that skips traditional advertising, retailers willing to slot challengers, and incumbents buying growth rather than building it. *Still open?* Winners are visible and survivorship bias is large; slotting fees, working capital and retailer fights are the cost of entry; the buyer pool is a handful of strategics. Feastables' slowdown shows growth is not guaranteed. Beverage subcategories are already crowded. *Transfer targets:* Hypotheses, pool sizes not researched here: large legacy categories with a health overhang and 2-3 dominant brands (cereal, candy, snacks, condiments, ice cream, yogurt, bread, pet food). Test each by naming the likely acquirers and whether a $100M revenue challenger has already appeared.

**Expert-labor marketplaces for a buyer class with an unconstrained budget (plus offshore staffing)**: A new buyer needs scarce human judgment urgently and will pay for speed. A marketplace vets experts quickly, takes a 30-40% margin and scales on matching speed. A smaller variant arbitrages wages between the US and the Philippines or Latin America for small businesses. The business is staffing; the AI link is the buyer, not the product. *Examples:* Mercor; Surge AI and Scale AI (comparators); Offshore virtual-assistant staffing (20four7VA as an example). *Shift:* AI labs needing expert-written data at scale and urgency; remote-work normalization lowering the friction of hiring offshore staff. *Still open?* Mercor-class is concentrated and AI-dependent (three customers, 'build it in-house' risk, gross vs net revenue confusion). Offshore staffing has thousands of small vendors and low barriers, so scale is the open question. *Transfer targets:* Hypotheses: other buyers with funded urgency and scarce expertise (defense contractors needing cleared engineers, utilities and data-center builders needing power/commissioning engineers, health systems needing coders). The wedge is vetting plus speed for a payer that already pays staffing fees.

**Wrapper intermediary on pass-through flows (government resale, risk contracts)**: Sit between a large payer and many suppliers or beneficiaries, carry the contract vehicle, compliance or clinical risk that neither side wants to carry, and book the whole flow as revenue. Growth rankings and revenue size therefore overstate economic scale; the question is always the net take rate. *Examples:* Carahsoft; Monogram Health (Inc. 5000 2025 #7). *Shift:* Government cloud/cyber buying through pre-negotiated contract vehicles; Medicare Advantage and value-based contracts that pay for managed chronic-disease cohorts; vendors that want those buyers but lack public-sector sales or clinical operations. *Still open?* Government resale is a relationships-and-contract-vehicle moat with incumbents already in place. Risk-bearing care has a long failure record (risk adjustment, medical cost trend); fast growth rank is not evidence of margin. *Transfer targets:* Hypotheses: other complex buyers that vendors cannot reach cost-effectively, such as hospital group purchasing, state and local cooperative purchasing, K-12 and higher education. Always get the net take rate before sizing; a gross-revenue path to $100M is not a profit path.

**Collectibles liquidity stack: live marketplace plus authentication chokepoint**: Collectibles (trading cards especially) became a liquid asset class, which created demand for price discovery (live video auctions), trust (grading) and eventually storage and lending. The grading company is a capacity-constrained chokepoint with pricing power; the marketplace takes roughly 8-11% of volume. *Examples:* Whatnot; PSA / Collectors Inc.. *Shift:* Pokemon and sports-card boom treated as an asset class, live-video commerce habits, and trust requirements for high-value raw cards. *Still open?* The backlog is visible unmet demand for grading capacity, but Collectors is dominant and the whole stack rides a single collectibles cycle that can reverse. Marketplaces are crowded (eBay, Fanatics-class players). *Transfer targets:* Hypotheses: asset classes with price discovery but no trust layer (vintage video games, LEGO, sneakers, watches, wine). The test is whether a credible grading standard exists and whether resale volume supports fees at $100M scale.

**Home energy as a service on a project-finance stack (weak revenue evidence)**: Own the asset (a home battery), act as the retail electricity provider, and sell aggregated flexibility back to the grid, financed with equity and asset-level capital. The claim is that the retail margin plus grid discharge revenue beat selling hardware once. *Examples:* Base Power (Texas, Illinois). *Shift:* ERCOT price volatility and reliability concerns, falling battery costs, and tax-credit-driven leasing economics (credit structure not verified in this pass). *Still open?* Two states, capital-intensive and unproven at scale; nobody in this search showed audited revenue. Not replicable by a small team. *Transfer targets:* Hypotheses with no evidence yet: other distributed assets that can be owned and aggregated (heat pumps, water heaters, EV chargers). Weak, include only if later evidence shows revenue per asset and financing cost.

## policy_created

**Budget surge plus buying reform turns a software-defined challenger into a prime**: A large, politically protected budget increase meets procurement that now rewards finished products (enterprise agreements, programs of record) over cost-plus programs. Whoever already has a deployed product and engineering velocity absorbs the dollars, then vertically integrates. Incumbents with real production capacity also win, so the failure of old primes is specific to software and autonomy. *Examples:* Palantir; Anduril; Rheinmetall. *Shift:* US defense budget surge plus Golden Dome (reported as a proposed $185B initiative), European rearmament under NATO pressure, and Army-wide enterprise buying that consolidates procurement actions. *Still open?* Top layer is crowded and priced for perfection (Anduril about 27.7x revenue). More open: sub-tier suppliers (energetics, rocket motors, castings, batteries), test and evaluation, secure software supply chain. The pool is hundreds of billions, so a $100M business needs a tiny share; the real constraints are cleared staff and procurement access. *Transfer targets:* Other government pools run by cost-plus integrators where a mandate exists: state Medicaid/SNAP eligibility and payment-accuracy systems, border and customs IT, public-health data, utility grid planning. FAA air-traffic modernization is a candidate from background knowledge only, not verified.

**Policy money flows first to whoever already holds permitted physical capacity**: When the government must spend fast, it buys from operators with sites, permits and staff already in place. The scarce asset is permitted, powered, cleared capacity, not an idea. Incumbents then sell assets back to the government at a premium. *Examples:* CoreCivic; GEO Group. *Shift:* A one-time appropriation (H.R.1, $45B for detention) with a multi-year spend window and an enforcement mandate that cannot wait for new construction. *Still open?* Closed and politically toxic for detention. The transferable lesson is to pre-position permitted capacity before the budget arrives. Reputational and political risk should be an explicit gate. *Transfer targets:* Cleared facilities (SCIFs), powered land near substations, munitions-grade energetics plants, shipyard and drydock capacity, drone test ranges, IL5/IL6 cloud regions, bonded warehouses and free-trade zones, where a new budget line needs capacity that takes years to permit.

**Liability-shifting mandate becomes a per-event verification rail**: A law makes the platform liable for a fact about the user (age, identity, provenance). The platform cannot build this at scale, so it buys a per-check API. Revenue is usage-based and scales with traffic, not headcount. *Examples:* Socure; Persona; Yoti. *Shift:* Free Speech Coalition v. Paxton (June 2025), UK Online Safety Act (Ofcom deadline July 2025), Australia's under-16 social media rule, US state app-store and children's laws, an EU age-proof app in development. *Still open?* Crowded at the vendor layer (Persona, Yoti, Veriff, k-ID, Socure). My arithmetic: if all of Yoti's $43M were age checks at 1M a day, that is roughly 10 cents a check, a thin price for a $100M outcome. Chokepoint risk is Apple/Google absorbing the check into the OS or app store (background knowledge, unverified). *Transfer targets:* Any law forcing a platform to attest a fact about a user: AI-content provenance and watermarking (EU AI Act Art. 50(2), provisional 2026-12-02), non-consensual deepfake takedown, right-to-work verification under tougher immigration enforcement, AML identity checks for licensed stablecoin issuers.

**Windfall refund creates a claims-recovery and claims-financing rail (until the government ships the free pipe)**: A court strikes down a levy, creating a large refund liability with a messy claims process. Layers appear: filing services, litigation, claim purchase at a discount, and loans secured on claims. The window closes when the payer offers a free self-service portal. *Examples:* US importers / CBP CAPE (IEEPA refunds); Refund claims market (hedge funds, Seaport Global). *Shift:* A statutory-authority ruling plus a Court of International Trade order that all importers of record are entitled to refunds. *Still open?* Mostly closed for IEEPA. Residual fee-able pool is the finally liquidated entries (about $11.4B), SMB importers who never filed, and the appeal outcome. Honest verdict: the headline pool was huge but the revenue pool for a new intermediary was small and short-lived. *Transfer targets:* Pending refund disputes: Section 122 tariffs (CIT held unlawful 2026-05-07 with relief limited to named plaintiffs; Federal Circuit stayed 2026-06-16), the new Section 301 forced-labor tariffs, and any other pool where a court may create refunds. Look for a long list of payers with a protective-filing deadline.

**Regulatory permission slip creates a licensed first mover**: A regulator or court says yes to a product that was illegal or grey. The licence is the moat, the product is simple, and growth comes from distribution plus legal capital. The window closes when incumbents get the same permission. *Examples:* Kalshi; Circle (GENIUS Act). *Shift:* Kalshi's 2024 court win against the CFTC, a CFTC that now sides with the sector, and the GENIUS Act (July 2025) for stablecoins. *Still open?* Kalshi's position depends on federal preemption holding and one state supreme court could change it; sports concentration is a risk. The stablecoin first-mover window closes in about 12 months as banks arrive and OCC applications open in 2027. *Transfer targets:* Places where a licence application window opens: OCC national trust charters for stablecoin issuers (2027), reclassifications that open a market (cannabis 280E relief is a candidate from background knowledge), drone beyond-visual-line-of-sight rules, nuclear micro-reactor licences. Leads to verify, not findings.

**Mandated government IT with rich federal match: incumbents pre-empt it, but a hard dollar penalty on the payer opens a lane**: Congress imposes a rule the states must operate and funds builds at a high federal match, so demand is real. But installed integrators bid first and, in Medicaid, offered discounts to protect their base. A different lane opens where the state itself bears a performance-priced cost (SNAP error rates): a named signer with a dollar number. *Examples:* Medicaid work-requirement systems (Deloitte, Gainwell, Conduent, Maximus, Optum and others); State SNAP agencies (error-rate cost share). *Shift:* H.R.1 (Public Law 119-21) work requirements and federal-to-state cost shifts. *Still open?* Medicaid system builds are closed. SNAP error reduction is open and the buyer has a hard number. My illustrative arithmetic: 40 states with $100-200M exposure each and a vendor paid 3-5% of exposure avoided gives a $120-400M a year pool, so $100M needs a large share and attribution is hard. *Transfer targets:* Any program where Congress converted a federal obligation into a performance-priced state cost share: SNAP now; Medicaid provider-tax and state-directed-payment limits (background knowledge, unverified); unemployment-insurance and child-care-subsidy integrity. Buyer is the state SNAP director or human-services CIO.

**Subsidy becomes a tradable asset (tax-credit transfer market); the cliff splits winners from the dead**: The IRA let developers sell tax credits for cash, creating a marketplace (matching, diligence, insurance) and a financing layer. When the OBBBA phased out wind, solar and 25D, volume rushed ahead of the deadline, then segments that lost protection collapsed while protected ones kept growing. *Examples:* Crux / US tax-credit transfer market; Sunnova and Mosaic (casualties). *Shift:* IRA transferability (2022); OBBBA begin-construction cutoff of 2026-07-04 and placed-in-service cutoff of 2027-12-31 for wind and solar; FEOC restrictions. *Still open?* Storage, nuclear and geothermal keep their timelines so volume continues there. The profit pool for intermediaries is unproven. Businesses whose customer is a retail subsidy recipient die quickly when the subsidy is withdrawn. *Transfer targets:* Other tradable government instruments: CBAM certificates (sales start 2027-02-01), 45Q/45V credits, state carbon and fuel-standard credits, RINs. Also the cautionary lesson for any idea whose demand depends on a retail subsidy.

**When regulation cannot keep pace with demand, the unregulated substitute earns a speed premium**: A regulated queue (grid interconnection, permitting) becomes the bottleneck on a hot demand wave. A supplier that bypasses the queue charges for speed. The policy lag is the product, which also means reform erodes it. *Examples:* Bloom Energy; FERC large-load interconnection (policy status). *Shift:* AI data-center load growth meeting a decades-old interconnection process, with reform proposals still unresolved. *Still open?* Risky: if FERC/RTO reform speeds interconnection, the speed premium shrinks. A business built on someone else's regulatory delay has an expiry date. Capital-intensive and already attracting large players. *Transfer targets:* Other regulated queues with a customer who has a very high cost of delay: transformer and switchgear supply, small-reactor licensing, water rights, spectrum, airspace approvals, port and rail slots.

**Compliance-deadline whiplash (anti-pattern): the date moves and the vendor revenue never arrives**: A rule creates a deadline and a flood of readiness vendors. A later political decision suspends, narrows or delays the rule, and the budget line vanishes. Forced spend is only real if someone enforces it. *Examples:* CMMC Phase 2 (US defense supply chain); CSRD sustainability-reporting vendors; EU AI Act and EUDR delays. *Shift:* Simplification and omnibus politics in the EU, and a US administration that reviews or suspends rules it did not write. *Still open?* A filter, not a market. Counting a deadline as forced spend requires (a) a statutory penalty with a named enforcer who has budget and is already acting, (b) the rule not sitting inside an active simplification or review process, and (c) the buyer losing revenue or a licence, not just reputation, by missing it. *Transfer targets:* Apply the filter to the next-wave list: EU Cyber Resilience Act reporting (in force 2026-09-11, enforcement posture unverified), EUDR 2026-12-30, EU AI Act Art. 50(2) 2026-12-02, CBAM certificate sales 2027-02-01, California CCPA ADMT 2027-01-01. SNAP cost share and Medicaid work requirements pass the filter; CMMC and CSRD currently do not.

**Price-control deal reroutes demand into a manufacturer-controlled channel; intermediaries get squeezed**: Government pricing pressure (negotiation, most-favored-nation) makes manufacturers open cash-price channels that bypass insurers and PBMs. The party controlling supply keeps the value; distribution partners are paid as marketing, and a refund-timing float problem lands on pharmacies. *Examples:* TrumpRx / MFN deals; Hims & Hers; Medicare drug price negotiation (Medicare Transaction Facilitator). *Shift:* IRA negotiation program plus MFN executive deals; TrumpRx is more fragile than it looks because it rests on voluntary agreements. *Still open?* Intermediary margin is thin and being competed away (Amazon One Medical entered GLP-1 the same week as the Lilly news). The pharmacy float problem is real but small per pharmacy, not a $100M business on its own. *Transfer targets:* Markets where policy forces price disclosure and a manufacturer-run direct channel, such as Part B drugs entering negotiation for 2028 (up to 15 more drugs). Other device or hearing categories are speculative and unverified here. Ask who controls supply and who bears the working-capital gap.

**Government exits as lender or payer; private capital fills only the creditworthy slice**: When a federal loan or payment program is capped, a funding gap appears. Private lenders take the bankable slice at higher prices, leaving a residual of able-to-repay but thin-credit borrowers where a new underwriting model could win or lose. *Examples:* Graduate lending after Grad PLUS elimination (SoFi, Sallie Mae, Earnest); Workforce Pell training providers. *Shift:* OBBBA caps on graduate and professional federal borrowing. *Still open?* The bankable slice is contested by SoFi, Sallie Mae, Earnest and others. Bottom-up check (my arithmetic): $8B of annual originations at about 10% interest is about $0.8B a year of interest income at 100% share, so about 12% of the gap yields $100M, which is heroic. Credit risk and limits on outcome-based contracts are the main caveats. *Transfer targets:* Other places the federal role was capped with a dated start and a quantified gap: Parent PLUS limits and state-directed-payment phase-downs for hospitals (both background knowledge, unverified).

**Rule volatility becomes a permanent compliance data layer (customs and trade)**: Frequent changes in tariff authority, rates and origin rules force every importer to repeatedly classify, value and document goods. The volatility itself is the recurring service, and it extends globally as other jurisdictions copy the moves. *Examples:* US tariff regime churn; Flexport and Zonos. *Shift:* Executive-branch tariff experimentation after the IEEPA ruling plus matching EU moves (low-value parcel duty, CBAM, EUDR). *Still open?* Crowded (Flexport, Zonos, Avalara, Descartes and others) and revenue proof for the software slice is thin. CBAM free allocation is 97.5% in 2026, so the near-term net cost is small and weakens spend (sources conflict on 2027). *Transfer targets:* Dated EU import rules: CBAM (definitive period since 2026-01-01; certificates on sale 2027-02-01; first declaration for 2026 imports due 2027-09-30), EUDR (2026-12-30; geolocation data required; check for a second delay), and forced-labor supply-chain tracing.

## too_early

**Autonomy crosses the labor-substitution threshold (safety proof plus patient capital, not just a better algorithm)**: A wage-heavy task becomes automatable only when two things hold at once: the model works inside a bounded environment AND the operator can prove safety to regulators, insurers and buyers. The 2016-2020 startups had the idea but not the proof or the capital to wait. Winners geofence, validate relentlessly, and launch driverless operations only where the proof exists, funded by a cash-rich parent or public markets. *Examples:* Waymo (Alphabet); Aurora Innovation (driverless trucks); Starsky Robotics (dead 2020). *Shift:* A decade of simulation and fleet data, a patient funder (Alphabet) and city-by-city regulatory permission (2020-2025); hardware kit cost roughly halving per generation. *Still open?* Robotaxi is consolidating around Waymo. Trucking is open but proof-heavy and extremely capital-intensive (Aurora's quarterly loss is ~130x its revenue). Not a bootstrap pattern. *Transfer targets:* HYPOTHESES, unsized: bounded-environment labor pools where a driver/operator is the dominant cost and the buyer already pays by the hour/mile/load: port and terminal tractors, rail and logistics yards, airport ground handling, quarry/mine haulage (OEMs already compete), farm field operations, fixed-lane middle-mile freight. Test: can the environment be fenced, and does the buyer have an hourly budget line that disappears?

**A regulator's 'yes' converts a banned or capped product into a legal one; the first licensed player owns the category**: Demand and technology already existed; the only blocker was legal permission. When a regulator, court or statute grants it, the first compliant, licensed entity with distribution captures a category that already had customers. Main risk: permission stays contested (state suits, delayed rules). *Examples:* Kalshi (Intrade dead 2013); Circle (Basis dead 2018, Diem dead 2022); Zipline (unlock still pending). *Shift:* A statute (GENIUS Act, 2025), an exchange designation plus litigation (Kalshi, CFTC-regulated since 2020 per Sacra), a rulemaking (Part 108, pending). *Still open?* Prediction markets: open but getting crowded (Polymarket US, Robinhood) and legally fragile because sports are ~80% of volume and states are suing. Stablecoin issuance is dominated by Circle/Tether; open space is the rails around it. Drones: pre-unlock. *Transfer targets:* HYPOTHESES: categories where a named regulator has signaled yes but only one or two licensed entrants exist (regulated event contracts on non-sports data; tokenized versions of existing securities/treasury products; B2B cross-border flows on dollar tokens; anything waiting on a federal rule with a published deadline). Price the legal premise first (lessons.md item 4): a state-by-state fight can erase the pool.

**A government payer code or compliance mandate creates the budget line a product never had**: Products that work but have no payer die. When government creates a billing code or a legal duty, it creates a buyer with a budget line. Risks: codes are barely used, or mandates are unenforced or delayed. *Examples:* Pear Therapeutics (dead 2023) / CMS digital mental health treatment codes; Age-assurance vendors (Persona, k-ID, Yoti). *Shift:* A dated administrative act (CMS final rule) or court ruling/statute creating a payer or a duty. *Still open?* Age assurance is already crowded with funded vendors, falling per-check prices and weak enforcement; payer-code products show little revenue. Weakest pattern for a $100M bottom-up path unless a mandate comes with enforcement and a fixed fee per user. *Transfer targets:* HYPOTHESES to verify: other new mandates with a deadline, a fine schedule and a named compliance owner (e.g., CMS prior-authorization API requirements running into 2027, e-invoicing mandates in Europe; both UNVERIFIED in this run). Check each rule's date, penalties and delay history.

**A scarce licensed asset is re-rated when a new use-case arrives**: A license (spectrum, permit, slot, right-of-way) is worth little under its old use and a lot under a new one. First owners fail or sit on it; the asset is later sold or leased at a multiple to whoever has the new platform. *Examples:* EchoStar to SpaceX (LightSquared dead 2012); AST SpaceMobile (Ligado L-band). *Shift:* Cheap launch and large phased-array satellites made space-to-phone service viable; the FCC reframed the band's use. *Still open?* The big spectrum prizes are taken (SpaceX, AT&T, AST). The pattern stays open for any scarce license whose best use has changed. *Transfer targets:* HYPOTHESES: stranded licensed or permitted assets whose best use shifted due to AI power demand or electrification: grid-interconnection positions at closed power plants, brownfield sites with transmission access, water rights near industrial clusters, rights-of-way/conduit, airport and port slots. A buy-side aggregator or broker with a data edge is a services-to-asset wedge. Needs pool sizing and a transferability legal check.

**A cost input falls ~10x because someone else scaled; the owner of the cost curve captures the category**: A category looks absurd at 10x the cost, and first-wave startups die on the capital and launch bill. A different player drives the input cost down for its own reasons and enters the category already holding the cheap input. *Examples:* Starlink / SpaceX (OneWeb dead 2020, Teledesic halted 2002); Grid-scale batteries (BNEF). *Shift:* Reusable rockets (SpaceX, background) and Chinese manufacturing overcapacity in cells (2023-2025). *Still open?* Satellite broadband is effectively closed to new entrants. Storage is open for integrators and software, but overcapacity-driven price collapse thins margins for Western manufacturers, and round 1 already found energy niches too small. *Transfer targets:* HYPOTHESES: look for finished-goods categories uneconomic only because one input is ~10x too dear, where that input already falls on someone else's curve (sensors, batteries, compute, actuators, satellite bandwidth); be the integrator/distributor downstream, not the upstream manufacturer. Candidate pools to size: storage-plus-load integration for commercial buildings and data centers; any service priced per kWh or per GB that could be cut ~80%.

**A deep-pocketed buyer's demand shock (AI buildout) re-rates a stalled supply technology**: When a buyer with enormous capex hits a hard bottleneck (power, inference speed), it pays a premium for any supplier who can deliver now. Technologies stuck in pilot mode for a decade suddenly have a customer whose problem is speed, not price. *Examples:* Bloom Energy (founded 2001); Fervo Energy (geothermal); Cerebras and Groq (Graphcore as the loser). *Shift:* The 2023-2026 AI buildout made power and inference speed the binding constraints for hyperscalers. *Still open?* Open at the component/bottleneck level but capital-heavy and concentration-risky. Bloom's backlog is mostly cancelable service; Cerebras depends on a few buyers and has thin GAAP margin. Everything rides one industry's capex cycle. *Transfer targets:* HYPOTHESES: the payer is a hyperscaler with a named capex budget. Next bottlenecks behind power and chips: electrical gear and transformers, cooling, on-site construction labor, interconnection studies and permitting, water, optical/fiber links. Accessible small-team wedges are services/software around these (permitting, interconnection, commissioning, skilled-trade staffing for data-center builds). Verify with capex data in the pools stage.

**A sovereign or infrastructure-capital backstop makes a capital-heavy, uninvestable industry bankable**: Strategic or capital-intensive industries die from price risk or cost of capital, not technology. A price floor, offtake, equity stake or standardized financing structure turns the business into a financeable contract and the customer's capex into a service fee. *Examples:* MP Materials (rare earths); Brookfield with Bloom Energy. *Shift:* US industrial policy for critical minerals (2025); infrastructure funds seeking AI exposure. *Still open?* Open, but it rewards firms that can structure and operate, not idea founders; revenue so far modest (MP ~$108.5M a quarter). *Transfer targets:* HYPOTHESES: where a government or large financier is publicly writing floors, offtakes or financing frameworks (critical minerals processing and magnets, grid equipment, munitions, shipbuilding inputs). For a small team the accessible layer is origination: underwriting, project data, servicing and insurance between equipment makers and infrastructure capital, since capital providers have mandates but few good deal flows (inference). Check each program's budget and eligibility.

**A closed buyer reforms its purchasing, and a product-first, self-funded vendor wins**: Defense and other closed procurement systems favored cost-plus incumbents. When the buyer changes the rules (commercial-first buying, flexible contract vehicles), a vendor that builds the product with its own money and sells it as a finished product can win contracts in months instead of years. *Examples:* Anduril (founded 2017); DoD acquisition reform (the unlock itself). *Shift:* Procurement reform (2025) plus wartime-pace demand signals; DIU/OTA groundwork since 2016. *Still open?* Capital is flooding in (record H1 2026), so valuations are stretched and founders chase the same subsystems; the unserved edge is the supply-chain and operations layer behind the hardware. Startup share of spend is still ~1%, which means room but also proof that spending is slow to move. *Transfer targets:* HYPOTHESES: other closed buyers with a reform or new contract vehicle and a large budget: border and homeland-security technology, Coast Guard and shipbuilding, state/local public safety and emergency management, public transit and utilities procurement. Small-firm wedge: compliance, program capture and supplier services; verify which budgets are actually appropriated.

**A consumer hardware form factor works when a category-owning incumbent supplies brand, channel and manufacturing**: New devices fail when they are ugly, expensive, short on battery and short on a reason to exist. The unlock was packaging a familiar object with just enough new function and shipping it through the incumbent that owns the category's brand and stores. *Examples:* Meta with EssilorLuxottica (Ray-Ban, Oakley); Google Glass, Intel Vaunt, North Focals (the dead). *Shift:* Good-enough voice AI, camera and audio in a normal frame, and a fashion brand with retail reach (my synthesis; the sources give numbers and failures but do not themselves explain the success). *Still open?* Closed for startups on glasses (Meta and EssilorLuxottica hold the channel). Open as a template: tech plus a category owner. *Transfer targets:* HYPOTHESES: categories with a dominant physical-retail brand and a clear sensing/AI addition (eyewear, hearing, fitness equipment, watches, dental and optical retail). A small company's route is to be the software/data partner to a category owner rather than shipping its own hardware.

**Labor-line conversion: human-in-the-loop services that died on labor cost become outcome-priced software once language models exist**: Services that needed humans to finish the job lost money at scale. Language models cut the marginal cost of the human part, so a vendor can price per resolved outcome against the customer's existing labor or outsourcing budget, a large already-budgeted pool. *Examples:* Sierra (founded 2023); Decagon; Facebook M (dead 2018); Olive AI as warning. *Shift:* LLMs from late 2022 onward, plus outcome-based pricing as a business model. *Still open?* Customer service is crowded (Sierra, Decagon and others). Other labor lines are less crowded but need domain depth and regulatory handling. *Transfer targets:* HYPOTHESES needing pool sizing: language-heavy, measurable-outcome, compliance-bounded back-office labor that is outsourced today and run by people on phones and documents: insurance first-notice-of-loss and claims intake, healthcare denial appeals and prior authorization (CMS API mandates give a deadline), freight brokerage carrier check-calls, property-management leasing and maintenance dispatch, collections and KYC operations. **ANTI-PATTERN: False dawn, where capital arrives before the paying customer**: A claimed unlock (new models, new hardware) produces huge rounds, but customers are still piloting and press treats pilots as demand.
