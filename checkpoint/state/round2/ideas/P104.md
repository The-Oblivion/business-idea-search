## 1. Slot Book (insider: airline MRO procurement director)

**One-liner.** Sign take-or-pay capacity blocks at independent engine shops and resell guaranteed-window shop visits to the operators too small to command a slot.

**Earned secret.** In a capacity-short market the scarce asset is a shop's ability to plan against a commitment. Slots go to whoever brings forecastable volume. A 30-aircraft airline's engine removals are lumpy (an unscheduled removal arrives with days of notice), so it gets leftovers, and its contract turnaround remedies are weak, so it pays twice: a cover engine plus an extended lease (Willis: airlines sign ~3-year leases for shorter needs). (U) Pooled demand from ~100 such operators has the smoothness one operator cannot offer. The tell that this is the real currency: lessors are now buying capacity through multi-year agreements (StandardAero-AviLease, Mar 2026).

**Customer / payer / signer.** Mid-tier airlines (20-80 aircraft, mostly 737NG/A320ceo, some LEAP/GTF) and small lessors. Budget lines already exist: engine MRO plus the engine-lease line that pays for cover. Signer: VP Technical Operations or Director of Engine Asset Management (lessor: Head of Technical). Reservation fee (10-15% of expected visit value, A) escrowed at booking, balance at release.

**Product.** 12-24 month take-or-pay blocks at 6-10 independent shops, resold as "visit rights" that are fungible across customers and workscopes inside the block (swap, defer, upgrade), with a guaranteed induction window, a kit-readiness plan (idea 5) and an optional pre-priced cover engine from partner lessors (idea 7).

**Why now.** Slots are the scarcest input and the buying pattern has just moved to forward capacity reservations; scarcity is forecast to persist through 2030 (Bain) and to worsen as LEAP and GTF visits climb (IATA).

**Wedge.** CFM56-7B and -5B, because 40+ shops and an independent majority mean many counterparties (and shops with open stands). First 10 customers: 737NG/A320ceo operators and small lessors in Latin America, Eastern Europe, India/Southeast Asia and Africa. First supply: a 60-visit pilot book at 2-3 second-tier shops, not StandardAero.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** Take of ~4.5% of visit value (reservation spread plus 3-5% commission, A) is ~$200k per $4.5M visit. $100M needs ~500 visits a year, ~$2.25B booked. That is 11% of all narrowbody visits and ~26% of mid-tier visits (A), which is heroic. It becomes ~8% of mid-tier spend only if the book also covers widebody and regional engines and airframe heavy-check, landing-gear and APU slots (A: ~$28B mid-tier slot-constrained spend). Verdict: works only if the book spans more than narrowbody engines; kill it if mid-tier engine-only share cannot reach ~15%.

**Who does it today.** Lessor capacity agreements (StandardAero-AviLease); engine-management firms that run RFQs and monitor shops (AJW Group: shop control, work-scope designation, advance material purchasing; Acumen: engine management, invoice review, reserve-claim analysis); Sanad (end-to-end engine services and asset management); Willis Lease (own module shop). (U) OEM LTSAs bundle priority for big customers. I found no neutral capacity aggregator for the mid-tier.

**Why they lose.** Shops sell direct but hate lumpy demand; engine-management firms are agents with no balance sheet and no inventory of slots; lessors only book capacity for their own assets; OEM LTSAs are bundled and non-fungible across customers.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Scale economies (portfolio smoothing earns better slot terms, which attracts more operators), counter-positioning against bundled LTSAs, and a cornered resource in multi-year capacity blocks.

**Legal premise.** Contract capacity, not a licensed activity; operator keeps airworthiness responsibility and shop keeps its ratings. Check take-or-pay exposure, assignment and subcontracting limits in OEM licenses (U), and buyer-pool antitrust (mild).

**First 90 days.** Interview 20 shop sales heads and planners; sign LOIs for ~30 CFM56-7B slots with each of 3 shops at a take-or-pay discount; pre-sell 40 visit rights to 8 operators as paid reservations; model the cancellation risk from 24 months of operator removals.

## 2. The Repair Library (insider: FAA DER / engine repair engineer, 15+ years)

**One-liner.** Own a library of FAA/EASA-approved repair schemes for engine piece parts, with field-evidence behind each, and run automated repair cells that use it.

**Earned secret.** Scrap versus repair is decided by whether an approved repair scheme exists for that damage limit, not by metallurgy. Schemes are OEM-owned or hoarded by one shop as a competitive edge, so the same weld is re-qualified again and again. What regulators, lessors and insurers actually accept is field evidence: the FAA's SAIB NE-13-33R2 and an EASA directive on DER-approved repairs of CFM56-5 stage 1 LPT nozzle segments (increased variation in the cooling features) show what happens without it. (U) Scrap rates on high-value airfoils run in the tens of percent; the one expert-call source I found on CFM56 airfoil scrap rates was paywalled, so verify in the first interviews.

**Customer / payer / signer.** Independent engine shops and lessor module shops (customers of repairs), lessors (who can require approved schemes in workscope clauses). Signer: VP Engineering or VP Materials at the shop; lessor Head of Technical for the specification.

**Product.** (1) An approved-scheme library (14 CFR 43.13 and Part 21 major-repair data via DERs; EASA Part 21 Subpart M for Europe), starting with warm- and cold-section CFM56-7B/-5B and V2500 parts that already have DER precedent. (2) Per-part royalties from licensed shops. (3) Own automated cells (laser cladding / directed-energy deposition, robotic blending) in 2-3 hubs near shops, with CT or flow-test data stored for every repaired part so each repair builds field evidence.

**Why now.** The bottleneck is parts: Bain names missing piece parts as the main driver of slow turnaround and says OEMs issue too few repair licenses; IATA asks for faster repair development and licensed production; IATA's CFM agreement was renewed in Jan 2026 and covers regulator-approved non-OEM parts and repairs; HEICO says repair is supply-constrained and the OEMs are racing to do it themselves (Pratt's additive GTF repair cuts process time 60%+ and expects to recover $100M of parts over five years; GE Singapore is approved for additive component repair).

**Wedge.** 5 high-scrap, high-volume parts on CFM56-7B with existing DER precedent; 2 independent shops as licensees; first 10 customers are second-tier CFM56 shops, lessor module shops (Willis, AerFin-type) and one airline in-house shop.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** (A) Engine MRO $50.7B x ~55% materials = $28B; piece-part repair ~20% of materials = ~$5.6B; independent-addressable ~40% = ~$2.2B. Year-5 mix: own cells bill ~$65M (1.2% of $5.6B) plus royalties of ~$35M (17.5% on ~$200M of licensed repair value, 3.6% of $5.6B) = $100M. Licensing alone would need ~$570M of repair value, ~26% of the independent-addressable pool, so the cells are required. Capital: 3 hubs at $25-40M each (A). R&D: ~150 schemes at ~$300k each (A). Pratt's single additive program (~$20M a year of recovered parts, S) shows the scale of one scheme; the model needs about five of those.

**Who does it today.** Chromalloy (DER repairs on CFM56 hot-section parts), GKN Aerospace (multi-year P&W repair contracts across 20+ component types), HEICO (repair and DER/PMA, Wencor), Jet Parts Engineering (PMA and DER, mostly outside the hot section), the OEMs' own programs (P&W additive, GE Singapore), shops' in-house schemes.

**Why they lose.** OEMs earn more on a new part than a repair; licensed shops will not license their schemes to rivals; HEICO optimizes for PMA parts and its own capacity; a neutral library sees field data from every shop and so becomes the standard others cannot match.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Cornered resource (approved data plus the evidence database), process power (repair-cell know-how), switching costs (shop qualification).

**Legal premise.** Major repair data must be approved (FAA or DER; EASA design approval); OEM manuals are proprietary, so a DER must substantiate independently (cost and liability, see the SAIB); (U) EAR export classification of engine technical data; OEM license terms (CFM says its licences are non-discriminatory and that its repair instructions do not apply to non-OEM parts).

**First 90 days.** Hire two DERs and a materials engineer; pick 5 parts using scrap data from 3 shops; run a 100-part pilot with CT/flow data; show the output to 3 lessors and 2 shops; get one lessor to name approved schemes in a workscope clause.

## 3. Fleet Buying Group (pattern: healthcare group purchasing organization)

**One-liner.** A buying group for aircraft maintenance, paid by vendors, that pools mid-tier volume and uses it to fund PMA offtake for the part numbers nobody develops.

**Earned secret.** (U) The price of a sole-source OEM part reflects the buyer's volume tier and aircraft-purchase concessions, not cost, so the mid-tier pays more because it negotiates alone. PMA developers choose part numbers by OEM price times visible volume; mid-tier volume is scattered and invisible, so parts HEICO skips never get developed. A pool can pre-commit offtake the way a hospital system commits volume.

**Customer / payer / signer.** Members: mid-tier airlines, small lessors and independent MROs (parts, component repair, consumables, ground support). Payer: vendors pay admin fees of 1-3% of purchase price (Premier benchmark) plus a member fee for benchmarking. Signers: VP Supply Chain / CFO at members; chief commercial officer at vendors.

**Product.** Year 1: price benchmark from members' PO data (top 200 part numbers). Year 2: pooled RFPs on rotables, expendables and component repair with rebates. Year 3: offtake letters that fund PMA/DER development for chosen part numbers, with the group sharing the savings. Fits IATA's third ask (predictable spare-part pricing through contracts).

**Why now.** Oliver Wyman: material inflation ran 100-200 bp above expectations in 2025 and more is expected; ~90% of surveyed firms report tariff effects; the Section 232 decision window ends ~Jan 5 2027. IATA made price transparency and non-OEM options a priority and renewed its CFM deal in Jan 2026. PMA adoption has historically been low (2.1% of parts deliveries in 2006, a projected 3.9% by 2011, S), so volume commitments have room to move share.

**Wedge.** 6 founding members of 30-80 aircraft (mainly 737NG/A320ceo) plus one lessor. First product is the price benchmark ("you paid 18% above the pool median on 40 part numbers"), which is free to the member and creates the vendor conversation.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** Fee 2.5% (A, within Premier's 1-3%) means $4B of managed spend. (A) Mid-tier parts spend is ~$16-20B (40-50% of the $40B+ claim, U), so $4B is 20-25%; counting component repair ($20B, Straits) and line services brings the denominator to ~$30-35B and the share to ~12-13%. Heroic but it is the only model here where revenue scales with volume without owning inventory.

**Who does it today.** SkySelect (AI procurement, 3,000+ suppliers, $9M raised Mar 2026), ePlane, PartsBase, Locatory and others (parts marketplaces), AVIAA (business-aviation GPO), alliance-level joint purchasing among airlines, bilateral deals like the Satair-CASC bulk-purchase MOU, IATA's MRO SmartHub PMA tool, AJW and AAR (independent supply).

**Why they lose.** Marketplaces automate search and RFQs but hold no pooled commitments and no contracts; alliances are airline clubs with slow cycles; OEM-owned distributors cannot push PMA or USM against their parent's margin; no one has the volume visibility to fund PMA offtake.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Scale economies (volume improves terms, which attracts members), switching costs (rebates and embedded contracts), a data asset (cross-airline price history).

**Legal premise.** Buyers' groups are normally judged under the rule of reason; (U) the FTC and DOJ withdrew the 2000 competitor-collaboration guidelines in Dec 2024 and the EU's 2023 horizontal guidelines give a joint-purchasing safe harbor at ~15% share. The healthcare GPO safe harbor does not apply to aviation. Vendor-paid fees need disclosure to members. OEM most-favored-customer clauses may cap OEM willingness to discount.

**First 90 days.** Recruit 6 founding members and sign data-sharing agreements; ingest 24 months of POs; publish a private benchmark for each; run one 40-line pooled RFP; get a PMA developer to sign a non-binding offtake letter for 10 part numbers.

## 4. Graded Parts (pattern: collectibles authentication plus flipping who pays)

**One-liner.** An independent grade, escrowed settlement and an indemnity for used and non-OEM aircraft parts, so a buyer can prove a part is what the paperwork says.

**Earned secret.** (U) A Part 145 quality manager rejects a few percent of USM lots on paperwork alone, and most are curable. The real cost of a fake is not the part but the aircraft on the ground, the removal of every part from that seller, and the regulator, so buyers will pay for indemnity against consequential removal if a neutral party grades the part and an insurer can price it (as Vinted charges the buyer for protection and PSA grades cards).

**Customer / payer / signer.** Buyers of USM and distributor-sourced material: airlines, MROs, lessors; lenders and insurers who want a standard. The buyer pays (the protection fee), not the seller. Signer: VP Quality or Director of Materials; at lessors, Head of Technical. Insurer and lender mandates are the lever.

**Product.** Physical inspection by contracted Part 145 stations plus document verification with the issuing organization, then a grade (A/B/C), escrow release on grade, and an indemnity underwritten by an aviation insurer (removal, replacement, aircraft-on-ground cost). Start with CFM56/V2500 accessories and LLP stubs where AOG Technics-type fraud occurred.

**Why now.** AOG Technics (forged certificates on ~50 CFM56 parts; parts found on 126 engines at Delta, United, Southwest, American, Virgin Australia) made provenance a board topic; CFM is suing in London and the SFO has charged a director; USM is up to 30% of material at some shops (Bain), so exposure is rising. There is no mandate for digital certificates, so demand comes from buyers, lenders and insurers.

**Wedge.** CFM56-7B and V2500 accessories and LLP stubs sold by teardown houses; first 10 customers: 5 teardown sellers who want a premium for graded stock and 5 shops that buy USM heavily.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** (A) Non-OEM-direct flows ~$20B (half of $40B+, U; USM alone $6.2B per the pool file). Fee: grade plus escrow 1.5% plus indemnity premium 1.0% = 2.5%, so $100M needs $4B of graded flows, ~20% of non-OEM-direct. Heroic: this only works if lenders, insurers and the airline coalition adopt the grade as the standard (the PSA path). If it cannot reach ~15% within five years, it is dead.

**Who does it today.** Aeroxchange (digital 8130-3 exchange; Boeing/Southwest pilot), Dust Identity (publishes on 8130-3 fraud; product not verified), the Aviation Supply Chain Integrity Coalition (Airbus, American, Boeing, Delta, GE Aerospace, United: keeps unapproved parts out, not an underwriter), Honeywell GoDirect Trade and the parts marketplaces (documents only), shops' own incoming inspection.

**Why they lose.** Document exchanges verify paper, not parts; marketplaces earn from sellers and will not alienate them; the coalition's job is prevention, not indemnity; no one holds inspection, escrow and underwriting together.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Cornered resource (independent grade standard plus claims history), branding (trust), network effects (sellers must be graded to reach buyers).

**Legal premise.** A grader must not issue return-to-service documents unless it is a Part 145 holder, so inspection is subcontracted to stations; indemnity needs an admitted insurer or MGA; (U) escrow needs the right money-transmitter or escrow-agent status.

**First 90 days.** Sign 2 Part 145 stations as graders; get a term sheet from an aviation insurer for a pilot indemnity; grade 500 lots from 10 sellers; present loss and rejection data to a lessor and a lender; talk to the coalition.

## 5. Kit-First (dilemma: shops are paid for stand time, not turnaround)

**One-liner.** Predict a shop visit's missing parts 60-120 days before induction, assemble a documented kit, and induct the engine only when the kit is complete.

**Earned secret.** (A) Touch time on a CFM56 overhaul is 90-120 days (light workscope ~45; V2500 60-70; GTF 260-365 per Acumen's indicative table) against real turnaround of 180-240 days, so 60-120 days are an engine waiting in a stand for parts. A planner knows by day 5 of teardown which 30-40 line items gate the visit (HPT blades, vanes, shrouds, LLP stubs), and (U) most can be forecast months earlier from borescope and flight data. Shops induct on slot date, not kit readiness, because empty stands cost them and angry customers call.

**Structural conflict.** Shops are measured on stand utilization and paid on time-and-material, so they cannot gate induction or reveal the findings forecast that would reduce their labor revenue. Parts principals sell what they hold. Lessors pay maintenance reserves but cannot direct workscope.

**Customer / payer / signer.** Lessors (reserve claims, idle days) and airlines (cover-engine cost) pay for guaranteed turnaround; independent shops buy kits. Signer: Director of Engine Asset Management (lessor/airline) or VP Materials (shop).

**Product.** A bill-of-materials forecast per engine, then a sourced kit (USM, PMA, repaired, new) delivered as customer-furnished material, with a contractual gate: the shop inducts only kit-complete and the customer gets a turnaround guarantee backed by a cover-engine option.

**Why now.** Parts shortage is the primary cause of slow turnaround (Bain); OEM parts demand exceeds supply by 10-20%; USM is up to 30% of shop parts demand; JetCare claims 9-12 month OEM lead times (company claim); retirement-fed USM supply is the only elastic source.

**Wedge.** CFM56-7B kits for lessor-owned engines going to independent shops; first 10 customers: three lessors and their two most-used shops.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** This is a principal materials business. (A) 300 visits a year x $2.5M average kit = $750M of revenue at a 12-15% gross margin = $90-112M of gross profit. Revenue crosses $100M at ~40 kitted visits, but the real test is $100M of gross profit, which needs ~300 visits, 6% of narrowbody visits, and ~$120-150M of inventory capital (A). If it is run as a fee-only forecast layer, revenue tops out near ~$20M, so the margin must come from materials.

**Who does it today.** AAR, AerSale, FTAI Aviation, HEICO/Wencor, StandardAero (own materials), JetCare, Stratton Aviation, AerFin, Aeras Aviation; (U) CFM/GE customer material programs.

**Why they lose.** Each sells its own stock and is rewarded for selling it, none forecasts the customer's complete bill of materials across all channels, and shops cannot adopt induction gating without losing utilization. The forecast needs teardown-findings data no single principal shares.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Process power plus a data-driven cornered resource (the findings dataset), with counter-positioning against shops' utilization metrics. Weak unless the dataset becomes proprietary.

**Legal premise.** Customer-furnished material is a normal shop arrangement (U: check contracts), traceability of every USM item, and shops must accept third-party kits.

**First 90 days.** Collect findings reports for 100 past CFM56-7B visits from 2 lessors; build the forecast; back-test against actual bills of materials; sell one pilot kit with a turnaround guarantee.

## 6. Shop-Floor Ledger (dilemma: legacy MRO ERPs cannot capture the work as it happens)

**One-liner.** Capture shop-floor work (voice, photos, tool data) as it happens, draft the paperwork and produce a tamper-evident evidence package that lessors can rely on.

**Earned secret.** (U) A large share of a shop's labor and nearly all of its quality manager's time goes to reconciling the traveler, the manual revision and the release tag after the work is done. The ERP stores what was typed, not what was done; that is why records fraud is possible and why lessors re-audit shops. The people who sign are not the people who type.

**Structural conflict.** MRO ERPs (built around planners at terminals and licensed per planner seat) do not capture floor-level evidence; shops billing time-and-material gain nothing from tools that cut hours unless a customer demands it; the ERP vendors earn on seats they do not want to shrink.

**Customer / payer / signer.** The shop pays (VP Operations / VP Quality), but the pull is lessors and insurers who will accept the evidence package for reserve claims and return-to-service. Priced per technician per year.

**Product.** Hangar-floor capture tied to the work package; draft task-card entries, findings reports and release documentation for human certifier review; tamper-evident logs; the dataset it produces feeds Kit-First and Graded Parts.

**Why now.** Oliver Wyman: 58% of surveyed MRO firms are still "experimental" on AI and data quality is the main barrier to scaling, so capturing data at the source is the unlock; Aer Lingus signed AISmartPlan for planning; lessors are tightening records scrutiny as engines are scarce (Acumen).

**Wedge.** Engine and component shops that serve lessors, starting with the findings report and release package for one engine type; first 10 customers: five independent CFM56 shops and five component repair stations that handle high volumes of lessor work.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** (A) ~800k technicians worldwide x $1,800 a year = 55,000 seats for $100M, ~7% of technicians. Value check (A): documentation is ~10% of time, a technician costs $75-90k loaded, so the saving is $7-9k a year against a $1.8k price.

**Who does it today.** EmpowerMX (EMX Vision), Amygda, Soji AI, SAMIS AI, AISmartPlan, flydocs (Lufthansa Technik; records for lease returns), Bluetail (business aviation records), the ERP vendors (Trax, Ramco, AMOS and others, U).

**Why they lose.** They sell copilots or planning to planners; none sells an evidence package that a lessor accepts; ERPs cannot restructure around floor capture without cannibalizing planner seats.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Switching costs (embedded in work packages and quality manuals) and a cornered data asset; weak against a bundle by ERP vendors.

**Legal premise.** Maintenance records need a human certifier's signature; AI drafts are not releases; (U) FAA AC 120-78A governs electronic signatures and records and EASA Part-145 record-keeping rules apply; liability and data-ownership clauses need to be settled.

**First 90 days.** Shadow two shops for a week and time the paperwork; build capture for one workflow (borescope findings to findings report); get a lessor to agree to accept the output for one reserve claim.

## 7. Engine Lifeline (shock: spare-engine scarcity plus new institutional capital)

**One-liner.** A neutral pool manager that lets institutional engine owners and idle airline spares serve 60-180 day cover needs under one standardized swap protocol.

**Earned secret.** (U) Lessors insist on ~3-year terms because each swap costs $150-300k of records, installation and logistics work and carries return-condition risk, and airlines often keep an engine long after the shop returns theirs, paying a second cost clock. Standardizing the swap (borescope baseline, records pack, quick-engine-change kits, insurance) makes 90-day cover profitable at higher day-rates and raises utilization above the 84% Willis reports. (U) Airline-owned spare engines sit idle ~25% of the time.

**Customer / payer / signer.** Airlines with an engine in the shop pay the rent (IBA: $6,500+ a day for newer engines); institutional owners (Bridgepoint/AIP/GFH, Sanad, credit funds) pay the manager. Signer: VP Technical Operations (airline); Chief Investment Officer or asset manager (owner).

**Product.** Aggregate 150-450 engines from several owners and airline spares into one pool with swap-ready records, standard rate cards, short-notice logistics and remarketing; the manager earns a share of rent plus a swap fee.

**Why now.** Engine availability is the main constraint on keeping aircraft flying (Willis); airlines are leasing rather than overhauling $10M+ engines to preserve cash; shop visits run 180-300 days; $1B institutional platforms are forming (Oct 2026) and need an operator; AIP/Bridgepoint are buying LEAP-1B spares for 2027-29 delivery.

**Wedge.** CFM56-7B and -5B first (utilization high, lease rates elevated), then LEAP-1A/1B; first 10 customers: four mid-tier airlines with engines in shop and three owners with idle engines.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** (A) Pool of 450 engines: 270 next-gen at ~$12M each (~$3.2B) earning $6,500 a day at 85% utilization = $2.0M a year each ($545M), plus 180 legacy at ~$4M (~$0.7B) earning $45k a month = $0.46M each ($83M); total rent ~$630M. Manager fee 12% of rent = $75M plus ~1,170 swaps a year at $40k = $47M, so ~$120M. Needs ~$4B of engines under management, about four platforms the size of Bridgepoint's $1B target. Heroic but not a stretch if institutional appetite persists.

**Who does it today.** Willis Lease (leases, utilization 84%, Q1 2026 lease rent $77M), AIP Capital (manages the Bridgepoint/GFH platform), Sanad (asset management), FTAI Aviation; (U) AerCap/Engine Lease Finance, AerSale, GA Telesis and OEM rental pools.

**Why they lose.** Principal lessors want long terms to protect yield and avoid swap cost; none aggregates other owners' engines or airlines' idle spares; institutional owners have no operations team and would rather outsource.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Network economies (more engines, more coverage, more airlines), process power (the swap protocol), scale economies in logistics.

**Legal premise.** (U) Cape Town Convention engine registrations and title; effect of installing third-party engines on OEM warranties and LTSA coverage; insurance; customs and VAT on temporary imports; returns to owners must meet lease conditions.

**First 90 days.** Pick CFM56-7B; price and test a standard swap kit; sign letters with 2 owners and 3 airlines; run one live swap and measure cost.

## 8. Certifier Capacity (shock: labor and the scarce signature)

**One-liner.** A roster of pre-vetted certifying staff with portable company authorizations, sold to hangars and line stations short of signatures.

**Earned secret.** (A) The binding constraint in a hangar is not hands, it is certifying staff: every task card needs an authorized signature, so throughput is bounded by certifiers per shift. Onboarding a contractor takes days to weeks of authorization paperwork, so rosters sit idle. A portable competency record accepted by 100 host shops removes that delay. This is travel nursing for aviation.

**Customer / payer / signer.** MROs, airline hangars and line stations. Budget lines: contract labor and recruiting. Signer: VP Operations or Director of Maintenance. The line is large (US shortfall 17,800 certified mechanics in 2025, rising to 22,000 by 2027).

**Product.** Recruit and retain certifiers with retention bonds, pre-clear them against each host's quality manual, and maintain a portable record of training, experience and authorization history; add a training pipeline funded by employers (SkyWest, Bombardier and Epic Flight Academy already run employer-sponsored programs).

**Why now.** Labor rates are up 5.5-6.0% a year; two-thirds of surveyed firms find hiring technicians hard and over half expect attrition to worsen; engine labor rates are rising 6.4% (North America) to 8.3% (rest of world); Boeing sees 710,000 technicians needed through 2044.

**Wedge.** US A&P with inspection authorization for engine and component shops on contract; first 10 customers: five independent engine shops and five regional hangars.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** Gross profit, not revenue. (A) Bill ~$65 an hour vs pay ~$43 (Mojave posting, S) = $22 an hour x 1,900 hours = $42k per FTE, so $100M needs ~2,400 FTE and ~$300M of billings. That is ~11% of the US shortfall, or ~0.3% of the workforce (A, ~800k worldwide).

**Who does it today.** Specialist aviation staffers (for example Total Aviation Staffing, S posting); (U) generalist staffers such as Aerotek and Kelly; employer pathways (SkyWest AMT Pathway, Bombardier apprenticeship with WSU Tech, Epic Flight Academy's $27,000 scholarship program); Part 147 schools.

**Why they lose.** Staffers sell bodies, not authorization-ready certifiers; employer pipelines serve only their own demand; no one has built a portable record that hosts accept.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Weak: a cornered resource in a vetted roster and switching costs from embedded authorizations; scale economies in recruiting. This is the least defensible idea and I rate it accordingly.

**Legal premise.** (U) An A&P does not by itself authorize release to service in a Part 145 station; the station's quality manual must authorize each individual; FAA-EASA bilateral arrangements recognize organizations, not individual licenses; W-2 versus 1099 classification and visa limits.

**First 90 days.** Interview 15 hangar managers on onboarding time; sign 3 hosts for 20 pre-vetted certifiers; measure days saved against a typical contract hire.

## Ideas I considered and killed

- **Merchant open-access test cells.** ITP Aero's >EUR5M upgrade supports 225 tests a year and Safran's new Queretaro cell targets 350 by 2030. Five cells is ~1,500 tests, about a quarter of narrowbody visits, and a test is a slice of a visit price, so revenue caps near $50-75M (A). Dead.
- **Engine shop-visit invoice audit / third-party inspection.** AJW and Acumen already sell it; ~4,700 visits x $20-100k tops out under $100M at a heroic share.
- **Lease-transition general contractor / records factory.** $1-2M per narrowbody return (IBA and Sofema 2019, mba) but ~1,500 returns a year (A) and a ~10% net fee needs more than half the market; flydocs (Lufthansa Technik) owns records.
- **Maintenance-reserve cash release.** Letters of credit already substitute for cash for strong credits; weak credits cannot buy surety.
- **GTF compensation receivables.** Compensation is moderating (Leeham, Jan 2026) and negotiated privately; one-off.
- **Aircraft-parts tariff compliance.** The Section 232 proclamation imposed no tariffs; customs software is crowded.
- **Jet engine cores to data-center power.** FTAI Power already vertically integrates it; the value sits in power, outside this pool. It does lift engine residual values, which helps ideas 5 and 7.
- **Generic AI maintenance copilot / parts search.** Cliche list and crowded (SkySelect, EmpowerMX, Amygda, Soji AI, SAMIS AI).
- **Component pool for mid-tier narrowbodies.** Lufthansa Technik's pool, Spairliners, AJW and Turkish Technic already run pools, and $100M of gross profit needs ~800 aircraft (~10% of the 737NG/A320ceo fleet) (A).

## Honest overall read

- The pool is mostly pass-through to OEMs and a few capital-rich independents (HEICO, FTAI, StandardAero, Sanad), so per-event service fees cannot reach $100M. Every surviving idea therefore needs a heroic 8-25% of a defined mid-tier segment, or a principal materials or capital layer.
- Best two: the Repair Library (a real cornered resource that scales through cells and royalties, but uncertain sizing) and the Fleet Buying Group (volume-linked revenue with no inventory, but a long build and a share near 12-25% of the mid-tier).
- Slot Book is the sharpest answer to the shock but depends on shops accepting take-or-pay from a new intermediary; Engine Lifeline depends on institutional appetite for engines continuing.
- Weakest: Certifier Capacity and Shop-Floor Ledger (both defensible only with a data or roster edge that is not yet shown).
