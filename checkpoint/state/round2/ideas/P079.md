# P079: US OEM and prime purchases of metal castings (defense-critical), 8 ideas

Pool: about $53.7B of 2025 US metalcasting revenue (AFS), +8.0% projected for 2026. Written mid-October 2026.

## What the searches changed (read first)

These facts were found in this run and shaped every idea below. [primary] means a primary document, [secondary] means press or trade coverage, [mine] means my own arithmetic or assumption.

- **GE Aerospace agreed on 2026-09-08 to buy Consolidated Precision Products (CPP) for $11.75B.** CPP has about $2.0B of expected 2027 revenue, 6,600 employees and more than 20 facilities (roughly $100M of revenue per facility [mine]). The price is about 26x 2027 EBITDA before synergies and about 18x after. GE says the casting shortage is "much more a supply-side challenge" than demand. GE will keep CPP's other customers. Howmet fell about 10% that day. [secondary: Leeham, Aviation Week summaries, Sentisense]
- **Vertical integration is spreading.** SpaceX is building a gas-turbine blade foundry in Bastrop, TX (permits pending). Hadrian is hiring casting and forging engineers (May 2026). Anduril is hiring casting supplier-quality roles. RTX says Fabri (investment casting, $13.5M raised) plans a new foundry every year. Doncasters raised $919M in a June 2026 IPO. [secondary]
- **Gas-turbine casting is a hard bottleneck.** GE Vernova backlog was reported at 116 GW in Q2 2026. Wood Mackenzie puts heavy-duty turbine capacity at 60-70 GW a year against roughly 110 GW of orders. TechCrunch reports four companies cast blades and vanes at industrial scale, all at capacity. [secondary]
- **DoW Manufacturing Technology Strategic Plan (2026-09-30):** castings and forgings are "single points of failure". It names a "financing impasse" (investors wait for customer commitments, customers wait for proven product) and qualification delays caused by poor understanding of process variables. Fixes it proposes: real-time process monitoring, advanced inspection, simulation, targeted testing. No new dollars. [secondary: defence-industry.eu; I did not open the plan]
- **DLA (NDIA 2024 slides):** castings and forgings are 3% of NSNs but 20% of the 100 oldest backorders. DLA already runs free tools: DECAF, ICON (cast tooling database and supplier database), MetaL FACT, AFCAT. [primary]
- **Navy submarine industrial base:** $2.3B spent FY18-FY23, $6.3B expected in total, supplier base down from about 17,000 to 5,000, supplier-provided material expected to grow over 200%. BlueForge Alliance holds a sole-source contract of up to $980.7M, and a Navy official cited $605M of supplier-development money in the FY24 supplemental. [secondary]
- **Section 232 was restructured on 2026-04-02 (effective 2026-04-06).** Duties now apply to full customs value. Tiers are 50%, 25% and 15%, but 10% applies to derivatives made entirely from steel melted and poured in the US. Importers need enhanced melt-and-pour documentation. Sources disagree on "entirely" (EY) versus at least 95% (Perkins Coie). The reduced Annex III framework expires 2027-12-31. The Feb 2025 derivative list covers about $150B of 2024 imports (CRS). [secondary]
- **CMMC Phase 2 (the November 10, 2026 deadline) was suspended on 2026-07-13.** I did not build anything on it. NIST 800-171 obligations remain. [secondary]
- **Pool structure:** about 1,956 metalcasting facilities (AFS, 2018), 75-80% with under 100 employees, about 184,100 employees. Cleaning and finishing are 20-35% of the manufacturing cost of an average casting (Foundry Magazine). Foundries lose at least 5% of revenue to scrap and rework (Swansea). One investment-casting plant reports 99.9% of returns are defects found in machining. [secondary]
- **Closures are continuing:** Dalton (First Brands bankruptcy), Neenah Lincoln (imports), Smith Foundry (EPA settlement), Friends Foundry (VIBCO's casting partner). Lacy Foundries sold its customer book after closing its casting plants (Feb 2025). [secondary]

Dead end I checked and did not use: the CMMC Phase 2 deadline (suspended). Not yet found: any NAVSEA acceptance of CT for casting acceptance (so CT is a screening gate in these ideas, not an acceptance method).

## INSIDER 1: Tooling Bank

**One-liner:** Be the Iron Mountain for casting patterns. Take custody of OEM-owned tooling before its foundry closes, scan it into a validated digital pattern, and become contractor of record when the part is re-sourced or printed.

- **Persona:** pattern-shop manager or foundry owner who later bought castings for an OEM (the Casting Source columnist Charbauski is the real-world version).
- **Customer:** mid-size and large OEMs buying custom castings (valves, pumps, ag, rail, mining, power), plus Navy/DLA programs with orphaned cast parts.
- **Payer and signer:** OEM VP or Director of Strategic Sourcing or Aftermarket Parts. Budget: supplier-risk, tooling management, aftermarket spares. Pays an annual custody fee, a one-time scan fee, and a casting margin when we route reorders.
- **Problem:** OEMs own the patterns and core boxes, but the tooling sits in a foundry's rafters. Foundries often do not volunteer where it is stored. Proof of ownership decides whether you get it back when a bank takes the building. Each lost pattern costs a new pattern, a requalification and months (BCG: 6-18+ months to cast or forge a part).
- **Solution:** (1) closure-triggered rescue: find foundries 3-9 months from closing (WARN notices, air-permit surrenders, trade press) and call their customers; (2) custody: retrieve, inventory, tag, store, document chain of ownership; (3) digitize: 3D scan plus CAD reconstruction plus gating record, validated by a sample casting; (4) route: when a pattern is lost or a foundry is slow, print the sand mold and run first article at a partner foundry; (5) registry: a live map of every tool, owner, location and condition.
- **Why now:** closures; the DoW plan names qualification delays; DLA funded a printed-sand pilot for obsolete critical castings ($15M potential, option exercised May 2025); Humtown runs a sand-printing fleet in Ohio.
- **Wedge and first 10:** "Closure Response Kit" per tool (about $2.5k scan plus $250 a year custody), sold to OEMs that buy from a foundry that just filed a WARN. First 10: mid-size valve, pump, ag and rail OEMs that are customers of the next 5 closing foundries. DLA's MetaL FACT team is a referral channel.
- **Path to $100M (honest):** I could not find a count of OEM-owned patterns, so this rests on my assumptions. AFS counts about 1,956 facilities. Assume about 2,000 OEM-owned pattern or core-box sets per foundry on average, so about 3.9M sets. At maturity: custody and registry $250 a set a year on 300k sets (about 8% of all sets) is $75M. Digitizing 12k sets a year at $2.5k is $30M. Routing 4k re-sourced sets a year at $25k of annual casting spend each is $100M of castings (about $15M margin if booked net). Total about $120M if the casting resale is booked as principal, about $50M if net. Needs about 8% of US OEM-owned tooling. Verify the pattern count first.
- **Who does it today:** DLA DECAF and ICON (free, only DLA-managed NSNs, no custody, no casting). MetaL FACT and AFCAT (free). OEM spreadsheets and generic ERP tooling modules. Casting Source advice. Humtown (sand printing) and ExOne/Desktop Metal through the CTC/DLA pilot. Xometry and Fictiv run their own networks. I found no startup doing custody plus twin plus routing.
- **Why they lose:** DLA covers DLA-managed NSNs only and neither holds tooling nor casts. Foundries have no reason to help customers leave. Print bureaus sell molds, not custody and qualification. OEM systems track assets they hold, not tooling at suppliers.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** cornered resource (custody, ownership proof, validated twin) and switching costs (a changed vault means revalidation).
- **Earned secret:** The pattern, not the foundry, is the real lock-in, and the OEM usually does not know where it is. Rescue has to happen months before the doors close, because once a lender or auctioneer controls the building, ownership paperwork decides who gets the patterns.
- **Legal premise to check:** several states give a possessory lien on customer-owned tooling (Illinois Tool and Die Lien Act covers patterns, subordinate to perfected UCC Article 9 interests; Michigan MCL 445.618 covers molds; Arizona fabricator lien). OEMs should file UCC financing statements and mark tooling. Scans of defense parts are ITAR/CUI, so the vault must be compliant.
- **First 90 days:** weeks 1-3, list every foundry closing in the next 12 months and interview 10 owners. Weeks 3-8, offer 3 OEMs a free audit of tooling at their top 5 foundries. Weeks 8-12, take custody of the first 200 tools, scan 30, print one mold and pour a sample. Kill test: if fewer than 3 of 10 OEMs will pay $250 per tool per year or accept routing, shrink or drop.
- **Outside-box score:** 7/10.

## INSIDER 2: Second-Source Factory

**One-liner:** A fixed-price factory that turns a sole-sourced or backordered casting into two prime-approved sources, where the approval package and process data are the product.

- **Persona:** prime supplier-quality engineer plus NADCAP auditor.
- **Customer:** aerospace, defense, shipbuilding and energy primes and tier-1s with sole-sourced or long-backordered cast parts; Navy sub-base suppliers; DLA.
- **Payer and signer:** VP Supply Chain or Director of Supplier Development at the prime; or the program supplier-readiness budget (BlueForge has $605M of supplier-development money cited for FY24). Priced per approved part number.
- **Problem:** castings and forgings are 3% of DLA NSNs and 20% of its 100 oldest backorders. Lead times are 6-18+ months (BCG). OEMs cannot see below tier-1. Second-sourcing belongs to nobody: SQEs are overloaded, every prime has its own package, and a small foundry cannot write one. The DoW plan blames qualification delays and poor understanding of process variables.
- **Solution:** a qualification line. Ingest drawing and spec; pick two candidate foundries from a capability graph; simulate gating and solidification; make tooling (printed molds where possible); pour a sample lot; CT or X-ray; run AS9102 first article and the prime-specific forms; shepherd through the prime's SQE and DCMA/NAVSEA gate; hand over a source with a process-fingerprint record. Keep it alive with annual change control (subscription). The registry of approved positions by part number becomes the format primes ask for.
- **Why now:** DoW plan; GE-CPP shows engine makers scrambling for capacity; the Navy sub base needs over 200% more supplier material; BlueForge funds supplier readiness; simulation, CT and sand printing cut cycle time.
- **Wedge and first 10:** Navy and DLA backordered or orphaned castings. First 10: tier-1 valve, pump and piping assemblers on Virginia and Columbia supply chains, plus referrals from DLA L&M.
- **Path to $100M (assumptions flagged):** no public benchmark for the cost of qualifying a second casting source. Assume $75k average per approved part number. Year 6: 800 approvals a year is $60M, plus a maintenance subscription of $10k a year on 4,000 live part numbers is $40M, so $100M. Needs about 150 buyer organizations with about 5-6 part numbers a year each outsourcing the work. The product assumption to test is that primes will outsource it.
- **Who does it today:** in-house supplier development and SQE teams (Anduril is hiring casting SQE and industrialization roles; Hadrian is hiring casting engineers); DLA MetaL FACT and AFCAT (free for DLA NSNs); American Metalcasting Consortium (DLA ManTech-funded projects); Cetim (France) qualification service; BCG-style advisors; low-cost-country sourcing agents; Xometry and Fictiv for commercial parts. BlueForge pays for supplier readiness.
- **Why they lose:** in-house teams cannot scale to the backlog; DLA and the consortium serve DLA NSNs and pilots, not prime production; consultants sell hours and carry no approval risk; marketplaces avoid flight-critical and SUBSAFE parts.
- **Moat:** process power (a repeatable qualification line), cornered resource (approved positions and data on which foundries pass first time), and a registry format that becomes standard.
- **Earned secret:** Second-sourcing a casting is rarely a metallurgy problem. It is a sequencing-and-paperwork problem that nobody owns: tooling, sample lot, CT, first article and prime forms sit in six different hands, and a 60-person foundry cannot write a prime's package.
- **First 90 days:** pick 10 part numbers with documented backorders (DLA DIBBS, Navy), approach the holders with a fixed fee, 50% payable on approval. Run two end to end at a partner foundry. Build the form library for 3 primes. Kill test: if no prime accepts a third-party package at the same gate, it collapses into consulting and is dead.
- **Outside-box score:** 5/10.

## PATTERN 1: Tier-Three Credit

**One-liner:** Multi-tier supply chain finance for defense and aerospace foundries and machine shops, repaid from the prime's payment so the borrower's weak credit matters little.

- **Pattern transferred:** "control the repayment channel" (payroll deduction or wallet sweep) plus "government exits as lender, private capital fills the creditworthy slice".
- **Customer:** foundries, forges and machine shops that are tier-2 or tier-3 on prime programs. The sponsor is a prime or tier-1 that does not want capacity to fail.
- **Payer and signer:** the borrower (foundry owner or CFO) pays about 10-14% all-in. The prime's VP Supply Chain signs a payment-direction program that costs them nothing and supports their resilience goals.
- **Problem:** the DoW plan describes a "financing impasse". J.P. Morgan and others say access to finance deteriorates sharply below tier-1. OSC loans run $10M-$150M and fund plant and equipment, not working capital. A $2M order can need $1.2M of metal, pattern repair and payroll up front and pay 90 days after the machine shop approves.
- **Solution:** the prime or tier-1 signs a payment direction for named POs. The platform advances up to 60-70% of PO value at acceptance (Twinco does this for retailers), then lends against WIP and finished inventory. It collects from the prime into a controlled account, takes a security interest in receivables and, where the OEM agrees, in OEM-owned tooling. DPAS rating and prime acceptance history are underwriting signals.
- **Why now:** DoW plan; Navy sub-base build; the FY27 request seeks about $20.2B for the Defense Strategic Capital Credit Program, but it lends at $10M+ for capex; banks are publishing defense working-capital playbooks; castings backlog.
- **Wedge and first 10:** 10 foundries on one prime's Navy program, a $10-20M warehouse line from a credit fund, one prime signing the program.
- **Path to $100M:** at about 12% all-in yield, $100M of revenue needs about $830M average outstanding, or about 280 borrowers at $3M. Assume working capital is 15-20% of casting revenue (my assumption), so $8-11B is tied up in US castings. $830M is 8-10% of that. Net revenue after about 6-7% cost of funds is roughly half, so the revenue test passes and the profit test needs about twice the book. Needs a $1B+ debt facility.
- **Who does it today:** prime-anchored supply chain finance (Taulia, C2FO, PrimeRevenue, from memory; tier-1 invoices); J.P. Morgan and Citi defense inventory and receivables finance; PO-finance shops (King Trade Capital, Wells Fargo PO Finance Group, SouthStar, $150k-$5M deals); Twinco (retail); OSC; SBA; ABL lenders. I found no defense-casting specialist.
- **Why they lose:** anchor programs fund only invoices the anchor approves; PO lenders price the borrower, not the prime; OSC funds capex; nobody lends against customer-owned tooling plus rated orders.
- **Moat:** counter-positioning against banks, scale economies in funding (more primes signed means lower losses and cheaper capital), switching costs once embedded in a prime's AP.
- **Earned secret:** Foundries rarely die from lack of orders. They die in the gap between buying metal and payroll for a $2M order and getting paid 90 days after the machine shop approves. Bankers underwrite the foundry; the right underwriting is the prime's payment and the part's quality acceptance history.
- **Legal premise to check:** UCC Article 9 generally overrides anti-assignment clauses for accounts (from memory), but each prime's PO terms and any CUI constraints need counsel. Lender licensing varies by state.
- **First 90 days:** find one prime or tier-1 sponsor (Navy valve or pump OEM) willing to sign payment direction for 10 named suppliers. Line up a $10M credit-fund facility. Fund the first 3 POs. Kill test: if no prime will sign payment direction, it is just PO finance with a worse price.
- **Outside-box score:** 5/10.

## PATTERN 2: Foundry BOT (build, staff, operate, transfer)

**One-liner:** Fluor plus an operating team for foundries. Design, power, permit, commission and run a plant for 3 years, then hand it over, so buyers with money and no casting people get capacity in about 24 months.

- **Patterns transferred:** "capex-supercycle pass-through to scarce physical trades" and "selling scarce credentialed expertise to a new concentrated buyer class".
- **Customer:** neo-primes and OEMs deciding to build casting capacity: SpaceX (Bastrop), Hadrian, Anduril, GE after CPP, Safran (Rennes), Navy sub-base suppliers.
- **Payer and signer:** COO, VP Manufacturing or Chief Supply Chain Officer. Capex for design and construction, opex for an operating fee.
- **Problem:** BCG says rebuilding experienced engineering expertise takes 5-10 years and training new production staff 3-6 months. New US foundries cost $100M+. Power and permits, not construction, set the schedule: A.Y. McDonald's nine-figure Wisconsin foundry waited on a 10-mile transmission line; SpaceX's Bastrop permits are pending. Equipment (induction furnaces, sand systems, heat treat, NDT) comes from separate vendors.
- **Solution:** standard modular foundry designs by process (steel sand, investment, aluminum). Site selection starts from power and air-permit availability, favoring shuttered foundry sites. Reserve long-lead equipment slots. A commissioning bench of retired and mid-career foundry GMs, metallurgists, melt supervisors and NDT leads. A 36-month operating contract with a yield incentive, then transfer, with an option to co-own.
- **Why now:** Pratt Asheville ($650M by 2027), Howmet Wichita Falls ($70M), Doncasters Bochum ($52M), Safran Rennes, Ford Meter Box (290,000 sq ft), SpaceX Bastrop (336 ha), Fabri's one-a-year plan. GE-CPP shows buyers will pay for capacity.
- **Wedge and first 10:** a fixed-fee 12-week "foundry readiness" study (power, air-permit path, equipment slots, staffing plan) offered to 10 neo-primes and OEMs, converting to design-build.
- **Path to $100M:** assume 25 US foundry projects over 5 years averaging $120M, so $3B of capex (my estimate; named projects above exceed $1B). Gross design-build revenue of $100M a year is about 17% share. Add an operating fee of 4% of plant revenue on 5 plants of $100M, so $20M. Net fees are only about 10-25% of that gross, so this is a services wedge. The scaling assets are standard designs, reserved equipment slots, a permitted-site bank and co-ownership stakes.
- **Who does it today:** equipment OEMs (Inductotherm, ABP, ALD, Disa, Sinto, Kuttner, from memory) sell machines and sometimes lines; general contractors; EPC majors (Fluor, Jacobs) that do not operate foundries; in-house teams at Howmet, CPP and Pratt; Fabri builds its own; BCG advises only.
- **Why they lose:** equipment vendors carry no schedule or yield risk and do not staff; EPCs do not know melting; in-house teams compete for the same scarce experts.
- **Moat:** cornered resource (the operator bench, equipment slots, permitted sites) and process power (standard designs).
- **Earned secret:** A foundry's schedule is set by the utility and the air permit. Its success depends on about 20 people (melt supervisor, metallurgist, core-room lead, heat-treat lead, NDT Level III) who each took 15 years to make. Buyers think they are buying equipment; they are buying those people.
- **First 90 days:** sign 3 readiness studies. Recruit a 10-person advisory bench of retired foundry GMs. Map 20 shuttered foundry sites with live air permits and grid service. Kill test: if every neo-prime hires its own team, drop the services and keep only the permitted-site bank.
- **Outside-box score:** 6/10.

## DILEMMA 1: The Non-GE Foundry

**One-liner:** Customer-funded independent castings capacity for everyone who just lost a neutral supplier, financed by 10-year take-or-pay contracts.

- **Customer:** non-captive buyers of airfoils and structural castings: industrial gas-turbine makers other than GE, engine and airframe makers without in-house foundries, Navy primes. I did not verify which companies buy from CPP.
- **Payer and signer:** Chief Procurement Officer or VP Supply Chain signs a 10-year take-or-pay (BCG recommends 10+ years against today's 5-7); CFO signs prepayments.
- **Problem:** GE is buying CPP (about $2.0B revenue, about 26x EBITDA). Howmet fell about 10% on in-sourcing fears. SpaceX is building its own. Four casters at industrial scale are reported at capacity. GE Vernova is taking slot reservations for 2031. A Vertical Research analyst said it remains to be seen whether non-GE CPP customers are affected. Non-captive buyers are price-takers in a queue.
- **Solution:** build or buy-and-expand an independent caster at about $100M of revenue per plant. Start with one hard niche where non-captive buyers share a design (for example one frame size of turbine vanes). Fund it with a buyer consortium's take-or-pay and prepayments plus project debt (OSC capex loans of $10-150M, infrastructure credit). Offer open-book cost-plus pricing and a no-captive policy (no engine business). Run in-plant CT and a process-data record per casting.
- **Why now:** GE-CPP (2026-09-08), SpaceX Bastrop, the DoW plan, the Doncasters IPO, the OSC FY27 request.
- **Wedge and first 10:** one product family, one modest first plant, or a distressed qualified foundry as the nucleus. First 10: signed capacity LOIs from non-captive buyers.
- **Path to $100M:** CPP's roughly $2.0B over more than 20 facilities is about $100M per facility, so $100M equals one CPP-average plant. At CPP's price-to-revenue of about 5.9x ($11.75B over $2.0B), a $100M plant is worth about $590M if the multiple holds. Capital need is my estimate of $150-300M per plant (a new US foundry is reported at over $100M; Pratt's integrated Asheville is $650M). The pool share is tiny. The constraints are capital and a 3-5 year qualification path.
- **Who does it today:** Howmet; CPP (becoming GE-owned); Doncasters (public since June 2026); Chromalloy; ATI; PCC Airfoils; RTX/Pratt (captive); Safran (captive, Rennes); SpaceX (captive); Fabri (small investment casting, shipping to Lockheed and RTX); Hadrian (in-house plans). Pursuit Aerospace bought Creasey Castings on 2026-08-11 (roll-up).
- **Why they lose (the dilemma):** every incumbent allocates scarce capacity to its highest-priority long-term agreements and cannot give rivals dedicated, open-book capacity without destroying its pricing power. CPP's new owner competes with CPP's other customers. Captive builders serve only themselves.
- **Moat:** cornered resource (qualified capacity under long-term contract), switching costs, scale economies, and counter-positioning (neutrality as the feature).
- **Earned secret:** Castings capacity goes to whoever is strategically closest to the supplier's owner. When your supplier becomes a competitor's subsidiary, you do not need a cheaper source; you need a neutral one whose capacity is contractually yours. Hypothesis: buyers will prefund it because dedicated capacity is worth more than the price premium.
- **First 90 days:** talk to 15 procurement heads of non-captive buyers to test willingness to sign a 10-year take-or-pay. Recruit two ex-Howmet or ex-PCC plant leaders. Identify 3 distressed qualified foundries as acquisition nuclei. Kill test: if no buyer will prepay, the capital stack does not close and the idea is dead.
- **Outside-box score:** 6/10.

## DILEMMA 2: Supplier of record with a CT gate and a yield guarantee

**One-liner:** A prime-approved supplier-of-record that buys rough castings from a vetted network, CT-screens before machining, machines, and ships finished parts at a guaranteed-good-part price.

- **Customer:** OEMs and primes buying machined cast parts (valves, pumps, vehicles, rail, heavy equipment, Navy components) who manage 5-20 foundries and shops per part family.
- **Payer and signer:** Director or VP Supply Chain, direct materials budget. Pays a finished-part price at an assumed 5-12% premium over piece price for the guarantee.
- **Problem:** defects surface late. One investment-casting plant says 99.9% of returns are defects found in machining. Foundries lose at least 5% of revenue to scrap and rework. Primes cannot onboard and police hundreds of tiny suppliers. Shops charge for machining scrap, and disputes follow.
- **Solution:** one approved supplier for the finished part. Qualify and audit foundries once, not per customer. CT or X-ray before machining. Machine in network or in own cells. Ship with a data package. Price per good part. Use the process-to-defect-to-machining-outcome data to steer each part to the foundry and process with the best predicted yield. Grow by buying a closing foundry's customer book when its approvals can be moved (the Lacy pattern).
- **Why now:** Lumafield launched a large-format CT (Saturn, 2026-09-10); the DoW plan calls for advanced inspection and process monitoring; capacity is short; neo-primes show appetite for casting.
- **Wedge and first 10:** commercial-grade heavy-equipment, valve and pump castings where CT screening is contractual. First 10: mid-size OEMs with open scrap disputes, then Navy tier-1s. I did not rely on any CMMC deadline.
- **Path to $100M:** revenue is finished-part sales. 40 customers at $2.5M is $100M, only about 0.19% of the $53.7B pool. Target gross margin 18-25% after scrap risk. Demand proof: Xometry's marketplace did $205M in Q1 2026 (castings not broken out).
- **Who does it today:** Xometry and Fictiv (networks, no yield guarantee, strongest in prototypes and low volume); CASTFAST (Germany, 30 foundries); Sourcing Planet/SUpplyDU (Europe); AFS's free supplier finder; vertically integrated foundry-machining houses (from memory); contract machine shops; Hadrian (CNC, adding casting).
- **Why they lose (the dilemma):** marketplaces take no risk and avoid critical parts; machine shops are paid by the hour and cannot carry casting yield risk; foundries lack CT, machining and capital; primes' ERPs track price and delivery, not per-casting risk.
- **Moat:** counter-positioning against marketplaces (we carry the risk), a data asset (process, defect, machining outcome), scale economies in CT.
- **Earned secret:** Nobody owns the seam between the foundry and the machine shop, and that is where the money disappears: porosity is invisible until a tool breaks through it after hours of machining. Whoever puts the CT gate before the machine and owns both sides' cost wins the argument and the data.
- **First 90 days:** find 5 OEMs with documented scrap-dispute costs on machined castings. Rent CT time at a service bureau. Run 3 part families end to end with two foundries and a shop. Get product-liability and E&O quotes. Kill test: if gross margin after scrap is under 12% on the pilots, the guarantee is mispriced.
- **Outside-box score:** 6/10.

## SHOCK 1: US-Pour (the melt-and-pour ledger for the 10% rate)

**One-liner:** The compliance rail for the April 2026 Section 232 rule that taxes steel derivatives made entirely from US-melted-and-poured steel at 10% instead of 25-50%: heat-level attestations from foundries and mills, audit-ready for CBP.

- **Customer:** importers of record of cast and steel derivative goods (valves, fittings, pumps, components), foreign contract assemblers in Mexico, Canada and Korea that use US castings, and US foundries and mills selling "US-poured" content.
- **Payer and signer:** VP Trade Compliance or Chief Procurement Officer at the importer (budget: duty cost). Foundry sales VP for the sell-side credential. Price about 1% of entered value or per attestation.
- **Problem:** since 2026-04-06, Section 232 applies to full customs value, with tiers of 50%, 25% and 15% and 10% for articles made entirely from US-melted-and-poured steel. Mill certificates are PDFs with no standard layout, heat numbers get mistranscribed, the exemption is all-or-nothing per article (per a commercial source), and EY and Perkins Coie disagree on "entirely" versus at least 95%.
- **Solution:** a heat-lot ledger binding mill and foundry melt records (heat number, chemistry, date, facility) to downstream lots, parts and shipments. It issues entry-ready attestation packages (US-content claim code plus certificates for CBP's ACE document system), spot-audits suppliers, and insures the claim. The same ledger serves DFARS specialty-metals and Build America, Buy America iron-and-steel proofs.
- **Why now:** proclamation of 2026-04-02; melt-and-pour reporting since 2024-11-21; the FY2026 NDAA reportedly names castings and forgings as priority components.
- **Wedge and first 10:** US foundries and mills that ship castings or bar to Mexican, Canadian and Korean assemblers, and the importers that re-import the derivative. First 10: 5 foundries and 5 valve, pump or fittings importers.
- **Path to $100M:** the Feb 2025 derivative list covers about $150B of 2024 imports; core steel and aluminum imports were $25B and $18B in 2025 (CRS). At 1% of entered value, $100M needs $10B through the rail, about 6.7% of the $150B list. For an importer, 25% to 10% on $10B saves about $1.5B a year (the fee is about 7% of savings); at the 50% tier it saves $4B. This is heroic and rests on how much of the list can be made entirely from US-melted steel, which is unknown.
- **Who does it today:** customs brokers and trade-compliance software (Descartes, Thomson Reuters ONESOURCE, Flexport, Zonos, Avalara) handle classification and duty math; mill-certificate extraction and traceability vendors (Algoscale DocumentIQ, Oxmaint, Kissflow, Assent); importer spreadsheets; mills' own certificates. I found no heat-level, foundry-native attestation product tied to the 10% rate.
- **Why they lose:** broker software works at the HTS and invoice level and treats mill certificates as attachments; mills and foundries do not want liability for importers' claims; importers' ERPs do not hold heat numbers.
- **Moat:** network effects (more melters on the ledger means more eligible content), cornered resource (verified melt records and audit history), switching costs.
- **Earned secret:** For a foundry, "melted and poured" is an event it controls and records, and the heat number is the only thing that survives from furnace to ship. It is mistranscribed all the time. The ledger is a data-integrity problem the foundry is uniquely placed to solve.
- **Legal premise to check (the main risk):** the exact "entirely" test; CBP guidance (a commercial source cites CSMS 68253075; verify it); whether supplier attestation alone suffices; liability for false claims; no customs-broker activity without a license. This pattern can die from policy whiplash: the Annex III reduced framework expires 2027-12-31, litigation is pending, and rates have moved repeatedly.
- **First 90 days:** read the proclamation, annexes and CBP guidance with a customs attorney. Interview 10 importers and 5 foundries. Prototype a ledger on 3 foundry heat records, ending in an entry-ready package. Kill test: if CBP accepts a plain supplier attestation, or the 10% preference is dropped, pivot to audit insurance or drop.
- **Outside-box score:** 8/10.

## SHOCK 2: Cleaning Room as a Service

**One-liner:** Run the foundry's finishing department and bill per casting: robotic grinding, cut-off and weld-repair cells plus trained operators in the foundry's building.

- **Customer:** mid-size steel, iron and nonferrous foundries (100-500 employees) with high-mix, medium-to-large castings still ground by hand; later primes' captive foundries.
- **Payer and signer:** foundry owner, GM or VP Operations, from the cleaning-room labor and abrasives budget.
- **Problem:** cleaning and finishing are 20-35% of manufacturing cost of an average casting, and the cleaning room has the highest turnover (Swedish study). A defense foundry found its cleaning room was the bottleneck and could not hire operators. A Pentagon official is quoted saying 122,000 more cast-and-forge workers are needed by 2028 (date unclear). Robot vendors sell capex projects with per-part programming; Eagle Alloy found them aimed at high-volume work and built its own cell.
- **Solution:** an outsourced in-plant department. Scan-to-path uses the foundry's own gating and method file (cut-off points are known before the pour). Force-controlled grinding and cut-off cells, CT-guided weld repair later, operators on our payroll. Per-casting or per-pound pricing with a guaranteed ceiling below current cost. We own the robots and the recipe library.
- **Why now:** labor shock; DoW plan; vision-first grinding robots (Pivot Robotics, YC W24) show feasibility; Benton Foundry reports two operators replacing seven grinders (vendor-reported).
- **Wedge and first 10:** steel foundries with Navy or defense backlog whose cleaning room is the bottleneck; 3 pilot cells.
- **Path to $100M:** assume manufacturing cost is about 90% of $53.7B, and cleaning is the low-end 20%, so about $9.7B a year (my assumption). Addressable high-mix medium and large castings are about 30%, so about $2.9B. $100M is about 3.4% of the addressable fee pool, or about 1% of all cleaning spend. In cells, at about $250k a cell-year (replacing about 5 FTEs plus abrasives), that is about 400 cells.
- **Who does it today:** integrators and vendors (Weiler, Vulcan Engineering, Barinder, ABB, Fanuc); foundry self-builds (Eagle Alloy, Benton); Pivot Robotics (funding unclear); Path Robotics for welding (from memory); staffing agencies; offshoring. I found no per-casting managed-service player.
- **Why they lose:** integrators sell capex and leave; foundries lack robot programmers; staffing firms cannot fix turnover.
- **Moat:** process power (recipe library keyed to pattern and method), switching costs (in-plant), purchasing scale. This is the weakest moat of the eight.
- **Earned secret:** Cleaning-room robots failed because every cast shape varies, but the foundry already holds the answer: gate and riser positions are in the method file and solidification model, and the pattern is fixed. This is a data-integration problem more than a robotics problem.
- **Legal premise to check:** collective-bargaining subcontracting clauses (many foundries are unionized), plus dust, noise and workplace-safety liability.
- **First 90 days:** visit 10 foundries and record cleaning-room cost per casting. Sign 3 pilots on one casting family each. Build the scan-to-path loop from one method file to one finished casting. Kill test: if the cell cannot beat manual cost per casting by 25% on 3 families, drop.
- **Outside-box score:** 5/10.
