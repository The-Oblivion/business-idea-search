# P068: US building owners' spend on fire and life-safety inspection, testing, maintenance and monitoring (round 2 ideas)

Pool: IBISWorld puts US fire protection and security-alarm installation contractors at $22.1B (year uncertain) and fire protection consultants at $8.1B. No clean ITM-only number exists; the pool file assumes recurring ITM, monitoring and code-driven repair is $8-12B (assumption, not sourced). Generated 2026-10-14.

## What the search changed (read before the ideas)

Things I found that kill or reshape the obvious ideas:

- **The AHJ report-filing rail already exists and is PE-backed.** Brycer's The Compliance Engine says it is trusted by more than 1,420 jurisdictions; contractors pay per-report fees set by each fire department (Austin $30 per system type per building per period; Charleston $15, with a $15 late fee); owners can open free accounts in some cities. TA Associates invested; Brycer bought InspectionReportsOnline on Jan 8, 2026. "Build the filing network" is dead.
- **Inspection software is crowded and consolidating.** ServiceTrade (1,300+ fire and mechanical contractors per one source; $85M from JMI in Dec 2021), Ember (ex-Fire Inspect, $10M seed, Jan 2025), Inspect Point ($28M, Mainsail, 2022). Life Safety Inspection Vault was taken over by TEGRIS Fire Protection in July 2026 (per a PitchBook/VC-backed summary I could not open in full). NOFire AI raised a seed in May 2026. A generic "AI report writer for inspectors" is dead.
- **Roll-ups are well capitalized and the income stream is rich.** Pye-Barker closed 22 acquisitions in H1 2026 (sources say 41-57 a year in 2024-25) with ADIA and GIC as minority holders. Cintas Fire Protection grew 10.7% organically in Q4 FY26 at a 50.8% gross margin (49.3% in Q4 FY25). A seller-side broker quotes commercial fire monitoring books at 45-65x monthly RMR versus 28-40x for residential-type books. Do not plan against that capital by being another roll-up.
- **"A better POTS box" is dead.** Ooma AirDial (41 reseller partners, management calls it early days, and admits resellers' POTS cost can exceed what they bill), DataRemote POTS-in-a-box, POTSBOX, Telguard (Carrier), Napco StarLink, Resideo/ADI, Honeywell, plus integrators Everon (ADT Commercial), Johnson Controls, Siemens, Convergint. Ooma also runs POTSTracker, a free discontinuance-notice tracker used as lead generation.
- **OEM clouds already exist, one brand at a time.** Honeywell CLSS Gateway, JCI Connected Fire Panels (JCI models only), Siemens Cerberus gateway.
- **Co-op purchasing vehicles are already held by the majors.** JCI Fire (Sourcewell 121024-JHN to Feb 2029), APi (121024-API), Siemens (UConn approvals). A "Carahsoft for fire" wrapper is dead.
- **RMR lending exists.** Alarm Financial Services ($150k-$1.5M facilities, including to commercial fire companies), Rockland Trust security-alarm group ($2-25M revolvers), SAFE Security (buys contracts). So "financing RMR" is a tool, not an idea.
- **Responder-data and pre-plan products exist.** RapidSOS links fire-alarm data from 100,000+ Emergency24 monitored accounts to 911; Intterra sells pre-plans; Knox (14,000+ agencies) already runs KnoxConnect and electronic boxes. A "Knox for data" is dead.
- **Sizes I could not find:** the number of POTS-dependent life-safety endpoints, the fire-watch market, any ITM-only market, installed fire panels. Every path below flags its assumptions.

Ideas I killed for size or incumbents (so nobody re-derives them):

- False-alarm elimination: NFPA counts 2.1-2.9M false alarm responses a year (about a third are system malfunctions in 2010 data); local response costs are $94-$411 (Mesa, Oneonta). Even with owner fines and service calls the owner-borne pool looks like a few hundred million; $100M would be a third of it.
- NFPA 25 age waves: the first 50-year sprinkler head tests start in 2026 and fast-response heads need first tests at 20 years; lab fee is about $50 a head ($200 minimum), samples are 1-4% of heads. Real, growing, but I put the pool at $100-300M a year, so $100M is not credible for one firm.
- Sprinkler corrosion programs (nitrogen inerting at $0.20-0.40/sf versus $2-8/sf pipe replacement): all cost evidence comes from a nitrogen vendor.
- Fire-door inspection (enforcement is in healthcare), hospital life-safety mock surveys, BESS fire ITM, third-party plan review and acceptance witnessing (Virginia, Tennessee since Oct 2024; construction-phase only, state-by-state), remote-lock collateral (you cannot switch off a life-safety device), AFFF conversion (industrial and environmental, not owner ITM).

Sizing anchors used below (sourced unless marked assumption):

- FCC Voice Telephone Services report: 16M end-user switched access lines in Dec 2024, falling 17.8% a year (2021-24 compound rate). My arithmetic: about 10.8M at end-2026 and about 6M at end-2029 if the pace holds.
- Assumption: 10-20% of remaining business copper lines serve life-safety or alarm endpoints (fire panel, elevator, area-of-refuge, burglar, door entry), so 1-2M endpoints and 0.6-1.2M sites in 2026. No source found.
- POTS economics (all vendor claims): Honeywell dealer flyer: $150-300 a month for two monitored POTS lines versus about $12 a month for cellular; AFA: $60-100 a month saved per building; Ooma: $80+ per copper business line is typical; a school dealer reported about $1,800 a month saved by cancelling 8 lines.
- Carrier clock: FCC order FCC 26-19 adopted 2026-03-26 (31-day auto-grant, blanket grandfathering); notice periods cut from 180 to 90 days in March 2025 (per Ooma/Frost). AT&T approved for its first ~500 wire centers and more than 30% of its copper footprint this year, exit of most copper by end-2029 outside California (DataRemote bulletin Aug 17, 2026; vendor). Lumen: no new POTS orders since May 1, 2025 (14 states). Verizon plans 5M+ lines to fiber by 2027.
- Code: NFPA 72 lets a cellular communicator be sole path only if supervised (vendors cite 5 or 60 minute check-ins; Telguard literature cites compliance to the 2019 and earlier editions). AHJ acceptance is local.

## 1. [shock] Zero-Down Copper Exit: pay for the cutover out of the phone bill

**One-liner.** Take over the copper exit for multi-site owners at no upfront cost: use the carrier invoices to find every analog life-safety line, replace them with one brand-neutral listed communicator per site, and fund it from the POTS charges that stop, with the new recurring contract financed by RMR lenders.

**Persona (earned secret).** Telecom manager at a national retail or bank chain, 15+ years.

**Customer.** Owner-operators with 250+ sites: retail and pharmacy chains, bank branch networks, school districts, health systems, self-storage and net-lease REITs.

**Payer and signer.** Pays from the telecom opex line (carrier invoices) that the contract shrinks, plus R&M. Signer: Director of Telecom or VP Facilities; CFO countersigns because it is cash-positive from month one.

**Problem.** Discontinuance notices go to the billing contact, not the person responsible for the fire panel, and a carrier cannot tell from the account whether a line ends at a desk phone, an elevator or a fire panel. These systems do not error out; they lose dial tone and the panel only fails its daily test. An unreported alarm path is an impairment (fire watch, insurer notice, see idea 6). Meanwhile the owner pays for two monitored lines per panel at vendor-quoted $150-300 a month.

**Solution.**
1. Census: ingest carrier invoices (account, line, service class), match each line to a wire center and its public discontinuance date, rank by date.
2. Site pass: tag every analog dependency (fire panel, elevator phone, burglar, door entry, fax or POS fallback), photograph the panel.
3. Replace: one listed multi-carrier communicator per panel, analog adapters for the rest, AHJ filing.
4. Contract: 60-month all-in service at about $60-90 a site a month, below the POTS bill, hardware and install carried by us.
5. Capital: the contract is bankable RMR (lenders already finance it); lenders value fire RMR well above its build cost.
6. Phase 2: the communicator already receives every panel event in Contact ID form from any brand, which is a cross-OEM telemetry stream with no extra hardware (trouble trends, battery, test-timer gaps, verified-ITM score for insurers).

**Why now.** FCC order of March 26, 2026; 90-day notices; AT&T wire-center approvals running now; rising POTS rates; code and AHJ acceptance of cellular is mature enough (listed sole-path units exist).

**Legal premise check.** Sole-path cellular needs supervision and AHJ acceptance; state fire-alarm licensing varies, so work is done by licensed subs or our own licensed entities. I did not check whether public-sector buyers (school districts, municipalities) need special statutory authority to sign a multi-year shared-savings telecom contract, so the public-sector wedge is gated on that.

**Wedge / first 10.** A free "copper census" for 10 operators with 300-3,000 sites (a regional bank, a drugstore or convenience chain, a school district, a health system, a storage REIT). They pay nothing for the audit; we show the dated list and the savings.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).**
- Contract: assume $75 a site a month ($900 a year). 110k sites is about $99M a year.
- Build cost: $1,000-1,600 a site (device $300-600; labor, programming, AHJ filing $400-800; census). 110k sites is $110-175M deployed over about 4 years, financed against RMR (30-40x monthly is $2,250-3,000 of collateral per site).
- Cost to serve about $20-25 a month (wholesale monitoring $5-10, data about $12, support), so gross margin is about 65-70% and payback about 18-21 months.
- Share: 110k sites is 9-18% of the assumed 0.6-1.2M site pool within 4-5 years. **That is heroic-ish.** It is helped by the fact that the top ~150 multi-site operators probably hold 400-500k sites (assumption from public store counts, not re-verified) and decide centrally.

**Who does it today.** Ooma AirDial and its 41 resellers; DataRemote, POTSBOX, Telguard, Napco, Honeywell and Resideo/ADI (all boxes); integrators with national accounts (Everon, JCI, Siemens, Convergint, Securitas); telecom expense firms (Tangoe, Calero, Cass) audit invoices and stop at the bill; POTSTracker for notices. I found no one running an owner-side, financed, brand-neutral program.

**Why they lose.** Box makers sell through dealers and never see the carrier bill. TEM firms do not do field work or install. Integrators bill labor and own the panel relationship, so they will not show a chain that the cheapest answer is a standard box. Honest risk: PE roll-ups can subsidize hardware to harvest RMR (they pay 45-65x for it), so they can copy the economics.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Switching costs (5-year contract, AHJ-registered path); scale economies in carrier and central-station pricing; cornered resource in the line-to-device census per enterprise plus the wire-center calendar; counter-positioning against dealers once telemetry gives owners independent proof of ITM.

**Earned secret.** The only complete inventory of analog lines is the telecom bill, not the facilities register, and carriers retire by wire center, so the order of work is set by a public calendar. The cutover also pays for itself: owners pay for two lines on a panel that can ride a data plan.

**First 90 days.** Build the wire-center calendar from AT&T, Verizon and Lumen notices; sign 3 owners for the free census; get wholesale rates from 2 UL central stations; collect term sheets from an alarm lender; get 3 AHJs to accept one standard sole-path package; pick the reference hardware (listed multi-carrier unit).

**Outside-the-box score.** 6

## 2. [shock] Wire-Center Sweeps: convert every unconverted small system in a retiring footprint, one visit each

**One-liner.** Treat each retiring wire center like a storm-response territory: build the list of every small commercial life-safety system inside it, convert them in one visit with standard kits, and sell the resulting ITM contracts and monitoring to dealers and roll-ups or keep the RMR.

**Customer.** Owner-managers of restaurants, strip malls, small offices, churches, daycare, clinics, small manufacturers. Many have no contractor of record.

**Payer and signer.** Owner or GM from R&M or telecom budgets. $1,200 once plus $45 a month, or zero-down on a 36-month term.

**Problem.** The same dial-tone failure as idea 1, but these owners do not have a telecom manager, a regional integrator or even a dealer who calls them. Their notice goes to a billing address. A dead path means fire watch in many jurisdictions (4 hours in Aurora, any length in Seattle).

**Solution.** A calendar of approved wire centers; lists from business registries and public ITM filings; pre-booked crews of 2 techs (one licensed) doing 5-6 swaps a day with pre-programmed communicators; AHJ packet; first-year ITM and monitoring contract; membership for impairments (idea 6). Contracts are either retained or sold.

**Why now.** Wire-center-by-wire-center approvals with 90-day notices (one vendor guide says AT&T's first ~500 wire centers were scheduled to begin decommissioning in June 2026; another says forced migrations start the following June, so timing is unclear). Dealers cannot canvass; roll-ups buy books rather than originate.

**Wedge / first 10.** Two approved wire centers in one metro, 800-1,500 businesses each, with a 100-door pilot and three AHJs agreeing on the acceptance packet.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).**
- Price: $1,200 install plus $45 a month ($540 a year).
- Volume: 10k, 25k and 40k conversions in years 1-3. Year 4 at 40k: install $48M, plus an RMR base of about 110k sites at $540 is $59M, so about $107M.
- Share: 40k a year against the assumed 1-2M endpoints leaving copper over about 4 years (250-500k a year) is 8-16% of annual conversions. **Heroic.** It only holds if close rates are 15% or better and the density really lowers cost per swap. The ITM contracts originated may be worth $1,000-2,000 each to a roll-up (assumption; they pay about 1-1.5x revenue for books).

**Who does it today.** Local alarm dealers on their own base; Pye-Barker and peers buying contract books; Ooma POTSTracker plus AirDial resellers (inbound lead gen, not field density); carriers send notices only.

**Why they lose.** Dealers sell to existing customers within a 30-40 mile radius; roll-ups pay 1x revenue to buy what a sweep originates for a few hundred dollars of CAC; resellers wait for inbound; carriers cannot recommend vendors.

**Moat.** Route density (scale economies) and the originated-contract book; calendar and list data.

**Earned secret.** Deadlines are by wire center, so demand arrives as local waves. A crew that has done 200 swaps in a wire center has a lower cost per swap than any dealer, and a swap is the cheapest moment in the whole industry to originate an ITM contract.

**First 90 days.** Choose 2 wire centers with approved dates; build lists; sign licensed subs in the state; run the 100-door pilot; test price points; sign a wholesale monitoring agreement; line up one roll-up as buyer of originated contracts; confirm door-to-door rules and permit needs.

**Outside-the-box score.** 6

## 3. [insider] Second-Opinion Desk: free quote checks that turn into the owner's neutral fire-and-life-safety program

**One-liner.** Check any deficiency quote for free against a price book and competing bids, then become the owner's single contract, bidding and records layer for fire and life safety across all sites, paid from the savings.

**Persona.** Owner of a fire-sprinkler contracting firm, 20+ years.

**Customer.** Owners with 50-5,000 sites: REITs, retailers, banks, K-12 and universities, health systems, hotels.

**Payer and signer.** Existing ITM and repair budget. VP Facilities or Director of Risk. Fee: 8-12% program fee, or principal margin of 20-25% when we hold the contract.

**Problem.** ITM is the loss leader; the deficiency list is the product. Cintas Fire Protection runs a roughly 50% gross margin, and one platform reports $822M of repair revenue tied to recurring inspections across its customers in 2025. The owner cannot judge whether a deficiency is urgent or fairly priced. Programming files and passwords sit with the contractor even though NFPA 72 (7.5.7 in the editions I could check) lists the site-specific software and passwords as items the installer delivers. Public owners keep signing sole-source awards to the incumbent.

**Solution.**
1. Free quote check (upload a quote, get a price comparison and an urgency call by a former sprinkler contractor).
2. A price database built from partner price books and bids.
3. Bid rounds across vetted local contractors.
4. Program mode: one contract, one set of reports, AHJ filings, one invoice, quarterly benchmark.
5. A records vault: as-builts, site-specific software, passwords, ITM history, so any contractor can take over.

**Why now.** Post-acquisition price increases at roll-ups (anecdotal), labor shortage, owners' budget scrutiny, and the copper cutover pushing capital questions onto fire budgets.

**Wedge / first 10.** Free quote checks for 20 mid-size owners (K-12 districts, bank networks), 5 converted to program.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** Principal revenue. Assume $2.5-4k a year per site of ITM plus repairs. $100M is 25-40k sites. Assume 1.5M+ sites sit with owners that have 50+ sites (assumption). That is 2-3% of sites. **Not heroic on share, but gross margin of 20-25% means $20-25M of gross profit.** The value is the price database, the vault and the contractor network.

**Who does it today.** National account arms of JCI, Everon, Siemens, Convergint, Securitas and Cintas; facilities outsourcers (JLL, CBRE, Cushman); marketplaces (ServiceChannel, Corrigo, SMS Assist); contractor tools (ServiceTrade, Inspect Point, Ember). I found no neutral, fire-specific, owner-side second opinion (not exhaustive).

**Why they lose.** Contractors earn their margin on the repairs. Outsourcers earn pass-through margin on subs. Marketplaces have no fire-code competence to say a deficiency is not urgent. None can promise neutrality.

**Moat.** Network economies (price data improves with every quote); switching costs (the vault); counter-positioning against repair-margin contractors.

**Earned secret.** A repair quote is priced by who holds the panel passwords, not by what the work costs.

**First 90 days.** Sign 3 friendly contractors for price books; run the quote checker free for 20 owners; get read access to AHJ reports through owners' Compliance Engine accounts; hire a former fire marshal for urgency calls; run the first bid round.

**Outside-the-box score.** 5

## 4. [insider] Panel Liberation: zero-down replacement of locked proprietary fire panels

**One-liner.** Move owners off panels that only one dealer can program onto open-protocol panels with zero-down monthly pricing, deliver the programming files and passwords to the owner, and bid out the ITM. The owner is paid from the lock-in premium.

**Persona.** NICET-certified fire alarm technician who spent 15+ years on closed brands.

**Customer.** Owners with legacy Simplex, Edwards or Siemens estates, especially public bodies that keep writing sole-source justifications: universities, school districts, municipalities, hospitals.

**Payer and signer.** Capital or R&M budget; Director of Facilities with purchasing. Zero-down is paid from the service premium the owner stops paying.

**Problem.** A NIST sole-source notice states Simplex's proprietary protocols mean only the Simplex dealer can modify and reprogram; a Palm Beach County agreement says the programming software is available only to factory-trained technicians; Worcester's DCU Center cites end-of-life panels and Simplex compatibility for a sole-source. Forum technicians say addressable devices rarely cross brands. The owner pays a premium for service and cannot get competitive bids.

**Solution.** Audit which panels are locked and which are end-of-life; replace with open-protocol panels that any certified dealer may program (check each brand's dealer rules); reuse wiring and conventional devices where possible; hand over the site-specific software and passwords at close-out (as NFPA 72 expects); install the standard communicator (idea 1) in the same visit; bid the ITM; finance on a monthly fee below the current service premium.

**Why now.** The copper cutover forces a visit to every panel anyway; older panels are going end-of-life (Alameda County replaced a Siemens MXL with end-of-service in 2018; Worcester; Treasury sole-source for three panels at $50-200k); sole-source rules bite public owners.

**Wedge / first 10.** A free "liberation audit" of 10 universities and school districts with Simplex 4100 estates.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** Bid data shows $50-200k for three panels, $600-625k for three buildings (Cornell), and vendor guides of $5k to $25k+ per installed panel. Assume $20k average. 5,000 replacements a year is $100M. Assume 60-80k panel replacements a year nationally (**assumption, no source**), so that is 6-8% of annual flow. After three years about 15k liberated sites at $3-4k a year of ITM, monitoring and repair adds $45-60M recurring. **Heroic-ish**, and the installed base of locked panels is unverified.

**Who does it today.** Every fire contractor does panel replacements; OEM dealers (JCI, Honeywell, Siemens, Carrier/Edwards) and national integrators. I found no one selling "escape from lock-in" as the product.

**Why they lose.** OEM dealers' service annuity depends on the lock; they cannot advertise "leave us". Independent contractors lack the capital and a standard migration process.

**Moat.** Counter-positioning against OEM dealers; process power in a repeatable migration; fleet data on which panels are locked and when they fail.

**Earned secret.** The lock is passwords and protocol, not safety. Most of the field wiring and many devices can be reused, and the AHJ only needs re-acceptance testing.

**Risks.** A Reddit specialist argues upgrading the panel is cheaper than replacing the system; some OEMs restrict reuse and could contest; owners may prefer an upgrade; AHJ re-acceptance costs.

**First 90 days.** Pick 5 public owners; run the audit; pre-clear two open panel families with their manufacturers' dealer rules; agree acceptance testing with 3 AHJs; line up a financing partner.

**Outside-the-box score.** 6

## 5. [dilemma] Full-Service Fire Protection: one flat price per square foot a year, repairs included

**One-liner.** Sell mid-size owners ITM, monitoring and all routine repairs for one flat annual price per square foot, priced from fleet data and backed by contractual liability insurance.

**Why incumbents cannot.** Time-and-materials repair is their profit engine (about 50% gross margin at Cintas Fire). A flat all-in price turns their margin into their cost, and OEM dealers also earn parts margin. Elevator contracts are routinely sold as full-maintenance agreements, but fire protection is sold inspection-only.

**Customer.** Owners with 5-100 buildings: school districts, small REITs, hospital groups, hotel owners, universities.

**Payer and signer.** The ITM and repair budget; Director of Facilities or CFO who wants budget certainty. Assumed price $0.20-0.40 per square foot a year.

**Problem.** Surprise deficiency bills of several times the ITM price; slow capital approvals; impairments that linger.

**Solution.** Price each building from age, panel brand, system types and ITM history; use the idea 1 event stream to cut visits and predict failures; include a 4-hour impairment response (idea 6) and an AHJ-pass guarantee; exclude abuse and new code upgrades; five-year terms; contractual liability or reimbursement insurance where state service-contract law requires it.

**Why now.** Roll-up price increases; labor shortage; telemetry arriving through the cutover; owners want opex predictability.

**Legal premise check.** This is a service contract, not insurance, but states regulate service contracts (registration, reserve or reimbursement insurance). Round 1 lost ideas to insurance reclassification. Check each target state before selling.

**Wedge / first 10.** 20 school districts and 10 small REITs, starting with fire alarm and extinguishers only, adding sprinklers after the first loss data.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** Average building 50k sf at $0.30 is $15k a year. 6,700 buildings is $100M. Assume 400-600k buildings sit with owners of 5-100 buildings (assumption), so 1.1-1.7% of buildings. **Not heroic on share; the risk is the loss ratio.** Gross margin must hold at 30% or better and adverse selection must be capped by building age bands.

**Who does it today.** JCI, Siemens, Honeywell managed services and performance contracts for large accounts; Cintas (route-based); contractors' own inspection-only agreements. I found no one selling flat-fee all-in fire protection broadly (not exhaustive).

**Why they lose.** See above: their pricing model punishes prevention. A hybrid entrant must run a lower cost to serve through telemetry and route density.

**Moat.** Underwriting data (process power); switching costs on five-year terms; counter-positioning.

**Earned secret (technician view, unverified).** A small set of components (batteries, detectors, flow switches, pump controllers, backflow) drives most repair spend, and their failure timing is predictable from age and trouble logs.

**First 90 days.** Get 24 months of ITM and repair history from 5 owners; fit a price model; pilot 200 buildings with a contractor holding the labor; line up a reimbursement insurer; check service-contract law in the first two states.

**Outside-the-box score.** 6

## 6. [dilemma] Impairment Desk: the first four hours after any fire system goes out of service

**One-liner.** A 24/7 desk that handles the hours after a fire alarm or sprinkler system is out of service (copper cut, panel fault, valve closed, construction shutoff): mobilize a remote or roving fire watch, notify the AHJ, central station and insurer with a time-stamped record, and dispatch the repair.

**Why incumbents cannot.** Guard companies sell hours, not outcomes (a remote watch cannibalizes them). Contractors repair but do not staff watches or file notices. Central stations cannot call owners directly (the dealer is their customer) and cannot sell to them. Insurers cannot mandate vendors. Four silos, nobody owns the hours between failure and fix.

**Evidence.** NFPA reports sprinklers operated in 92% of fires where they were present and large enough to activate and controlled the fire in 96% of those operations; when they failed, the leading reason was a shut-off system (three-fifths in 2010-14). ISO CP 04 11 protective-safeguards endorsements make keeping the sprinkler system in service a condition of coverage and require notifying the insurer of impairments (IRMI notes a 48-hour exception for certain shutoffs); failure can suspend coverage. Many AHJs require a fire watch for impairments over 4 hours (Aurora IL), 8 hours (Syracuse) or any length (Seattle). Private fire watch quotes run $35-150 an hour; municipal fees $40-150. Wood-frame builder's risk markets have asked for 24-hour on-site guards. FM Approvals Standard 3270 now covers remote fire watch systems (BlackStarTech FireSight certified for hot work), but I found no AHJ acceptance of remote watch for impairments.

**Customer.** (a) Owners of monitored commercial buildings, sold as a membership; (b) general contractors on wood-frame builder's risk; (c) central stations, dealers and roll-ups as a white label; (d) hospitals for interim life safety measures.

**Payer and signer.** Owners: operations or risk budget; property manager or Director of Risk. GCs: security and fire-watch general conditions; project executive or risk manager. Insurers indirectly via premium credits.

**Solution.**
- Intake: failure-to-communicate lists and tamper alarms from central stations, owner calls, communicator telemetry (idea 1).
- Mobilize: an on-call network of vetted guards plus camera and thermal analytics where the AHJ or insurer accepts them.
- Record: AHJ notice, insurer notice (protective-safeguards duty), central-station notice, restoration log.
- Restore: dispatch of a licensed contractor with the parts.
- Price: membership of $15-40 a site a month, per-event fees, and a per-site monthly price for builder's risk.

**Why now.** Copper cutovers create a wave of silent path failures; FM 3270 and insurers' tech openness; wood-frame scrutiny; guard wage inflation.

**Wedge / first 10.** Builder's-risk fire watch for wood-frame multifamily (clear existing budget, insurer decides acceptance, not the AHJ), then impairment memberships through two central stations.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** All volumes are assumptions; no fire-watch market number was found.
- Membership: 100k sites at $360 a year is $36M (about 5% of an assumed 2M monitored commercial fire accounts).
- Events: 40k events a year at 50 hours and $30 net is $60M (13% of an assumed 300k impairment events a year).
- Builder's risk: 150 sites at $90k a year is $13.5M.
- About $110M total, **heroic on every leg**. It dies unless AHJ impairment-notification logs show the real event count.

**Who does it today.** Guard companies' fire-watch services (Allied Universal, Securitas, GardaWorld and local fire-watch guard firms); alarm contractors' emergency service; BlackStarTech FireSight for hot work; central-station call lists; insurers' risk engineers.

**Why they lose.** See above. No one owns the whole sequence, and the guard industry loses revenue if it adopts remote watch.

**Moat.** Counter-positioning against guard hours; AHJ and insurer acceptance as a cornered resource; on-call network density.

**Earned secret (central-station view).** The failure-to-communicate list is the first sign of an impairment. The real cost is the scramble, because most owners learn about the watch requirement and the insurer-notice duty from an operator reading a script.

**First 90 days.** File public-records requests for impairment notifications in 5 cities; map which AHJs accept remote watch; get one builder's-risk carrier to accept an FM 3270 system; sign a guard network in two metros; pilot with two central stations.

**Outside-the-box score.** 7

## 7. [pattern] Hyperscale Life-Safety Crew: the scarce-trade play behind the data-center build

**One-liner.** A specialist prime for data-center fire detection, suppression and integrated systems testing, plus ITM for operating halls, with an in-house apprenticeship academy and prefabrication to relieve the scarce-labor constraint.

**Pattern.** Capex-supercycle pass-through to scarce physical trades (Comfort Systems, Quanta, IES): enter through the scarce input. My inference for why it has not been done as a stand-alone specialist: fire protection is bought as a subtrade inside the MEP package.

**Customer.** Hyperscalers, colocation operators, and the GCs and MEP primes that build for them.

**Payer and signer.** Capex line for fire and life safety; Director of Construction or VP Critical Environments; GC procurement.

**Why now.** Census data center construction ran at $50.7B annualized in April 2026 (+28% year on year; June +46%). A 2026 trade source puts fire suppression at 2.5-4% of construction cost (weak source). A secondary summary says 29% of the fire protection workforce is expected to retire by 2026 and shortfalls are 14-20%.

**Solution.** Design-build packages for detection (aspirating), pre-action, clean agent; NFPA 4-style integrated systems testing on its own schedule and budget; frame agreements that reserve capacity; an apprenticeship academy paid by customers' capacity reservations; prefab riser and header assemblies; ITM contracts for operating halls.

**Wedge / first 10.** Commissioning and integrated testing for two colocation builders in one metro, then installation.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** $50.7B x 2-3% is $1.0-1.5B a year of fire and life-safety install work; integrated testing adds an assumed $100-250M; operating-hall ITM adds more. $100M is 7-10% of the install and testing pool. **Heroic-ish and cyclical.**

**Who does it today.** Johnson Controls, Siemens, Convergint, APi Group, Comfort Systems USA and EMCOR fire protection units; regional sprinkler primes; commissioning agents; engineering firms such as Jensen Hughes.

**Why they lose.** Generalist primes spread scarce techs across all customers and do not offer reserved capacity. This is not a business-model conflict, only a focus and labor advantage.

**Moat.** Cornered resource in trained labor (the academy); process power in testing; long frame agreements.

**Earned secret (from memory, unverified).** In a data hall the cost is not the system but the false discharge: a gas release has knocked out disk arrays before. Hyperscalers pay for people who have run the test sequence before.

**Risks.** A capex pullback; hyperscalers self-perform; large primes buy the labor.

**First 90 days.** Recruit a commissioning lead; pick the two builders; secure a training partner; price an integrated-testing package.

**Outside-the-box score.** 4

## 8. [pattern] Hood-to-Alarm Program for restaurant franchise systems

**One-liner.** Sell the 20-30 largest restaurant franchisors and purchasing co-ops one program for hood cleaning (NFPA 96), kitchen suppression, extinguishers and alarm and sprinkler ITM, with proof of service for insurers and AHJs.

**Pattern.** Concentrated-operator vertical platform: a few dozen operators control many locations and run the same repetitive function. Not done because hood cleaners (thousands of small firms and one franchise brand) and fire contractors are different trades.

**Customer.** Franchisors, their supply chain arms and co-ops; franchisees.

**Payer and signer.** Franchisee R&M, mandated by brand standards. Franchisor VP Operations or supply chain; co-op CEO.

**Why now.** Insurers and landlords ask for service certificates; labor shortages in cleaning; franchisors seeking one national vendor for compliance.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** Hood cleaning runs about $1,200-$4,500 a year (price guides); suppression, extinguishers and alarm ITM add about $1-2k. Assume $4k per restaurant. 25k restaurants is $100M as principal. The BLS-style count is about 731k restaurants with employees and about 70% independent (vendor estimate), so roughly 220k chain units (assumption). That is about 11% of chain units. **Heroic**, but one franchisor decision moves 500-5,000 units.

**Who does it today.** Hoodz (BELFOR Franchise Group; had a National Account Program), local cleaners, Cintas (extinguishers and suppression), Pye-Barker, JCI/Ansul dealers, and FM platforms (ServiceChannel, Corrigo, SMS Assist) that route tickets.

**Why they lose.** Hoodz is a cleaning franchise with local variance; fire contractors do not clean; FM platforms do not own compliance proof.

**Moat.** Franchisor approval as a cornered resource; a compliance record insurers can query.

**Earned secret (unverified).** Insurers and landlords care about the cleaning certificate and suppression tag; a missing record is what hurts after a kitchen fire.

**First 90 days.** Meet 5 franchisor operations VPs; pick a 200-unit pilot; sign local cleaners and a suppression contractor in two metros; ask 2 insurers what proof they accept.

**Outside-the-box score.** 5
