# P058 - No Surprises Act IDR awards paid by health plans and self-funded employers (round 2 ideas)

Written 2026-10-14. Eight ideas, two per lens. Every number is tagged: (S) = sourced, (A) = my arithmetic or assumption, (U) = unverified claim from a secondary source, a company boast, or my own recollection.

## Pool facts used (all checked this session unless marked)

- (S) Money: WSJ analysis of CMS data, via Healthcare Dive: about $15B of IDR payouts in 2025, more than triple 2024. Georgetown/Health Affairs Forefront (Aug 26 2026), as quoted in the ERISA Industry Committee (ERIC) white paper of Sep 22 2026: 2025 alone cost $16.6B; 2022-25 total $22.4B = $15.6B paid above the QPA + $4.2B parties' internal costs + $2.7B administrative and IDR-entity fees.
- (S) Volume: about 2.6M disputes initiated in 2025; about 1.75M in H1 2026, 50% above H1 2025 (Fierce Healthcare). The administrative fee fell from $115 to $15 per party for disputes initiated on or after Jun 11 2026. Batches can hold 50 line items.
- (S) Outcomes: providers win about 85% (88% H1 2025, 85% H2 2025). Median prevailing offer 404% of QPA vs plan offer 100% (PayerPrice, 2025-Q2). 2025 median award as % of QPA: ED 315, anesthesia 315, radiology 468, pathology/lab 416, surgery 1,355, neurology/neuromuscular 2,450, plastic surgery (breast) 3,239 (Georgetown-linked table, quoted by ERIC and Davis Wright Tremaine).
- (S) H2 2025 counts (HFMA/Becker's): about 1.15M payment determinations; about 633k ED determinations (medians 296-334% of QPA); nearly 160k neurology/neuromuscular determinations (medians 1,938-2,394%). (A) So ED is 55% of determinations and neuro 14%; $15B / about 2.3M determinations a year is about $6.5k per determination.
- (S) Who pays: 67% of covered workers are in self-funded plans, 80% at firms with 200+ workers (KFF via ERIC); employer coverage reaches about 154M nonelderly people, so (A) about 100M self-funded lives. ERIC says self-funded employers absorb about 90% of IDR costs; Elevance says 90% of IDR award dollars through its plans hit self-funded clients.
- (S) Concentration: the top 10 initiating parties filed about 66-69% of disputes in 2025 and the top four (HaloMD, TeamHealth, Radiology Partners, SCP) 56% of H1 2025 (ERIC). Payers: UHC, Aetna, Cigna, BCBS Texas = 1.61M + 1.00M + 0.74M + 0.62M of 5.3M determined line items through 2025-Q2 (PayerPrice), so (A) about 75%.
- (S) Controllable levers are not where people look: 22% of H1 2025 determinations were defaults, but providers won only 59-75% of defaults vs about 86% of contested cases; one sponsor's "QPA plus 10%" offers were "functionally random" after two years; provider win rate by IDR entity ranges from 19% to 99% (Georgetown 2024). Entity names are not in the public file, so only parties see the dispersion.
- (S) Employer pain: one large sponsor $3.5M (2025) to a projected $12M (2026) on about 2,000 disputes, about $4,000 excess per claim; City of San Antonio plan $40M over a $250M budget, four locations of one out-of-network ER chain = 53% of its ED claims cost, 99.6% provider win, average claim $886 to $1,846; NY Empire Plan more than $200M added claims and a near 10% premium rise; 32BJ Health Fund $37M of determinations vs $8M expected; 1199SEIU $52M above OON rates over 4,919 disputes; Teachers Health Trust (35k lives) signed a direct deal with TeamHealth in weeks and saved $500k in fees in a year (Marketplace, Oct 9 2026; ERIC). TeamHealth says it has approached about 15 employers for direct contracts; ERIC says employers balked at quotes "far above market".
- (S) TPA economics: Premera's 2026 self-funded schedule passes through the $115 federal fee and the entity fee (refunded if the plan prevails) and charges a $2,500 management fee on its own IDR cases. Health Affairs-based estimate: plans spend about $857 of internal time per dispute (via Healthgram, U). ERIC: some sponsors were pitched by contingency representatives; at least one concluded the fees would eat most of the savings.
- (S) Entities: 17 certified (Medlitix not accepting; Dane Street added Jan 2026). 2026 fees $425-$800 single, $625-$1,150 batched (2-25 lines). Loser's fee is kept by the entity, winner's refunded. Parties pick or veto within 3 business days, else random assignment. CMS says entities are often uncompensated for eligibility work; the Ops Rule keeps eligibility with entities on a 5-business-day clock. CMS convened entities Oct 1 2026; more than 7M disputes closed since 2022. Entity fees 2023-24 were $636M (Georgetown); H1 2025 admin + entity fees $844M (HFMA); (A) 2025 admin + entity fees about $1.8B ($2.7B cumulative minus $0.885B for 2022-24).
- (S) Conflict-of-interest pressure: 48 organizations asked the Departments to investigate IDR entities (May 14 2026); Rep. Pallone wrote to six arbiters; entities are paid per case and only on a determination; (U) Insurance Business reports Leonard Green holds a stake in a certified entity's parent while owning an anesthesia group that files disputes.
- (S) Law: en banc Fifth Circuit (TMA III, Aug 11 2026) held the QPA rules unlawful on ghost rates and on excluding bonus/risk-share payments; replacement rule pending; unresolved whether corrected QPAs reach past claims; further review possible. Ops Rule (May 28 2026): plan-level IDR registry, standard CARC/RARC remit codes, preferred-entity naming and conflict disclosure in the open-negotiation response, no firm penalties for missed deadlines. TiC final rule (Oct 5 2026): MRFs by network, quarterly, attested (Turquoise commentary, U). Pallone's Lower Premiums, Faster Payments Act (Oct 2026) would end arbitration for services from 2028 and pay a CPI-indexed median contracted rate (Jan 31 2019), with annual QPA audits and no judicial review; ASA, ACEP, ACR oppose; a 67-group coalition and ERIC want a benchmark or a restored QPA presumption. Not law.
- (S) Enforcement is weak: Fifth Circuit held there is no private right of action to enforce IDR awards (Supreme Court denied review Jan 2026); a NY court dismissed a state-law workaround for $1.7M of awards; CMS closed 664 late-payment-after-IDR complaints and 844 QPA complaints against payers in 2022-25; EDPMA's 2024 survey: 24% of disputes unpaid or paid incorrectly within 30 days (provider-side survey).
- (S) Competitors found: Zelis NSA Claim Advantage (AI, payer-side, Jun 2026); Claritev (company says it resolved 270k federal IDR arbitrations and negotiated 400k post-payment disputes in 2025, about a tenth of volume); Daffodil Health ($16.3M Series A Feb 2026, $20.9M total); PayerPrice (IDR analytics for providers); FAIR Health (nonprofit, NSA Reference File, about $37M revenue per aggregators, U); Serif Health and Turquoise Health (MRF data); HaloMD (27% of resolved disputes in 2025; boasts $1B a year for itself and clients, U); Pivotal Health (ex-Radix); CollectionPro; Tribunus Health (contract negotiation firm).

Honest overall read:

- Every idea needs 8-30% of its own sub-pool to reach $100M. None is clean. The strongest cluster is employer-side: 3 (Lockstep) + 2 (Sponsor Desk) + 7 (Plumb Line) + 4 (Float) share customers and data; 1 (Pre-Op Lock) is the most non-obvious and sells to payers and TPAs. 8 (Gavel) is the only one that does not need employers, and must never be affiliated with 5, 6, 7 or any party (entity independence rules bar plans, issuers, providers and affiliates).
- What I killed on the way, so nobody re-proposes it: (a) an AI defense desk for mid-size payers and TPAs. The top four payers are about 75% of determined line items and run in-house teams, Claritev already does about a tenth of volume, Zelis launched an AI product in June 2026, and contested provider win rates have been flat near 86%, so the only prize is the $857-a-dispute labor line in the 25% long tail. (b) Employer-side contingency representation: ERIC's own interviews say fees eat the savings. (c) IDR-award financing as a stand-alone: no private right of action to enforce awards. (d) A "QPA true-up" claims pool for past claims: whether corrected QPAs apply retroactively is open.
- Regime risk is the shared cliff. Pallone's bill would end arbitration for 2028 services. Ideas 1, 2, 3 and 7 survive a benchmark regime in some form (7 becomes the whole market); 4, 5, 6 and 8 are IDR-dependent and have a roughly two-year runway plus tail if it passes.

## 1. Pre-Op Lock: ancillary network check at prior authorization (INSIDER: payer network-management / utilization-management director, 15+ years)

- **One-liner:** Catch the 20x-QPA awards weeks before the surgery, by finding the out-of-network ancillary vendor a surgeon always uses and locking an in-network rate or rerouting the case at prior authorization.
- **Customer:** Mid-size TPAs and regional Blues (and the self-funded sponsors behind them) with high neuro and surgery IDR exposure. First 10: ten TPAs/regional payers with 100k-1M lives in TX, FL, AZ, TN, NY, CA (the top states by line items: TX 2.08M, FL 470k, AZ 467k, TN 314k, NY 180k, CA 119k, PayerPrice).
- **Payer and signer:** VP Network Management or Chief Medical Officer at the TPA/payer; for carve-outs the sponsor's benefits director. Budget line: cost containment / UM vendor fees. Price (A): 12-15% of verified avoided award dollars, with a floor of about $25 per scheduled-procedure authorization.
- **Problem:** The ER is the headline but scheduled care is where the multiples are. (S) Neuro/neuromuscular is about 14% of determinations at 19-24x QPA; surgery 13.6x; breast reconstruction 32x; surgery awards alone were $3.8B in 2023-25. (A) Using medians as proxies and assuming equal QPA per line, neuro dollars are about twice ED dollars despite one quarter of the count. (U) The neuro category is probably mostly intraoperative neuromonitoring and similar ancillary add-ons; the sources do not break it out. These services happen at in-network facilities with an out-of-network ancillary, the plan sees the case weeks ahead through prior authorization, and nobody checks the ancillary's network status then.
- **Solution:** A pre-service layer on the plan's prior-auth feed (X12 278 or UM portal) plus 24-36 months of claims. Learn the (surgeon, facility) to ancillary-vendor map; flag out-of-network vendors; then (1) open a single-case agreement at a benchmark-linked rate before surgery, (2) attach an authorization condition and tell the surgeon which in-network vendors exist, (3) reroute to a facility whose usual ancillaries are in-network, (4) keep a prudence file for ERISA fiduciaries. Revenue is a share of avoided awards.
- **Why now:** The 2025 data made the 20-30x outliers visible; the NYT "$440,000 breast reduction" story (Apr 2026) and ERIC's Sep 2026 interviews moved sponsors; $15 filings make every residual ancillary claim worth disputing; the Ops Rule portal gives sponsors visibility.
- **Wedge:** Spine and neuro procedures with intraoperative monitoring, plus assistant surgeons, at three TPAs in Texas, Florida and Arizona. Outcome measured against a matched control of authorizations.
- **Path to $100M (A):** Scheduled-procedure ancillary awards (neuro + surgery + plastics) assumed at 30% of $15B = $4.5B in 2025, about $6-7B in 2026E. At 12% of avoided dollars, $100M needs about $830M avoided a year, i.e. about 12-14% of the category (28% of it if only half is avoidable). Heroic but the lever is the payer's own authorization, and two of the top four payers or about 20 mid-size TPAs would carry it.
- **Who does it today:** Payer network teams negotiating single-case agreements by hand; UM vendors paid per review (eviCore, Carelon; U, general knowledge); provider-side prior-auth automation (Cohere Health); post-service repricing (Zelis, Claritev); IONM vendors (for example NuVasive Clinical Services) contracting with facilities. I found no vendor selling an ancillary-network check at authorization; a search for IONM-specific IDR strategy returned only payer coverage policies.
- **Why they lose:** UM vendors are paid per review and judged on medical necessity; network teams are judged on in-network rate, not dispute exposure; Zelis and Claritev engage after the claim exists; provider-facing prior-auth tools serve the provider.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Process power and switching costs (embedded in the authorization workflow); a pooled facility x surgeon x vendor map across payers is a data network effect. Counter-positioning against per-review vendors.
- **Earned secret:** The ER is unpredictable; the 2,450% awards are the opposite. The ancillary vendor is set by the surgeon's habit and is visible two to six weeks ahead, and one claims-history lookup predicts it.
- **First 90 days:** Pull 24 months of claims from 2-3 TPAs; build the vendor map for the top 100 spine/neuro surgeons per metro; produce a forward 90-day exposure list; negotiate 10 single-case agreements; measure avoided award vs matched control. Hire a former payer network contractor and an IONM billing lead.
- **Legal premise to check:** (U) Whether intraoperative monitoring counts as an ancillary service under the NSA (if not, notice-and-consent is possible, though rarely obtained); whether ERISA claims-procedure rules and state UM/gold-card laws allow conditioning authorization on ancillary participation; HIPAA business-associate terms for the feed.

## 2. Sponsor Desk: the employer's own IDR control tower (INSIDER: TPA claims-operations VP, 15+ years)

- **One-liner:** Give the employer that actually pays every award a control tower and a written authority grid its TPA must follow, priced flat per member, because contingency representation fails on the math.
- **Customer:** Self-funded sponsors with 1,000+ lives, public plans and Taft-Hartley funds first, mid-market through brokers later. First 10: City of San Antonio plan, NY Empire Plan, 32BJ Health Fund, 1199SEIU Funds, Teachers Health Trust, and five ERIC-member employers. All have disclosed IDR losses publicly.
- **Payer and signer:** The plan fiduciary committee: VP Total Rewards or CFO; for funds, the trustees and fund administrator. Budget: plan administrative expense paid from plan assets, next to the TPA fee. The Ops Rule says delegating to a TPA does not relieve the sponsor of responsibility and gives each self-funded plan its own registry number and portal visibility.
- **Problem:** TPAs run IDR as a billed-back cost centre ($2,500 management fee plus pass-through on a Blue plan's schedule) with no performance standard; the sponsor cannot see which provider groups drive exposure; in practice no one at the TPA has written offer, entity-strike or settlement authority from the sponsor, so claims staff take the safe default. Exposure is concentrated: one chain's four ERs were 53% of San Antonio's ED claims cost.
- **Solution:** Ingest the TPA's IDR reports, portal notices and claims; show exposure by provider group, entity, specialty and trend; flag defaults and ineligible filings; push an authority grid (offer bands, entity strike list, settlement bands, direct-contract triggers) to the TPA as plan instructions; produce fiduciary-grade reports; hand targeted cases to 1, 3, 5 and 7. Flat fee of about $3-6 per member per year (A), no savings share.
- **Why now:** ERIC's Sep 22 2026 white paper; Marketplace (Oct 9 2026); CBO's Jun 2026 call for research; the registry/portal rollout; 2027 renewals. (S) Sponsors told ERIC they "cannot reliably determine" what IDR costs them.
- **Wedge:** A free 30-day audit of the sponsor's last 24 months of IDR determinations, built from the TPA's own reports.
- **Path to $100M (A):** About 100M self-funded lives. At $5 per life per year that is 20M lives (20%). With modules 1, 3 and 7 raising revenue per life to about $12, it is 8.3M lives (8%). IDR cost per self-funded life was about $100-135 in 2025 ($15B x 65-90% / 100M), so $5 is under 5% of the spend.
- **Who does it today:** Payer/TPA-side: Zelis NSA Claim Advantage, Claritev IDR services, Daffodil's AI tool. Provider-side: PayerPrice, HaloMD, Pivotal. Consultants (Aon, Hub) publish checklists but sell no tool. Unnamed contingency representatives pitch sponsors. TPAs' own IDR reports (for example Premera's). I found no sponsor-side software.
- **Why they lose:** TPAs and carriers earn per-case fees and cannot grade themselves; Zelis and Claritev sell to TPAs, so an adversarial sponsor tool conflicts with their channel; brokers and consultants are paid by carriers (U) and sell advice; contingency fees consume the savings.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Switching costs (policy engine plus plan-level registry identity); a cross-TPA record, because sponsors use several carriers and no single TPA can bundle it; pooled entity-outcome data that no public file has.
- **Earned secret:** The offer number is not the lever. Contested provider win rate is flat near 86%, offers were "functionally random", and defaults are less provider-friendly than contests. The levers are concentration, entity choice, eligibility, and the sponsor's written authority, which nobody has ever issued.
- **First 90 days:** Obtain IDR reports from 5 sponsors; build the provider-concentration view; sign 3 paid pilots; draft an ASO addendum with ERISA counsel; get registry-ready.
- **Legal premise to check:** HIPAA access for a vendor acting as the plan's business associate; whether ASO contracts let a sponsor direct offers; (U) Texas HB 1592 lets self-funded plans opt into the state process (TDI rules Dec 2023), uptake and results unknown.

## 3. Lockstep: employer master agreements with the ten big filers (PATTERN: PBM-style benefit manager on a pass-through flow; cooperative purchasing; marketplace that finances the gap)

- **One-liner:** A multi-employer buying coalition that signs rate-card master agreements with the groups filing two-thirds of all disputes, so the plan pays a fair collared price in seven days instead of rolling dice at 404% of QPA 150 days later.
- **Customer:** Public plans, Taft-Hartley funds and large self-funded employers, acting through their TPAs; counterparties are the top-10 filers. First 10: the five public and union plans above plus five large employers; start with radiology (Radiology Partners files nearly all radiology disputes, S via Radiology Business) and ED.
- **Payer and signer:** The sponsor, same as idea 2, from its claims budget; Lockstep takes 10-12% of verified savings against an expected-IDR counterfactual plus a fast-pay spread shared with idea 4. On the other side the group's CEO/CFO signs the master agreement.
- **Problem:** A zone of agreement exists but nobody can reach it. (A) A provider nets about 280% of QPA from a 404% award after a 20% intermediary share, about 4% cost of waiting 100-150 days, and about 10% nonpayment or underpayment risk (EDPMA: 24% unpaid or incorrectly paid at 30 days). The plan's all-in cost of that award is 404% plus about $857 of internal cost plus its entity-fee share. A contract at 260-300% with seven-day pay beats both. Carriers cannot offer it: paying above QPA voluntarily sets a floor that feeds the statutory four-year contract-history factor and their own network pricing, and their ASO schedules earn fees on disputes. Individual employers cannot judge quotes ("far above market", ERIC).
- **Solution:** (1) A sponsor coalition negotiating through a messenger model; (2) master agreement: rate card by specialty and market indexed to the QPA-of-record (idea 7) with a collar, three-year term, a non-precedent clause, and a regime-change clause that swaps the index if a benchmark law passes; (3) plan opt-in by instruction to the TPA, claims routed at adjudication; (4) seven-day pay through idea 4; (5) annual reconciliation and a prudence file.
- **Why now:** Employers are loud (ERIC, Marketplace); TeamHealth says it would prefer to be in network at fair rates and is approaching employers one by one; Pallone's 2028 date gives groups a reason to lock multi-year rates now; TMA III will raise QPAs and compress the award-to-QPA ratio.
- **Wedge:** Radiology plus ED for the five public/union plans; Teachers Health Trust is the existence proof (direct deal in weeks, $500k saved in fees alone).
- **Path to $100M (A):** Self-funded award dollars were $9.8-13.5B in 2025 (65-90% of $15B) and perhaps $13-20B in 2026 if H1 volume growth holds. Savings of 30% on converted flow at an 11% take means $100M needs $3.0B converted, i.e. 15-23% of self-funded flow. Heroic; the concentration helps, because about ten counterparties cover two-thirds of filings.
- **Who does it today:** TeamHealth direct deals, one employer at a time; Tribunus Health (negotiation firm quoted by HFMA); the open-negotiation modules in Zelis, Claritev (400k post-payment disputes negotiated in 2025, company-reported) and Daffodil; employers alone. I found no multi-employer neutral coalition.
- **Why they lose:** Carriers: precedent and ASO fees. Zelis/Claritev/Daffodil: paid by payers, per claim or on savings, and cannot be adversarial to TPA customers. HaloMD: its fee is a share of the award, so settling at 270% cuts its income. TeamHealth alone: no benchmark credibility, no pooled volume.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Network economies (employer volume attracts group acceptance and vice versa), counter-positioning against fee-on-dispute models, and scale in negotiation.
- **Earned secret (ED/radiology revenue-cycle director):** The groups do not want to arbitrate either. Net of intermediary cut, delay and nonpayment, a 404% award is worth about 280% to them. What they cannot do is quote a number that becomes the national floor; a coalition with a neutral benchmark can give them cover.
- **First 90 days:** Letters of intent from the five public/union plans (about 1.5-2M lives, A); antitrust counsel on the messenger model; ERISA counsel on prudence; one pilot rate card with one national group; build the index with idea 7.
- **Legal premise to check:** Antitrust (buyer coalition, no boycott); how an IDR entity treats a settlement under the "previous contracted rates" factor; ERISA prudence and prohibited-transaction analysis for above-QPA rates; state payment laws for fully insured members (exclude them).

## 4. Float: seven-day pay against contract-rate receivables (PATTERN: float and spread on a payment rail; control the repayment channel; legal-shock claims pool)

- **One-liner:** Pay the group in seven days at a known discount, funded against contract-rate claims the plan has agreed to pay and awards from payers that reliably pay, with the remittance flowing through a lockbox the funder controls.
- **Customer:** Hospital-based physician groups signed to idea 3 or 5 agreements; independent anesthesia, radiology and EM groups first. First 10: ten independent groups with $2-10M of open awards (Becker's cites one group carrying $2.1M of unpaid awards).
- **Payer and signer:** The group's CFO or practice administrator, from its working-capital line; discount 1.5-3% (A). The plan authorizes the lockbox as part of the master agreement.
- **Problem:** Cash arrives 100-150+ days after service; the 30-day award deadline is routinely missed (EDPMA 24% unpaid or incorrect); there is no private right of action to enforce awards and CMS complaints recovered about $30M in four years, so financing against a bare award is fragile.
- **Solution:** (1) Contract-rate claims under ideas 3 and 5: pay at seven days, the plan funds the lockbox on a 30-day schedule, enforceable as an ordinary contract. (2) Determined awards: score by payer x entity x eligibility cleanliness, buy only from payers with a good payment record, take assignment of proceeds, price for lateness, decline awards with eligibility defects (Elevance alleges about 70% of HaloMD's paid disputes were ineligible, U). (3) Fund with a warehouse line and later ABS.
- **Why now:** Idea 3 creates contract receivables that did not exist; independent groups are cash-strained; the Fifth Circuit ruling and Supreme Court denial pushed risk onto whoever holds the paper.
- **Wedge:** Fast-pay for the first Lockstep deals, then determined awards from the three best-paying payers for ten independent groups.
- **Path to $100M (A):** $5B of annual volume x 2% = $100M; average 50 days outstanding means about $685M of capital. Volume: $2B of Lockstep contract flow plus $3B of independent groups' determined awards (about half of an assumed $6-7B in 2026E). Heroic without Lockstep.
- **Who does it today:** I found no source on financing IDR awards. Hidden incumbents: medical-receivables lenders and factors (U), PE-backed groups self-funding, Zelis Payments on the payer rail.
- **Why they lose:** Zelis is paid by payers and early pay erodes payer float; intermediaries are paid on the award, not on speed; banks do not understand the collateral.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Scale economies in funding cost plus a proprietary payer-payment-behavior dataset (cornered resource).
- **Earned secret:** The risk is lateness and mis-payment, not default. Priced correctly, lateness is the revenue.
- **First 90 days:** Collect award ledgers (payer, entity, dates) from three groups; fit the payer-behavior model; line up a $25M warehouse; pilot on one Lockstep deal.
- **Legal premise to check:** Validity of assignment of proceeds, true-sale vs loan treatment and state usury, champerty, HIPAA business-associate terms. This is the weakest premise in the set.

## 5. Cold Open: neutral price opinion and settlement inside open negotiation (DILEMMA: entities, carriers and intermediaries cannot do it)

- **One-liner:** A confidential, non-binding price opinion plus sealed-bid settlement inside the 30-business-day open-negotiation window, so a payer or sponsor can pay a number "a neutral said" and a provider gets certainty, before either side spends the dispute toll.
- **Customer:** TPAs, regional payers and sponsors on one side; independent groups and billing firms on the other; both pay per case. First 10: mid-size TPAs and regional Blues, plus sponsor-instructed streams from idea 2.
- **Payer and signer:** TPA VP claims operations or sponsor benefits director; provider revenue-cycle director. Budget: IDR operations cost (about $857 internal per dispute; $2,500 billed back by Premera).
- **Problem:** Open negotiation is a formality today (notices by secure email or fax at some plans). The Ops Rule moves it into the portal with a response due by business day 15 and a required preferred-entity naming, but there is still no neutral voice. Any settlement above QPA looks like a concession, so both sides go on to pay the toll: entity fee of $425-$800 to the loser, internal cost, about 54 days average to determination (2024).
- **Solution:** Idea 7's price opinion plus entity-level outcome data produce a range; each side submits a confidential reservation range; the platform clears inside the overlap; payment through idea 4; non-precedential by design; the sponsor gets a fiduciary file. Fee about $100-150 per evaluation plus 1-1.5% of settled value (A).
- **Why now:** The portal makes the window a structured step for the first time; volume at $15 will swamp internal teams; TMA III makes every QPA contestable, which means every party wants a neutral view.
- **Wedge:** Low-dollar, high-count categories (pathology/lab, radiology reads, anesthesia) where the toll exceeds the stakes, then ED.
- **Path to $100M (A):** 2026E about 3.5M disputes (H1 2026 was 1.75M, CMS count). 15% take-up = 525k evaluations x $150 = $79M, plus 40% settling at about $5k x 1.5% = $16M, about $95M. Needs about 15% of all disputes; heroic unless sponsor instructions (idea 2) supply the volume.
- **Who does it today:** Open-negotiation modules in Zelis NSA Claim Advantage, Claritev and Daffodil (all payer-paid); ADR bodies (AAA, JAMS) with no healthcare payment product at scale that I found; (U) blind-bid claim settlement in auto insurance in the 2000s.
- **Why they lose:** Entities must stay neutral and cannot predict or advise; carriers cannot name an above-QPA number without precedent; HaloMD is paid on the award; payer-paid vendors are not neutral.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Cornered resource (credibility as a neutral with courts, entities and the Departments) plus network economies once both sides use it.
- **Earned secret:** Every dispute passes through open negotiation and it is the cheapest point to settle, but no party can be first to name a number. A neutral provides cover.
- **First 90 days:** Settle 200 low-dollar disputes for two TPAs at an opinion price; track settlement rate, days saved and entity fees avoided; get a view from ERISA counsel on non-precedent language.
- **Legal premise to check:** Statute allows settlement at any time; confirm settled amounts are not evidence under the contract-history factor; must never be affiliated with an IDR entity (idea 8).

## 6. Guild: group-owned IDR and contracting mutual for independent hospital-based groups (DILEMMA: contingency intermediaries cannot do it) - weak, flagged

- **One-liner:** A mutual owned by the independent groups, with flat fees, an eligibility warranty and a built-in route into master agreements, aimed at the third of filings that are not one of the ten big filers.
- **Customer:** Independent anesthesia, radiology, EM, pathology and neonatology groups (U: over a thousand). First 10: ten groups now using a contingency vendor and carrying unpaid awards.
- **Payer and signer:** The group's managing partner or CFO, from the revenue-cycle vendor line (today a share of the award). Members hold equity; patronage dividends return profit.
- **Problem:** Intermediaries win by filing, not by collecting. (S) 42% of disputes in the last two quarters of 2025 were challenged as ineligible; Elevance's Georgia suit (May 27 2026) alleges about 70% of HaloMD disputes with a payment were clearly ineligible (U); the Ops Rule adds 5-business-day information requests with default awards; Pallone's bill sets a 2028 sunset. Independent groups bear the lost fees, the delay and the reputational risk, and no vendor advises them to convert to contracts.
- **Solution:** A group-owned entity doing flat-fee IDR operations with an eligibility warranty (the mutual eats the fees on ineligible closures), pooled outcome data (never prospective prices, for antitrust), a standing conversion pipeline into idea 3 rate cards, and access to idea 4.
- **Why now:** Insurer suits against the leading intermediary; the Ops Rule's default regime; a legislative sunset date that makes durable contracts valuable.
- **Wedge:** Warranty on eligibility for batched anesthesia and radiology disputes.
- **Path to $100M (A):** Independent groups' share of filings, 31-34%, applied to $15B is $4.7-5.1B in 2025 and $6-7.6B in 2026E. An 8% fee gives a $480-610M pool; $100M is about 20% of it. Heroic.
- **Who does it today:** HaloMD, Pivotal Health, CollectionPro (claims a 92% success rate, U), Zotec and R1 for billing, Waystar; physician societies lobby (ASA, ACEP, ACR) but run no service. No mutual found.
- **Why they lose:** A contingency vendor's economics rest on filing volume and a share of the award; an eligibility warranty or a contract recommendation cuts its revenue. A mutual returns profit to members.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Counter-positioning plus member lock-in; pooled data.
- **Earned secret (ED/radiology revenue-cycle director):** A group's real yield is the cash after ineligible closures, delay and nonpayment, not the headline award; the vendor's yield is filings.
- **First 90 days:** Recruit 10 founding groups through a state anesthesia or radiology society; audit their last 24 months of closures and unpaid awards; counsel on the mutual structure and clean-team data pooling.
- **Legal premise to check:** Antitrust on competitor data pooling; reputational risk of helping providers win more; the Pallone sunset.

## 7. Plumb Line: QPA-of-record after TMA III (SHOCK: the benchmark under every regime just became contestable)
