# P121: US farm equipment repair, maintenance and parts (round 2 ideas)

Pool: about $18B a year of repair and maintenance (2023, Senate Ag deck citing ERS, not checked against the ERS table) and $24.0B for "farm supplies and repairs" (NASS, 2024). Shock: FTC and five states settled with Deere on July 8, 2026 (10 years, dealer-equivalent repair resources for farmers and independents, anti-retaliation; still needs Judge Johnston's approval).

"(my estimate)" means I could not source it. Treat those numbers as hypotheses to test in the first 90 days.

## What the research changed

- **The diagnostics tool is already priced at almost nothing.** Deere's Operations Center PRO Service (launched July 31, 2025) lets an owner or an independent shop (with the owner's permission) diagnose and reprogram, reported at $195 per machine. Multi-brand Jaltest runs about $4-7k plus $1-2k a year. A diagnostics subscription is dead.
- **Deere is already cutting parts prices.** It lowered prices on almost 190,000 parts in 12 months and starts a second-tier "alternative" part this summer, sold through dealers "and other places customers buy ag parts today" (DTN, March 2026). Deere also expects about $1.2B of tariff expense in 2026, roughly 3x 2025. A cheap-parts story alone will be answered by the OEM.
- **Dealers make their money in the shop and at the parts counter.** Titan Machinery FY2026 (year to Jan 31, 2026): total revenue $2.43B, service revenue $177.9M at a 61.5% gross margin, parts revenue about $428M. Service is 28.6% of gross profit and parts 34.6%, so about 63% of gross profit comes from the two lines the settlement attacks.
- **Technicians are the binding constraint.** BLS counted 36,830 farm equipment mechanics (May 2023, mean pay $52,120). NAEDA says the average dealer would hire five more; in a Sept 2023 poll 65% of dealers expected losses above 9% of revenue from unfilled technician jobs.
- **Independents already do most installs.** A NAEDA-cited survey (dealer trade group, so interested) says nearly 60% of parts sold by a dealer are installed by someone other than the dealer, and the average dealer sells parts to nine independent shops.
- **OEM "uptime" exists only for new machines.** Deere JD Protect gives a 24-hour uptime assurance on new X/S-Series combines if every maintenance event is done by the dealer; AGCO's Harvest Promise is European; Gleaner and Fendt programs are dealer-bound.
- **EPA helped independents too.** In Feb 2026 EPA said farmers and independent mechanics may repair DEF systems; in March it removed DEF sensor requirements. This shrinks any DEF-derate business (killed below).

## Ideas

### 1. Harvest Pit Crew (insider: custom harvester)

**One-liner.** A per-machine, per-season uptime subscription for custom harvesters and big grain farms: winter inspection, parts caches and roving techs staged along the northbound harvest route, a loaner combine, and a 24-hour-or-credit promise on the older, mixed-brand machines the OEM programs exclude.

- **Customer.** Interstate custom crews (typically 4-10 combines) and Plains grain farms running two or more combines with no real shop.
- **Payer and signer.** Crew owner or farm owner/operations manager. Paid from repairs-and-maintenance and custom-hire/rental lines, priced per machine-season (my estimate $12-18k, against custom rates near $25-30 an acre and about $46 an acre total cost for combine plus header per Nebraska Extension).
- **Problem.** A combine down in the one dry window costs more than the repair bill. OEM service levels cover only new machines and require dealer maintenance; dealers cannot promise more because they cannot fill technician jobs.
- **Solution.** Winter inspection and baseline; predicted-failure parts caches by model and hours in 4-6 depots along the Texas-to-Canada route; two-person roving tech teams with dealer-equivalent tools; a loaner pool; a service credit if a machine is not cutting within 24 hours. Year two adds sprayer and planter seasons so techs and depots earn year-round.
- **Why now.** Settlement parity plus PRO Service reprogramming let non-dealers fix electronics-heavy combines; EPA's Feb 2026 DEF guidance; Deere expects North American ag equipment sales down another 15-20% in 2026 so crews keep older machines running; AGCO's FarmerCore (mobile repair trucks) shows demand, but only via dealers.
- **Wedge.** Winter 2026-27: inspect 40-60 combines for 8-10 crews in KS/NE/SD, hire 6 ex-dealer techs, one Kansas depot. First run June 2027 on about 100 machine-seasons.
- **Path to $100M.** 6,000 combine-seasons x about $16.5k = $99M, roughly 6-9% of an estimated 70-100k combines under 15 years old (my estimate from unit sales: about 3,579 US combines sold in 2025 per a secondary reading of farmdoc data; Power Systems Research ~7,100 North American units built in 2023; not a census count). Custom crews alone likely cap near $60-70M (my estimate of ~4,000 crew combines; the only national crew count I found is 3,431 crews in 1971). The rest must come from farms plus a spring season. Revenue is gross billings at 25-30% gross margin (my estimate). Upper end is heroic share.
- **Who does it today.** Deere JD Protect, AGCO Harvest Promise (EU), GleanerCare, Fendt Gold Star, dealer pre-harvest packages, RDO Ready+, AGCO FarmerCore, crews' own mechanics and chase trucks, scattered independent mobile mechanics. No brand-agnostic route-based service found.
- **Why they lose.** A dealer sits at a fixed address in a franchise territory, earns about 61.5% margin on service hours from breakdowns, cannot service the red combine and the green tractor under one contract, and cannot hire a roaming bench.
- **Moat.** Scale economies from route density plus counter-positioning against hours-billed dealer service.
- **Earned secret.** A crew's profit is cutting hours, not repair cost per hour; its own mechanics fix most problems but lack the right part at 9 p.m. in a town with no dealer. The route is predictable (Texas/Oklahoma wheat late May, Kansas June, Nebraska and Dakotas into Aug-Sept), so parts demand can be staged by week and county. (Insider claims; verify in 30 interviews.)
- **First 90 days.** Interview 30 crew chiefs and 10 big-farm owners; sign 8 crews to paid winter inspections; hire 6 ex-dealer techs; buy PRO Service access and one multi-brand tool; line up a reinsurer for the 24-hour credit; price test $12k/$16k/$20k.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 6.

### 2. Shared Shop (insider: large-farm shop manager)

**One-liner.** Run the shop for clusters of large farms: embedded lead techs, a shared regional bench and parts cache, maintenance planned around the field calendar, priced per machine per year with an uptime promise and shared savings.

- **Customer.** Farms of about 6,500+ acres or $5M+ in sales (my estimate: 20-25k; the 2022 Census counted 105,384 farms at $1M+, producing over 75% of sales), each with 30-120 machines and 1-3 in-house techs.
- **Payer and signer.** Owner/COO or CFO; repairs line plus shop payroll and parts stock, consolidated into one per-machine fee.
- **Problem.** Big farms keep shops because dealers are slow and expensive (a Titan executive says most shop rates exceed $140 an hour), but one to three techs cannot cover five brands, electronics and hydraulics, skip services at peak, quit for dealer pay, and each farm hoards its own parts.
- **Solution.** Take over the shop. The farm's techs transfer in; a regional bench covers electronics and hydraulics; 6-12 neighboring farms share a parts cache; plans follow the field-operation calendar and machine data; pricing is a fixed per-machine fee plus pass-through at cost plus a share of savings against the trailing three-year repair spend.
- **Why now.** Dealer-parity tools lower the barrier to run electronics-heavy fleets; dealer technician shortage; trough-year cost pressure; US tractor unit sales fell from 288,187 in 2020 to 195,857 in 2025 (secondary source) so fleets are older.
- **Wedge.** One county cluster in IA/NE/KS: three farms of 8-15k acres, one hub shop; 30-day baseline then shared-savings proposal.
- **Path to $100M.** 500 accounts x about $200k all-in repair, maintenance and shop labor (my estimate: a 6,500-acre farm at $25-30 an acre plus tech payroll) = $100M gross billings, about 2% of the estimated 20-25k farms above $5M. Gross margin 15-20% (my estimate): large revenue, modest profit at first.
- **Who does it today.** In-house shops; dealer fleet and contract-hire packages (UK precedent at a 10,000-acre farm; no US equivalent found); Deere Operations Center and JD Protect; independent mobile mechanics.
- **Why they lose.** Dealers bill hours at about 60% service margin and cannot sell a fee that rewards fewer breakdowns, nor service other brands; OEM tools are single-brand.
- **Moat.** Switching costs (our people, tools, records and parts cache live inside the customer's operation) plus scale economies in shared bench and parts.
- **Earned secret.** The real cost is the five-day planting or spray window, not parts or labor. Two techs skip routine services at peak, and every farm in the county hoards parts because dealer lead times are unpredictable, so inventory that could be shared sits idle. (Insider claims; verify.)
- **First 90 days.** Interview 20 shop managers; sign 3 pilot farms in one cluster; collect three years of invoices and downtime logs; hire a lead tech and parts coordinator; draft the shared-savings contract; lease the hub shop.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 7.

### 3. Machine Passport (pattern: collectibles authentication and liquidity stack)

**One-liner.** The PSA/ACV of farm machinery: a serial-keyed condition grade and service-and-telemetry history for used tractors, combines and sprayers, sold to lenders, auctions, dealers and buyers, with warranty and financing attached at the sale.

- **Why it hasn't happened here.** The record-holders (OEMs, dealers) are counterparties and single-brand, and until the settlement only dealer tools could read the full machine history.
- **Customer.** Ag lenders, auction houses, dealer used-equipment managers, used-machine buyers.
- **Payer and signer.** Lender head of equipment finance (appraisal and monitoring lines), auction VP operations (condition reports), dealer used manager (trade-in appraisal); buyers pay for inspections at the sale.
- **Problem.** No "TractorFax" exists. OEM certified pre-owned covers only late-model, low-hour units of one brand (Deere: 8R/9R tractors up to 3 model years and 1,500 hours; S-Series combines up to 2 years and 1,000 hours). Lenders mark collateral blind.
- **Solution.** Publish an inspection standard and grade; sell inspections through auctions and lenders; build a serial-keyed record from independent shops, auction reports and machine-data reads; sell lenders monitoring and residual marks; attach warranty and financing; later add a dealer-to-dealer wholesale exchange for aged inventory.
- **Why now.** Parity tools; lenders marking collateral blind in a downturn; Tractor Zoom's Q2 2026 dealer playbook reports chronic aged inventory in 4WD tractors over 425 hp and a trough into late 2026-2027; dealer machinery inventories $5.72B in Dec 2025 (farmdoc).
- **Wedge.** Inspection and report for the largest used units sold through two auction houses and ten lenders in one region; publish the standard openly.
- **Path to $100M.** All my estimates (no sourced used-transaction count; I do not use the unverified $70-100B "used market" figures): 25,000 inspections x $1,100 = $27.5M; 150,000 lookups x $60 = $9M; 400,000 loans/leases monitored x $75 a year = $30M; warranty and financing attach on 15,000 sales x about $1,200 = $18M; wholesale fees $10-15M. About $95-100M. Needs roughly a quarter of high-value used transactions and a third of equipment lenders: heroic, a 6-8 year outcome.
- **Who does it today.** OEM certified pre-owned programs; Machinery Scope (independent inspections, appraisals and warranty since 2013); Tractor Zoom (dealer market data); TractorHouse/Machinery Pete listings; auction condition reports (Purple Wave, BigIron, RB Global). No multi-brand serial-keyed history product found.
- **Why they lose.** OEMs and dealers are counterparties and cover only their own brand; auctions earn on volume and carry no condition risk.
- **Moat.** Network economies (every shop, auction and lender adds records) plus switching costs (lender covenants reference the grade).
- **Earned secret.** The hour meter is the least informative number; idle-time ratio, derate events, controller replacements, reflashes and operating conditions predict remaining life, and only dealer tools could read them until now. (Inference; I did not verify which fields the tools expose.)
- **First 90 days.** Define the grade sheet with three master techs and two lenders; run 100 paid inspections at two auctions; test which fields PRO Service and a multi-brand tool expose; sign one lender for a 500-loan pilot.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 6.

### 4. Tech Bench (pattern: capex pass-through to scarce trades, travel-nurse model)

**One-liner.** A bench of OEM-neutral farm-equipment technicians on 13-week W-2 contracts who follow the planting and harvest waves from state to state, with a short-program training pipeline financed by employers and the new Workforce Pell.

- **Why it hasn't happened here.** Ag is small to national staffing firms (36,830 BLS-counted techs), and technician credentials are brand-specific and tied to dealers.
- **Customer.** Dealers, independent shops and large-farm shops that cannot fill technician jobs.
- **Payer and signer.** Dealer GM or service director (contracted-labor and recruiting lines), billed hourly (my estimate $85-95) against shop rates they resell at over $140.
- **Problem.** Technicians constrain every other idea here. NAEDA says the average dealer would hire five more; 70% of dealers planned technician hires in 2025; 34.7% expected losses above 20% of revenue from vacancies (Sept 2023 poll). Demand is a wave, not a level.
- **Solution.** Employ technicians (benefits, housing stipend, tool truck, OEM-neutral certification), rotate them south to north with the seasons, place them 13 weeks at a time, and build the feeder with 8-15 week programs paid by employers.
- **Why now.** Workforce Pell began July 1, 2026: programs of 150-599 clock hours, awards roughly $1,260-$4,310 for 8-15 weeks, 70% completion and 70% placement tests, governor and Education Department approval, and programs must have run a year first. The settlement shifts demand toward independents with no recruiting machine.
- **Wedge.** Spring-planting 2027 bench of 25 techs for 15 dealers and shops in the Upper Midwest.
- **Path to $100M.** About 650 FTE x 1,700 billed hours x about $90 = $99M, roughly 1.8% of BLS-counted farm equipment mechanics. Gross margin 22-28% (my estimate). Pell is a subsidy, not the revenue.
- **Who does it today.** Dealer recruiting and OEM programs (Deere recruits at the Farm Progress Show; Caterpillar reportedly wants dealers to hire 40,000 techs in two years); staffing firms (e.g., Aventure Staffing places diesel techs at a Minnesota ag dealer at $24-33 an hour); AED Foundation; community colleges. No ag-specific travelling bench found.
- **Why they lose.** Dealers compete for the same techs; generalist staffing offers no neutral credential, housing or tool trucks; schools cannot deploy.
- **Moat.** Cornered resource (a loyal, credentialed bench) plus scheduling process power. Weakest moat in the set: a staffing business until the credential has value.
- **Earned secret.** Brand certification does not travel with the tech, yet most of any repair (hydraulics, electrical, engine) is brand-agnostic; a neutral credential plus a bench that follows the wave covers peaks without permanent hires. (Inference; verify with 15 service directors.)
- **First 90 days.** Interview 20 service directors; recruit 10 pilot techs; set up employer-of-record and workers' comp; contract one community college for an 8-week pilot; check Workforce Pell eligibility in two states.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 3.

### 5. Any-Shop Plan (dilemma: open-network breakdown coverage)

**One-liner.** Mechanical-breakdown coverage and an uptime promise for out-of-OEM-warranty tractors, combines and sprayers that pays any qualified shop on a preferred price schedule, underwritten from machine data and sold through lenders and used-equipment sales.

- **Customer.** Operators of late-model high-value machines outside OEM warranty.
- **Payer and signer.** Farm operator or CFO (repair reserve); distribution signers: lender equipment-finance head, auction VP, dealer used manager.
- **Problem.** Lumpy repair bills in a downturn. OEM plans are dealer-locked (Deere PowerGard carries a $500 deductible per work order on large tractors and needs dealer service); RDO Ready+ is dealer-backed; HSB's farm equipment breakdown cover excludes tractors and combines. Machinery Scope (CNA-backed, up to 10-year-old machines) is the one real independent.
- **Solution.** Underwrite from fault history and condition grade (idea 3); price by system; pay certified shops (idea 6) on a labor and parts schedule; supply exchange components (idea 7); add a loaner for harvest and planting (idea 1).
- **Why now.** Parity tools make fault data readable outside dealers; farm-income pressure; independent repair now legitimate.
- **Wedge.** Used combines, sprayers and 4WD tractors 3-10 years old sold through two auctions and four lenders in the Plains, with one admitted or fronting carrier.
- **Path to $100M.** As carrier or program manager booking premium: 20,000 contracts x $5k = $100M, about 4-5% of an estimated 450k late-model high-value machines outside OEM coverage (my estimate, no sourced count). As a pure MGA keeping about 25%, it needs about 80,000 contracts (about 18%): heroic.
- **Legal premise.** Commercial service contracts can be treated as insurance where no exemption exists (NCOIL), so build through a licensed insurer or reimbursement policy. Adds time and capital.
- **Who does it today.** Machinery Scope, Deere PowerGard/JD Protect, OEM plans at CNH and AGCO, dealer programs, HSB for implements and electronics.
- **Why they lose.** Their plans exist to route dealer labor and genuine parts; paying an independent at about $90 an hour instead of $140+ would cannibalize the 61.5%-margin service line and OEM parts margin.
- **Moat.** Process power (loss-ratio edge from data, shop schedule, reman) plus counter-positioning.
- **Earned secret.** Dollar losses cluster in a few systems (engine and aftertreatment, drivetrain, hydraulics, electronics) and are usually foreshadowed by fault-code frequency and fluid condition the OEM can see and an independent underwriter can now read; the largest controllable cost is price per claim, not claim count. (Verify with claim files.)
- **First 90 days.** Find a fronting carrier and reinsurer; collect 12 months of repair invoices from 20 shops for loss costing; build a rate sheet by system; pilot with one lender on 200 funded machines; get a state-by-state legal opinion on the form.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 5.

### 6. Standard Shop (dilemma: multi-brand independent network with a nationwide guarantee)

**One-liner.** NAPA AutoCare for farm equipment: certified independent shops under one brand, a 12-month or 2,000-hour repair guarantee honored by any member, one parts and credit program, and farmer-facing booking.

- **Customer.** Independent ag and diesel shops (1-8 techs) that already do most installs.
- **Payer and signer.** Shop owner (dues plus purchases through the program). Farmers pay through the invoice.
- **Problem.** Farmers who would save 20-40% (my estimate) still use the dealer because nobody stands behind an independent repair and the dealer has the tools, parts and credit. Deere's Harvest Terms (0% until after harvest on its Multi-Use Account; $4.87B of revolving charge receivables at FY2025) is a retention weapon no independent can match.
- **Solution.** Certification standard (tool access, training, insurance), a nationwide guarantee backed by a reinsurer, a parts program (OEM-via-dealer, second tier, aftermarket, reman), a credit program, lead generation and booking.
- **Why now.** Parity; the AFBF memoranda (Deere, CNH, AGCO, Kubota and others, reported as 70-80% of equipment sold) give independents manuals and tools beyond Deere; trough-year cost pressure.
- **Wedge.** 40 shops across IA/IL/NE/KS under a published standard; guarantee launched with a reinsurer; one parts hub.
- **Path to $100M.** 500 member shops x about $200k a year through the program (parts, reman, supplies) = $100M, plus dues of about $600 a month ($3.6M). This is parts-distribution economics (22-28% gross margin, my estimate). No census of independent shops found; my estimate is 8-12k shops touch farm equipment, so 500 is about 5%.
- **Who does it today.** Dealers' parts counters (nine independent shops each, per NAEDA); Summit Tractors' partner network (single brand); mail-order and salvage suppliers. No multi-brand ag network or franchise found.
- **Why they lose.** Dealers are single-brand franchisees and cannot endorse rivals' repair; OEMs cannot guarantee non-OEM work without conceding the service margin.
- **Moat.** Brand (the guarantee) and network economies, plus purchasing scale. Brand takes years.
- **Earned secret.** Independents do not lose jobs on price; they lose on trust and parts credit. A shop owner knows farmers switch for 25% off if the shop can say any network shop will fix it free if it fails, and no single shop can say that. (Verify.)
- **First 90 days.** Interview 30 independent owners; sign 15 founding shops; draft the standard and warranty with a reinsurer; test a parts program through two dealers' wholesale accounts and one aftermarket distributor.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 5.

### 7. Core Bank (shock: parity plus tariffs plus an aging fleet)

**One-liner.** Win the cores and own independent remanufacturing of farm machine components: pay cash for failed engines, hydraulic pumps and motors, final drives, transmissions and electronic modules, remanufacture to OE spec with a warranty, and sell back on exchange.

- **Customer.** Independent shops, farm shops and custom crews; dealers as overflow.
- **Payer and signer.** Shop owner or farm shop manager; parts cost of goods sold.
- **Problem.** OEM reman (Deere's: available through dealers, up to about 30% cheaper than new, up to 2-year warranty on transmissions and axles) feeds on cores that dealers return to the OEM stream. Independents scrap or lose cores, and until now could not flash a replacement controller. An industry source says agricultural engine cores are hard to find in the US even though demand is high.
- **Solution.** A national cash-for-core program through shops and farm shops; a central plant with testing and flashing capability; a brand-agnostic catalog (engines from Cummins, FPT, Perkins, Yanmar and Kubota are shared across brands); next-day freight; warranty backed by a reinsurer.
- **Why now.** Settlement parity plus PRO Service reprogramming makes used and reman controllers installable by independents; Deere expects about $1.2B of 2026 tariff expense (about 3x 2025), raising new-part costs; new tractor unit sales fell from 288,187 in 2020 to 195,857 in 2025 and combines from 5,030 to 3,579 (secondary source), so repairs on older machines rise; CNH and Tractor Zoom see 2026 as the trough.
- **Wedge.** Hydraulic pumps and motors, hydrostatic drives and final drives for the top five combine and tractor platforms; one plant in Iowa or Nebraska; core buying at 150 shop locations.
- **Path to $100M.** About 35,000 exchanges a year at about $2,900 blended (my estimates: 4,000 engines at $9k, 1,000 transmissions at $12k, 6,000 hydraulic pumps and motors at $3.5k, 4,000 final drives and axles at $5k, 14,000 turbos, injectors and small items at $800, 6,000 electronic modules at $1,500) = about $105M. I have no sourced count of component failures; at a 3-5% annual event rate across roughly a million higher-value machines the reman-able event pool is far above 35,000, so this is under 10% of events (verify in shop job logs). Anchor: Deere Reman employs over 500 people at five Springfield-area sites plus a core center of over 200, so OEM reman is a substantial business.
- **Who does it today.** Deere Reman; AGCO Reman; engine makers' programs; local rebuilders and independent sellers (reman Kubota engines listed with $2,000-2,500 core charges); All States Ag Parts/Parts ASAP (used, aftermarket and reman, 24 locations, owned by Kinderhook as of the 2019 deal); Deere's new second-tier parts.
- **Why they lose.** OEM reman runs through dealers who return cores to the OEM, and cannot buy cores from independents or rebuild rival brands; local rebuilders cannot flash controllers or offer a national warranty.
- **Moat.** Cornered resource (the core stream) plus scale economies in testing and flashing and process power in OE-spec reman.
- **Earned secret.** In reman the product is the core, not the rebuilt unit; Deere treats its core center as the lifeblood of the business. The independent channel is the leak in the OEM core stream, and most failed major components are rebuildable if the housing is sound. (Insider claim; verify.)
- **First 90 days.** Interview 20 shops on cores scrapped and failure frequency; buy 200 cores of the top five SKUs; contract a rebuild partner; prove the flashing workflow; price against Deere Reman and OEM new.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 7.

### 8. Elevator Depots (shock: OEM second-tier parts go through "other places")

**One-liner.** Consigned depots of fast-moving farm parts inside co-ops and ag retailers, delivered by the retailer's own trucks and billed to the farmer's existing retailer account, which settles when grain is delivered.

- **Customer.** Ag retail and co-op locations (CHS about 400-450, sources conflict; Nutrien over 1,800 retail locations across all markets; Bushel's network covers more than 3,500 grain and ag retail facilities and over 50% of US grain origination) and their farmer customers.
- **Payer and signer.** Farmer pays on the retailer account; co-op or retailer GM/COO signs a consignment program (no capital; commission about 10-12%, my estimate).
- **Problem.** Downtime is parts latency: the dealer counter is 40 miles away and out of stock, and mail order takes days. Dealers cannot add counters without territory and cost conflicts.
- **Solution.** 300-500 SKUs of wear and maintenance parts (filters, belts, chains, bearings, hoses and fittings, knives and sections, sensors, fluids), serial-number fit lookup, auto-replenishment from regional hubs, and Deere's new second-tier alternative parts as first-party supply.
- **Why now.** Deere says its alternative parts will be sold through dealers and other places customers buy today, and it cut prices on about 190,000 parts; more repairs move to farmers and independents; retailers need non-input revenue in a down farm economy (Nutrien retail sales $17.6B in 2025, slightly down); Bushel launched a Lien Protection Program in July 2026 to digitize grain settlements.
- **Wedge.** 40 co-op sites in two states, one hub, 300 SKUs chosen from independent shops' job logs.
- **Path to $100M.** 600 depots x about $170k consigned sales = about $102M (my estimate: a rural parts counter does $300k-$1M, so this is incremental), about 3% of an estimated $3-4B of brand-agnostic wear and maintenance parts. Revenue is gross sales; contribution about 10-15% after the retailer's commission (my estimate).
- **Who does it today.** Dealer parts counters; Messick's, TISCO/SMA, A&I Products, All States Ag Parts (24 locations), Sparex (owned by AGCO since 2010), mail-order suppliers with 35,000+ SKUs, Tractor Supply and farm stores; a cautionary case is La Coop fédérée in Quebec exiting machinery parts distribution. I found no US co-op parts-distribution program at CHS or Nutrien.
- **Why they lose.** Dealers face territory and cost limits; mail order cannot deliver in hours; farm stores lack serial-fit data and replenishment.
- **Moat.** Scale economies (hub and replenishment density) plus switching costs (account and point-of-sale integration). Moderate to weak.
- **Earned secret.** A co-op manager's agronomy and fuel trucks visit every farm weekly and his account system already carries the farmer's balance to harvest; what is missing is inventory in the right place. (Common practice; settlement-at-delivery mechanics unverified here.)
- **First 90 days.** Interview 15 co-op managers; mine 500 repair invoices for the SKU list; negotiate a consignment term sheet with two co-ops; line up Deere second-tier supply and two aftermarket suppliers.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 6.

## Ideas I killed

- **Dealer-equivalent diagnostics subscription.** Deere sells PRO Service access at $195 per machine; multi-brand Jaltest exists.
- **PartsTech for ag (parts-ordering aggregator).** PartsTech has about 15,000 shops and $48.5M raised in auto; revenue not disclosed. Supplier-fee models are thin for a standalone $100M business.
- **Shop-management software for independents.** Even 10k shops at $3k a year is $30M.
- **DEF and aftertreatment retrofit service.** EPA removed DEF sensor requirements (March 2026) and plans to remove derates for new engines; the spend is shrinking.
- **Farm-machinery claims and estimating platform for insurers.** The pool is too small.
- **AI parts-counter or service-writer agents.** Roughly 4,000 dealer locations x $30k a year (my estimate) is $120M at 100% share.
- **Claims service for the $99M class settlement.** The whole fund is $99M.
- **Neutral harvest-terms card repaid from grain settlements.** Needs about 20% of the Deere Multi-Use Account book to reach $100M and operating-lender consent on crop liens; the useful part is folded into Elevator Depots.
- **Roll-up of independent shops.** A financial play, not an insider insight.
