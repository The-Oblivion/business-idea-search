# P067 US uniform, linen and workwear rental: 8 ideas (round 2)

STATUS: COMPLETE (all 8 ideas written). Written 2026-10-18. Research notes: .backups/P067.notes.md. Every version of this file: .backups/P067.vN.md.

About 30 searches and fetches (the brief said 8-15; the pool's "open, no startups" label turned out to be wrong, so I kept checking). Weekly usage was 75% at start; the user cap is 92%.

A trade article says about 450 independent uniform renters and about five national ones. CSCNetwork (buying co-op since 1979) has 180+ members. Apparel Services Network (ASN, run out of Prudential Overall Supply) groups about 20 regional industrial launderers for national accounts.

So the pool file's "no funded startups, open" is wrong about roll-ups, co-ops and GPOs. It is right that I found no venture-backed attack on route-based rental itself.

## The shared thesis

The merged Cintas+UniFirst (about 40% of the $27.7B by my arithmetic, $11.1B of roughly $27.7B; far more on the narrower $14B First Research definition) will be the first company in this industry with a real size advantage over the roughly 450 independents that hold about $11B of revenue. Independents face a PFAS capex wall, labor scarcity and a thin sales force. Eight ideas below attack different parts of that gap. Several are complements (the pool, mill and foundry make a supply side; the dealer channel, brokerage and Route Desk make a demand side), but each is written to stand alone.

## 1. Approved Buyer: a pre-cleared consolidator for rental divestitures, distressed route books and succession sales

Lens: insider (antitrust counsel who has run remedy negotiations, plus a third-generation TRSA owner)

- Customer: sellers. Cintas or UniFirst if the FTC or Canada demands divestitures, any PE owner that carves up Vestis, and independent owners with no successor. Buyers of the platform later: Elis, Alsco, large PE.
- Payer and signer: the sponsor's investment committee and the unitranche lender sign for capital. The CEO of NewCo signs each purchase. The seller signer is the Cintas general counsel or CFO if there is a remedy, or the owner of an independent.
- Problem: if a remedy is ever imposed, the FTC wants a buyer that is a viable competitor (operators, route density, money), not a financial vehicle. Divested customers leak when they did not choose the new owner. The seller's synergy case also depends on keeping customers. Independents face succession gaps and a PFAS capex wall ($1.9M-$5M per plant, TRSA-cited consultant), and Alsco and PE platforms are already buying them.
- Solution: a permanent-capital platform with the operating bench in place before any deal. That means 3 ex-regional presidents and plant GMs on signed offers, an equity pool for route service reps, and a 24-month price-lock plus month-to-month option for acquired customers. The playbook is built so that a remedy package, a Vestis fragment or ten independents can all be absorbed on one back office.
- Why now: the Second Request is certified complete and the earliest close is 2026-12-11, so a remedy decision lands in Q4 2026 to Q1 2027 if there is one. Vestis is in play. Independents' sale prices are being re-rated by the $5.5B UniFirst price (about 2.2x revenue by my arithmetic). Cintas cannot buy more.
- Wedge: sign LOIs with 5 independents in Cintas/UniFirst overlap metros before the FTC acts, so the platform is a real candidate whichever way it goes. First 10 customers: those 5 independents plus 5 more, average revenue about $10M.
- Path to $100M of revenue (bought, not organic): about 10 independents at about $10M each. At roughly 1.0x revenue entry (my assumption; no multiples were disclosed in any 2026 deal I found) that is about $110M of enterprise value, about $55M of equity at 50% leverage. A remedy package, if it comes, could be $150M-$300M of revenue and $300M-$600M of EV. Exit at 1.8x-2.2x revenue to a strategic or PE firm that missed Vestis. This needs no heroic share: $100M is under 1% of the pool.
- Who does it today: Alsco (Advanced Linen, Mar 2026; Topper's Toronto, 2025), Falcon Affiliates (Century Linen plus Angelica New England plants), Surge Private Equity (White Plains Linen, Aug 2025, its 15th platform), UniFirst (Clean Uniform, $300M), Elis NA. Generic "AI roll-up" is on the cliche list. This is not that: the edge is operator-led remedy readiness, not AI.
- Why they lose: Alsco is family-owned and capital-limited. PE platforms are generalists with no operating bench. Cintas is antitrust-blocked. Nobody else has a cleared, retention-engineered buyer ready for a package.
- Moat (7 Powers): scale economies in route density is the real one, and it only appears after the third or fourth deal. Cornered resource (the operating bench and pre-clearance) is temporary. Be honest: the moat is operational, not structural.
- Earned secret: divestitures are won or lost on whether the route service reps and the first renewal notices stay put, not on the plants. The buyer the FTC accepts is an operator with local density, so financial buyers need a bench they do not have. (The 20-30% first-year leakage on divested books is my insider estimate, not sourced.)
- First 90 days: line up $150M-$250M of equity commitments, sign the 3 operating partners, LOIs with 5 overlap-metro independents, build the retention and price-lock term sheet, and brief two lenders.
- Outside-the-box score: 4. Honest verdict: capital and execution, not insight. It is here because it is the only idea in this pool that reaches $100M without a share target.

## 2. Rental-grade garments with RFID at the loom: a nearshore mill for the people who are not Cintas

Lens: insider (laundry plant GM / garment-room manager)

- Customer: independents, Vestis, Alsco, hospital laundries and, later, the garment pool (idea 5).
- Payer and signer: the owner or purchasing VP. Budget line: merchandise in service, about 15% of revenue and replaced about every 18 months.
- Problem: a rental garment is a 100-wash asset, not an apparel item. Independents buy apparel-grade product through distributors, then pay labor to sew or press in RFID tags afterward (retrofit labor often costs more than the tag; my plant experience, unsourced). Tariffs on apparel have swung with court rulings (the IEEPA tariffs fell; the Section 122 tariffs were held unlawful by the CIT on 2026-05-07 with relief limited to named plaintiffs, then stayed, per the round-2 pattern file). Cintas has 5 captive factories and UniFirst 3; neither sells to rivals.
- Solution: a vertically integrated Mexico/CAFTA-DR cut-and-sew and finishing plant that makes rental-spec garments and linens (100+ industrial washes, emblem-ready, FR and non-FR), with a UHF RFID tag sewn in and serialized at the factory and pre-loaded into the buyer's system. Retired garments are taken back for re-emblem or fiber recovery.
- Why now: the tariff regime is volatile, so USMCA and CAFTA-DR duty-free sourcing is worth more. The merger widens the sourcing-scale gap between Cintas and everyone else. PFAS-free finishes are being forced by state law (see idea 8), so a new mill can design them in.
- Wedge: start with scrubs, chef coats, aprons and bar mops, which are standard styles where RFID-at-birth saves the most. First 10 customers: independents from the CSCNetwork and ASN rosters.
- Path to $100M of revenue (gross, product sales): about $2.2B of annual garment and linen buying by Vestis, Alsco and the independents (my estimate). $100M is about 4.5% of it. Gross margin 15-20%, so $15M-$20M of gross profit. Capital: about $40M-$60M for a plant.
- Who does it today: Cintas and UniFirst captive factories (not sold to rivals), Superior Group of Companies (FY2025 revenue about $566M, Fashion Seal Healthcare sells to healthcare laundries, dealers and distributors), Standard Textile (private, estimated $300M-$500M), 1888 Mills (towels, from memory), Carrington Textiles (joined TRSA Sep 2025 to build a US presence).
- Why they lose: Superior and Standard are apparel-first with distributor channels. Cintas and UniFirst will not sell to competitors. Nobody serializes at the loom.
- Moat (7 Powers): scale economies and switching costs once the buyer's ERP is keyed to the mill's serial numbers. Weak by itself; strong only paired with ideas 3 and 5.
- Earned secret: nobody counts the hand labor of retrofitting RFID. Also, a garment's wash count, not its age, decides when it dies, and serialization is what finally makes that measurable.
- First 90 days: pick a contract sewer in Mexico or Honduras, run 20,000 RFID-ready scrubs and aprons for 3 independents on a 12-month price, and measure wash-cycle life against their current stock.
- Outside-the-box score: 3. Honest verdict: a sensible supply play, low non-obviousness, thin margin. It matters most as the feeder for ideas 3 and 5.

## 3. The Laundry Foundry: a neutral, automated, PFAS-ready wet-processing hub for rental companies

Lens: pattern (autonomy crossing the labor-substitution threshold, in a bounded environment)

- Customer: independent rental companies (they keep the routes, the customers and the brand), Big-3 overflow, hospital systems closing in-house laundries, and hotel groups with underused on-premise laundries.
- Payer and signer: the independent's owner or COO. For hospitals, the VP of supply chain. Budget line: cost of goods (plant labor, energy, water, equipment replacement, environmental capex).
- Problem: about 450 independents each run a small plant that is sub-scale for robots and for PFAS pretreatment. The cost to treat one laundry's wastewater is quoted at $1.9M-$5M plus $100k-$500k a year (a laundry consultant cited in a TRSA article). A foldable-robot line already runs at Greif in Germany (Sewts Velum, since Nov 2022), but only large plants can pay for it.
- Solution: a "TSMC for laundry." Large hubs of 50M-75M lbs a year that wash, finish, sort by wearer with RFID and fold with robots on behalf of rental companies, and never sell to an end customer. A contract firewall protects each client's customers and data. Independents keep trucks and sales and may sell their plants to the foundry and lease back.
- Why now: PFAS capex and state laws, labor scarcity, merger-driven plant closures releasing permitted buildings, and a pure-play neutral operator being credible exactly when the largest rival gets bigger.
- Wedge: one metro hub, built from a distressed or closed plant, with first customers 5 independents within 100 miles plus one hospital system. Weekly route cycles tolerate a 100-mile haul.
- Path to $100M: at about $0.46/lb (25% of the $1.85/lb industry average; my arithmetic) $100M is about 215M lbs a year, or 1.4% of TRSA's 15B lbs. That is about 4 hubs of 55M lbs. Capital about $40M-$60M a hub, so about $200M. Needs roughly 15-20 independents' volume. The share is small; the risk is whether independents will hand over garments at all.
- Who does it today: no neutral pure-play found. Toll-style arrangements are informal and I found none documented. Large healthcare launderers (Angelica, Healthcare Linen Services Group) and Alsco compete for the same end customers. Robot builders: Sewts (Series A EUR7M, 2023), Foldibot (pre-seed), plus equipment makers (Jensen, Kannegiesser, Milnor, Girbau; named in trade coverage, not researched individually).
- Why they lose: rental companies will not give a competitor their garments and customer lists. A foundry that is contractually barred from selling to end customers removes that objection, in the way fabless chip firms trust a foundry.
- Moat (7 Powers): scale economies (fixed plant and robots spread over volume) and switching costs (RFID and ERP integration). Counter-positioning against Cintas, which cannot be a neutral processor.
- Earned secret: plant economics are set by utilization and by the wearer-specific sort and assembly step, not by washing. Independents run well under capacity. The "underwash" surprise (25-50% in audits) means real volume is lower than contracts imply, which is why you need take-or-pay.
- First 90 days: sign 3 letters of intent for volume, get an engineering quote on a retrofit plant and a PFAS pretreatment train, and do a robot-folding trial with an existing Velum or Fanuc cell.
- Outside-the-box score: 7. Honest verdict: strongest bottom-up in the file after the roll-up, because $100M is only 1.4% of pounds. It is capital-heavy and depends on independents trusting a neutral third party.

## 4. Route Desk: a managed-service layer for multi-site operators' route-based services

Lens: pattern (concentrated-operator vertical platform that widens into a system of record)

- Customer: multi-site operators with 50-2,000 locations: restaurant groups, dealership groups, dental and veterinary groups, car-wash chains, senior living, hotel management companies.
- Payer and signer: the VP of procurement, COO or CFO. Budget line: facilities and operating supplies (indirect spend). Fee: about 8% of spend, mixing a managed pass-through with a fee.
- Problem: a location manager signs each local 5-year auto-renewing contract, so headquarters sees a patchwork. Cintas has no customer above 1% of its $11.3B revenue, which tells you large chains are stitched together from local contracts. Since the merger, national bidders drop from three (Cintas, UniFirst, Vestis) to two plus regionals.
- Solution: audit and consolidate the chain's local contracts under one master agreement with one invoice, one garment spec and one RFID standard. Subcontract each market to the best local operator (an independent, Alsco, or even a Big-3 branch). Show a price index. Then widen to mats, restroom, pest, grease, shredding and fire inspection, so the product becomes the system of record for route-service spend.
- Why now: the 3-to-2 change, UniFirst repricing, Vestis uncertainty, and chains' cost pressure. A 5-year window is also opening: customers who renewed in 2021-22 reach their next notice date in 2026-27.
- Wedge: auto dealership groups and veterinary or dental groups of 50-300 sites; they already buy through GPOs and have no linen specialist. First 10 customers: 10 multi-site groups, 30-100 sites each.
- Path to $100M of net revenue: 8% net take means about $1.25B of managed spend. If multi-site accounts are about 25% of the pool (my assumption, about $7B), that is an 18% share, which is heroic. The honest version: uniform alone does not get there; it only works if the desk expands into adjacent route services and reaches about 4% of a pool several times larger. I did not size those pools.
- Who does it today: ASN (about 20 independents, industrial apparel, supplier-owned), DealerShop (a GPO for 2,000+ dealerships, uniforms among its categories, claims 10-30% savings, supplier-agreement model), CSCNetwork (buying co-op for launderers), Cintas and UniFirst national account teams, and exit consultants (Cost Analysts). I did not check facility-management platforms such as ServiceChannel.
- Why they lose: GPOs are paid by suppliers and cannot be neutral on price. ASN is supplier-owned and industrial-only. The Big 3 will not subcontract to rivals. Consultants are one-shot.
- Moat (7 Powers): network economies (more chains means more supplier density and a better price index) and switching costs (the system of record). Moderate, and it takes years.
- Earned secret: the signer is the location manager, not procurement, so the first product is "show headquarters what each location pays," which is free to the chain and paid by the savings.
- First 90 days: get invoices from 2 dealership groups, benchmark them, and publish the spread between best and worst location.
- Outside-the-box score: 5. Honest verdict: it works only if it widens; call it a bet on becoming the facility-route-services system of record.

## 5. The Garment Pool: a third-party fleet of standard garments and linens that any rental laundry can lease by the wash ("CHEP for workwear")

Lens: dilemma (the incumbents' lock-in is a financing device for stranded garments)

- Customer: independents (and Vestis and Alsco) as lessees; their month-to-month customers in hospitality, food service, healthcare and light industry as the end users.
- Payer and signer: the laundry's owner or CFO. Budget line: merchandise in service (garments are about 15% of revenue, bought on an 18-month cycle), financed today out of operating cash or bank lines.
- Problem: each garment is embroidered, sized and bought for one customer, so it is stranded if that customer leaves. That is why contracts run 5 years, auto-renew with a certified-mail notice window, carry termination fees and charge loss at replacement cost. The contract is the collateral for the fleet. Independents carry the same terms and the same capital need, and customers hate both.
- Solution: a pool owner that buys standard-spec, serialized garments (from the mill in idea 2 or any source), leases them to launderers at a fee per wear cycle, and shuttles them between plants. Customer branding goes on as a patch or name tape the customer owns. Retired or returned garments are re-issued to other customers, not scrapped. Contracts can then be month-to-month because the garment is no longer stranded.
- Why now: customers are anxious about a merger that gives the largest supplier more pricing power. Independents need cash for PFAS and automation. RFID makes per-wear metering possible. Tariff swings make owning a large inventory riskier for a small laundry.
- Wedge: standard products first (white chef coats, aprons, scrubs, lab coats, bar mops, flat linen), where patch branding is acceptable. First 10 customers: 10 independents each converting 20% of a product line.
- Path to $100M: the pool fee is about 18% of the end-customer billing it supports (garment cost about 13%, plus financing and margin; my estimate). $100M is about $550M of end-customer billing, which is about 5% of independents' $11B, or 2% of the whole pool. Fleet capital about $70M-$100M, debt-financed (a lender guide confirms fleets are financeable; I found no specialist garment lessor).
- Who does it today: none found as a third-party lessor. Big-3 re-use garments internally. Hospital scrub dispensing programs pool scrubs inside one vendor. Suppliers sell garments outright. CHEP and IFCO pool pallets and crates (analogy only). I could not confirm that MEWA in Europe runs a pooled model, so I do not rely on it.
- Why they lose: Cintas, UniFirst and Vestis would give up termination fees, loss-charge revenue and customer-specific lock-in if they pooled. Independents cannot pool alone.
- Moat (7 Powers): counter-positioning is the main one. Scale economies and switching costs (RFID ledger) come second.
- Earned secret: the five-year contract is a financing device for the garments, not a sales feature. Remove the stranded-asset risk and the lock-in has no reason to exist. (That is the insider claim; no contract text was available to test it.)
- First 90 days: buy 50,000 serialized garments, lease them to 3 independents for a 6-month pilot on one product line, build the wash-cycle ledger and a warehouse debt line, and draft the lease and loss-cap terms.
- Outside-the-box score: 8. Honest verdict: the sharpest structural insight here, and surprisingly light on capital. Biggest risk: customers who insist on custom-fit, custom-look garments, plus FR and hi-vis items that need special care.

## 6. White-label rental for uniform dealers and promotional-product distributors

Lens: dilemma (channel conflict: the Big 3 sell direct, and dealers cannot carry a fleet)

- Customer: uniform dealers, embroiderers and promotional-products distributors who sell purchase programs to small businesses and want recurring revenue. End users: small businesses now buying shirts, aprons and scrubs outright.
- Payer and signer: the small-business owner pays the rental fee; the dealer owner signs the partnership. Budget line: the customer's existing uniform budget (purchase moves to rental), and the dealer's commission.
- Problem: dealers lose the best accounts to Cintas, whose direct-sale business competes with them (Cintas promo revenue is about $257M, per ASI's distributor ranking). Dealers have no fleet, plant or route to offer rental. Small businesses dislike 5-year terms and loss charges.
- Solution: a white-label rental program under the dealer's brand. Month-to-month, posted prices, pooled garments (idea 5) washed at independents or the foundry (idea 3), delivered by a local route partner. The dealer owns the relationship and earns a recurring commission (about 10%) plus new-hire kits and emblem work. The platform supplies the fleet, billing, SLA and insurance.
- Why now: merger-anxious customers, a dealer industry looking for recurring revenue, and the pool and foundry making fleet-free rental possible.
- Wedge: 50 dealers in 3 metros with 20 accounts each in food service, auto repair and dental, about 1,000 accounts. First 10 customers: 10 dealers.
- Path to $100M of net revenue: the platform keeps about 25% of billings after paying the launderer (about 65%) and the dealer (about 10%). $100M needs $400M of billings, about 100,000 accounts at $4,000, which is about 6% of the 1.8M customer locations TRSA counts. At 20 accounts per dealer that is 5,000 dealers (the dealer count is my assumption; ASI's network is about 25,000 suppliers, distributors and decorators, not all in uniforms).
- Who does it today: Cintas runs a dealer program in Canada (cintas.ca ConsolidatedDealers). More than half of about 450 independents sell direct (trade article). I found no white-label platform for dealers.
- Why they lose: the Big 3 will not white-label a competitor's channel. Independents lack tech and a pooled fleet. Dealers lack everything but the customer.
- Moat (7 Powers): network economies (more dealers brings more routes and a better pool), switching costs (dealer customer accounts on the platform's billing).
- Earned secret: most small businesses never talk to a Cintas rep; they talk to the person who embroiders their shirts.
- First 90 days: recruit 10 dealers, pilot with one independent laundry, and test conversion of a dealer's existing purchase customers to month-to-month rental.
- Outside-the-box score: 6. Honest verdict: plausible, but needs about 6% of the market through a channel whose size I have not verified.

## 7. Route Brokerage: a commission-only, multi-supplier sales force for independents

Lens: shock (the merger's talent and customer release creates a new buyer: independents that need sales capacity)

- Customer: independent launderers with plant capacity and weak sales, plus Vestis and Alsco. The sellers are ex-Cintas and ex-UniFirst reps and managers.
- Payer and signer: the independent's owner or GM. Budget line: sales cost. Cintas spends 25.9% of Uniform Rental and Facility Services revenue on selling and administration, and independents under-invest in sales because they cannot afford a full team.
- Problem: customers follow their route service rep. Overlapping reps and managers at Cintas and UniFirst will be cut or reassigned as $375M of synergies are taken (if a third of that is labor at about $80k fully loaded, that is about 1,500 roles; my arithmetic). Independents win accounts only when they can reach them.
- Solution: a platform with a CRM, quoting and pricing tool, standard month-to-month paper, onboarding and a shared brand. Reps are paid on commission (about 20% of first-year contract value plus about 3% residuals) and can sell for non-overlapping independents in a metro. The platform keeps about 30-35% as gross margin on those commissions.
- Why now: reps and managers are about to become available, customers are about to be repriced, and independents are looking for alternatives to a hiring market they cannot win.
- Wedge: one metro with 3 independents and 6 ex-reps. First 10 customers: 10 independents.
- Path to $100M of gross commission revenue: non-Cintas/UniFirst base is about $15B (independents, Vestis, Alsco); gross new bookings about 12% a year (Cintas organic growth is 7.6% plus churn; my assumption) is about $1.8B a year. At 15% of that, the platform books about $275M a year of first-year value: 20% is $55M a year, plus residuals on a growing book (about $25M by year four), reaching about $80M-$100M by year five or six. Net of rep payouts, about 30-35% of that. Needs 15% of non-Big-2 gross new bookings, which is heroic.
- Who does it today: independents' own reps; Big-3 captive sales forces; lead-generation sites; contract-exit consultants on the buyer side. I found no commission-only multi-supplier channel.
- Why they lose: the Big 3 cannot sell for rivals. Independents cannot afford a full sales team. Individual reps lack tools and paper.
- Moat (7 Powers): weak at first (brand and tools). Becomes scale and network economies if supplier density improves service. A funded recruiting engine is the real asset.
- Earned secret: sales people are the scarce asset and accounts follow them. (That the book follows the rep is insider belief; the 25.9% selling-and-admin ratio is sourced.)
- First 90 days: recruit 6 ex-reps, sign 3 independents, build a one-page month-to-month contract, and measure close rate in one metro.
- Outside-the-box score: 5. Honest verdict: real but needs a lot of share. It is a bet on the merger releasing talent; if integration retains most reps, the pool shrinks.

## 8. Clean Effluent: PFAS source control and pretreatment as a per-pound service for textile laundries

Lens: shock (policy-created forced spend)

- Customer: industrial and healthcare laundries (NAICS 812332 lists 1,488 establishments in one unverified aggregator) and hotels' on-premise laundries.
- Payer and signer: the plant owner, CFO or EHS director. Budget line: environmental compliance capex and operating cost. TRSA's own estimate is a high-six-figure-or-more pretreatment investment for a typical laundry. A laundry consultant puts it at $1.9M-$5M installed plus $100k-$500k a year in media change-outs.
- Problem: PFAS ride on stain- and soil-resistant garments, barrier gowns, flatwork and flame-resistant fabrics. Drain samples from almost all soil classes and garment types contained PFAS. Water authorities are sampling and sending questionnaires and best-practice letters in about nine states. Ordinary dissolved air flotation or microfiltration will not reach the likely limits, and carbon or ion exchange needs very fine pre-filtration (0.05 micron) and may face limits on disposing of spent media. Laundries are only about 5% of PFAS waste (Michigan EGLE), so regulators may act slowly.
- Solution: three steps. (1) Fingerprint the fleet: screen garment SKUs and wash streams to find the few that cause most of the discharge. (2) Segregate those into a dedicated wash line. (3) Treat only that stream with a small ultrafiltration plus ion-exchange skid, with spent media managed and destroyed, and reporting to the water authority. Priced per pound, with media change-outs and reporting included. Plants that cannot host tanks route that stream to the foundry (idea 3).
- Why now: New Jersey S1281 (signed 2026-08-27) bans intentionally added PFAS in apparel, naming work uniforms, from 2028-08-27, with a PPE exclusion. New York's ban has been in force since Jan 2025. California's total organic fluorine limit falls from 100 ppm to 50 ppm in Jan 2027. Connecticut requires labels now and bans from 2028. The garment bans force new fleets; the wastewater side follows.
- Wedge: Michigan and the other sampling states, starting with the largest healthcare and FR-heavy launderers; first 10 customers are plants that have already received a water-authority letter.
- Path to $100M: cost-plus about $600k a plant per year (capex amortized over 10 years plus operating cost; my midpoint of the consultant's range) means about 170 plants. If 30% of 1,488 establishments need treatment by 2030, that is about 450 plants, so $100M needs about 38% of them. Heroic, and it assumes a rule actually requires treatment.
- Who does it today: TRSA's free PFAS Planning Toolkit and committee work, consultants, general industrial water-treatment vendors (not individually researched). I did not find a laundry-specific PFAS service company.
- Why they lose: laundries lack in-house PFAS chemistry and have little space. Generic water vendors do not understand garment sources. Free TRSA guidance does not install anything.
- Moat (7 Powers): process power (a library of garment-to-PFAS fingerprints) and scale economies in media handling. Thin.
- Earned secret: you cannot treat all the water; you find the 5-10% of garment SKUs that carry most of the PFAS and treat only that stream. (The 5-10% figure is my hypothesis; the study I found says almost all garment types showed PFAS.)
- First 90 days: sample three plants' wash streams by SKU, publish the fingerprint, and price a small skid.
- Outside-the-box score: 4. Honest verdict: this fails round 2's "compliance deadline whiplash" test today. No statute requires laundries to treat wastewater, the water-authority limits are local and the federal rules are in flux. It becomes real only when a water authority enforces a limit. Keep it as a watch item and a feeder for idea 3.
