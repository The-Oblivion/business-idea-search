# P021 US personal-auto physical-damage claims ($92B): 8 ideas (round 2)

Written 2026-10-18. About 35 searches and fetches (the brief said 8-15; I went over because the pool file's "nothing funded on ADAS calibration" turned out wrong and several first answers were thin). [M] = from general knowledge, not re-verified this run. "My arithmetic" = a number I derived, not a sourced figure.

## Pool facts used (checked this run unless marked)

- Pool: about $92B incurred losses (pool file: $154.3B premiums x 59.9% loss ratio, my arithmetic). Claim count is not sourced anywhere I found. I use about 15M collision-plus-comprehensive claims a year (my arithmetic: $92B over roughly $6K a claim; HLDI puts collision frequency at 5.9 claims per 100 insured vehicle years, average collision loss $10,267 for MY2022-24).
- Total loss (TL): CCC says 23.1% of claims in 2025 (22.6% through Sept); Copart said 24.2% in Q4 2025; J.D. Power says 27% in 2025, up from 16% in 2022. At 23.1% of 15M that is about 3.5M TLs a year (my arithmetic). Verisk: personal-auto claim volume fell about 5% in 2024 and 3% in 2025. Copart US insurance units fell 9.5% (Q1 2026) and 10.7% (Q2 2026): fewer claims, higher TL share.
- Shops: North America collision market $55-60B (Boyd investor summary). Big 4 MSOs (Caliber, Crash Champions, Gerber, Classic) about 4,019 locations and 31.7% revenue share (Matthews, end 2025). Gerber 2025 revenue $3.1B and bought Joe Hudson's (258 locations, $1.3B) in Jan 2026. 130+ PE firms active. About 800 independent single-shop closures in 2024. Technician supply gap 57% (TechForce 2026). Customer-pay is about 20% of repair-order count (MSO Symposium, secondhand).
- Estimate fight: insurer-side automation is real. CCC Estimate-STP was used by 15 insurers incl. 7 of the top 10 by premium, about half of US claims volume (CCC, early 2023); Mitchell Intelligent Estimating; Audatex Qapter; CCC Intelligent Reinspection (Jul 2024). Supplements: Mitchell Q2 2018, 38.99% of estimates supplemented, average variance $948; CCC (older) 52% of appraisals with at least one supplement, about $500 per appraisal. Shop-side tools are tiny: Mitchell Estimate Review (self-audit inside UltraMate), GuideCoat (BainbridgeAI), VaraFix (launched 25 Aug 2026). No funding found for any of them.
- Parts: PartsTrader Spring 2026: OEM new parts +4.3% since the tariffs, aftermarket under 1%; Enlyte Trends 2026: OEM +4.21%, aftermarket +3.89% (2025). About 44% of OEM collision parts sold in the US are made overseas. Median delivery: OEM 8.7-9.0 days, aftermarket 2.9-3.1, recycled 3.4-3.8, price-matched 4.9-5.6. Order mix: OEM 52%, aftermarket 20%, price-matched 18%, recycled 6%. Taiwan 232 rate cut from 25% to 15% on 1 May 2026.
- Calibrations: CCC says 28.3% of repairable estimates carry one (21.8% a year earlier). KPMG: North American calibration services about $1.2B in 2024, US about $3.0B by 2030; over 90% of mechanical shops and 70% of collision shops lack in-house capability (KPMG); Revv survey: 57% of shops do some in-house, 44% fully equipped. Missed-calibration suits: 3 in 2018 to 61 in 2024 (an analyst's count); settlements $200k-$1M+ (analyst estimate). NY signed a glass calibration disclosure law 19 Dec 2025; IL HB 4373 and a MD anti-denial bill are pending. Players already here: Safelite (TruRoad), LKQ (Elitek), Caliber (Protech), Repairify asTech, AirPro, Opus IVS, Driven Brands. **The pool file's "nothing funded on calibration" is wrong.**
- TL speed and cost: FNOL to release about 19-22 days (LexisNexis 2022; Hi Marley says 20); lienholder details confirmed around day 13 in a LexisNexis timeline; storage plus rental about $54 a day (Hi Marley) to $105 a day (Canadian Underwriter 2019). Enterprise US collision length of rental: 15.1 days in Q2 2026.
- Salvage: Copart plus IAA about 80% of US salvage (one investor write-up; other sources split it 40-45% and 30-35%); insurers supply 80-85% of units; Copart owns 275+ yards. A major carrier moved volume to IAA in 2025 (carrier not verified).
- Litigation and regulation: Progressive CO projected-sold-adjustment settlement $15.24M (opt-out deadline 5 Nov 2026), Progressive NY $48M (93,000 drivers, 2024), State Farm AR $15.6M (an Audatex 9% "typical negotiation adjustment"). Condition-adjustment (CCC) classes keep failing on appeal (9th Cir. Liberty Mutual; 3rd Cir.). NAIC 12-state AI examination (pilot 2 Mar to Sep 2026) covers TL thresholds, damage assessment and parts valuation; vote on nationwide adoption at the Nov 2026 NAIC meeting; insurers are responsible for vendor AI.
- CCC itself: 2026 revenue guide $1.158-1.164B; AI revenue over $120M annualized; owns Safekeep (subrogation, 2022) and EvolutionIQ (2025); runs CCC Payments (Nvoicepay), Total Loss Care, and a Zag-powered replacement-car program. **Anything an insurer-paid platform can build, CCC is already building or buying. Every idea below therefore sits where CCC's customer base blocks it, or where it needs a yard, a van, a balance sheet or a licence.**

## Dead ends found this run (so the next reader does not redo them)

- ADAS calibration vans/network as a plain network: crowded (Safelite, LKQ, Caliber, Opus IVS, Repairify, AirPro, PE roll-ups). Only the proof/authorisation layer (idea 7) is still arguable.
- TL replacement-car leads to dealers: already run through CCC and Mitchell with TrueCar/Zag (3,000+ dealers, free to insurers). A dealer or administrator guaranteeing the ACV gap is unlicensed insurance in NY (DFS opinions 2004, 2009).
- Subrogation: CCC bought Safekeep and a top-5 insurer adopted it in Q2 2026. Arbitration Forums handled about 1.2M disputes and 2.4M subro demands in 2024.
- Claims payment rails: CCC Payments exists; I found no evidence that insurers force 3% virtual-card fees on collision shops (that is a medical-billing story).
- Policyholder-side contingency TL audit: 30+ states license public adjusters, NY's 2004 opinion excludes auto, Michigan says a contingency adjuster cannot be an independent appraiser; fee pool is small and the legal premise is a patchwork. Not worth an idea slot.

## 1. Counterweight (lens: insider, a collision estimator who became an MSO VP of operations)

- **One-liner:** A revenue-cycle outsourcer for multi-shop repairers that does for body shops what R1 does for hospitals: it fights insurers' automated estimate-stripping with OEM-backed documentation, learns what each carrier pays for, and takes a share of the money it recovers.
- **Customer:** PE-backed and independent MSOs with 8-300 locations (the Big 4 are 31.7% of revenue; Focus Advisors tracks 13 independent non-dealer MSOs with 8+ shops still adding stores).
- **Payer and signer:** The MSO pays from recovered insurer dollars (gain-share) and from the office/estimating labour line it replaces. Signer: CFO or SVP operations; the VP of insurance relations is the champion.
- **Problem:** Insurers now run estimates through straight-through and rules-based review (CCC Estimate-STP covered about half of US claims volume in early 2023; CCC Intelligent Reinspection since Jul 2024). Operations get flagged, reduced or stripped by rules the carrier configured; shops report rewriting estimates three or four times (a Collision Repair survey of senior Canadian industry figures, so not a US sample). Supplements run on 39-52% of estimates. The BainbridgeAI author (a vendor) says estimating systems default blends to 50% of a full refinish while the SCRS blend study found 31.59% more time than a full refinish. Shops have no counter-automation.
- **Solution:** Four layers, sold as one service. (1) Pre-submission completion: every estimate checked against OEM procedures and carrier rules, with citations attached. (2) A carrier-rule engine trained on every submission, approval and denial across customers (carrier x adjuster x operation x document -> approval odds). (3) A supplement desk (people plus software) that argues in carrier portals and on recorded calls, so verbal approvals become a ledger. (4) Back-office: payment posting, parts credit and return reconciliation, deductible collection.
- **Why now:** Insurer automation crossed half of claim volume. Repairable claims are down about 10% so shops are fighting for margin per job. PE platforms (130+ firms) are centralising back offices and want a measurable line. The NAIC examination makes insurers answerable for vendor AI, which gives documented disputes more weight.
- **Wedge:** Start with the three operations that are both frequently stripped and OEM-documented (calibrations/scans, adjacent-panel blend, coating removal) for 3 MSOs, charging only 12% of verified incremental approved dollars. First 10 customers: PE-backed platforms of 20-80 shops reached through Focus Advisors-type advisers.
- **Path to $100M:** Insurer-paid repair revenue is about $49B (my arithmetic: $57.5B midpoint of Boyd's range x 85%). If documented incremental operations are 2.5% of that (my assumption; the supplement data suggest $200-450 per repair order gross, of which I assume a quarter is the contestable part) that is $1.2B a year; a 12% fee is $147M, plus a platform fee of $8 on about 8.8M insurer-paid repair orders ($70M) = about $217M at full coverage. **$100M needs about 46% of insurer-paid repair orders. That is heroic.** It only works if the top 15-25 consolidators (45%+ of revenue) all adopt, which is how MSO consolidation is heading (Romans: top three at up to 37% by 2028).
- **Who does it today:** Mitchell Estimate Review (a shop self-audit tool inside UltraMate that imports carrier requirement profiles, undated); GuideCoat (BainbridgeAI); VaraFix (Aug 2026); in-house estimators and MSO central teams. CCC, Mitchell and Audatex all serve the insurer side. No funded shop-side revenue-cycle company found.
- **Why they lose:** CCC, Mitchell and Audatex are paid per claim by insurers and cannot ship a product designed to raise claim payments. Mitchell frames its tool as avoiding rejection, not as recovering money. GuideCoat and VaraFix are small founder-led tools with no carrier-behaviour dataset, no desk and no MSO distribution.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Counter-positioning against insurer-paid platforms, plus a cornered resource (carrier x operation approval data nobody else can see) and switching costs once payment posting runs through it.
- **Earned secret:** Shops do not lose most on the lines a carrier denies. They lose on the three rewrites it takes to win them, and on the lines the estimator never writes because denial is expected. A system that knows what a given carrier pays with which document converts "expected denial" into automatic inclusion and removes the rewrite.
- **First 90 days:** Sign 3 MSOs for no-fee pilots on gain-share only. Ingest 6 months of their estimate and supplement history to price the leakage baseline. Build the carrier-rule dataset from their submissions. Hire 5 former DRP managers and estimators as the desk.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 6.
- **Risks to kill it:** Carriers retaliate by deselecting DRP shops that use it; recovered-dollar attribution is disputed; the 2.5% assumption is much lower in practice.

## 2. Severity Pool (lens: insider, a DRP manager at a tier-1 carrier)

- **One-liner:** A purchasing pool for tier-2 and tier-3 personal-auto carriers and MGAs that guarantees MSOs a share of claim volume in return for big-carrier repair terms, taking an admin fee and a slice of the savings, with the guarantee backed by pooled volume rather than any single carrier.
- **Customer:** Regional mutuals, MGAs and insurtech carriers (Root, Branch, Clearcover class) that write personal auto but cannot move a labour rate or a parts discount on their own volume.
- **Payer and signer:** The carrier, from loss-adjustment expense and claims vendor budget; the savings show up in loss. Signer: chief claims officer (MGAs: COO). Success fees are booked against realised severity.
- **Problem:** The big carriers get volume-tiered labour rates, parts discounts, SLAs and warranties from MSOs. Small carriers pay more for the same repair and are on skeletal networks. State Farm has trimmed its DRP from about 20,000 shops to about half, so MSOs are choosing whom to serve.
- **Solution:** Contract once with the Big 4 and 15-20 other MSOs for a pooled-volume tier (labour rate, P&M rate, parts discount, calibration rate, cycle-time SLA, warranty). Route each member carrier's claims into it. Take $30 a claim plus 10% of verified savings, and carry the performance guarantee on severity per claim. Add rental (days), salvage and glass legs once the repair leg is running.
- **Why now:** MSOs are consolidating (Big 4 at 31.7%) and want guaranteed volume while repairable claims fall 10%. Carriers are under pressure on severity (average repair $4,818 per CCC; tariffs add about $100 of parts per repair order per PartsTrader). A Branch/CrashBay network launched in under three weeks in Jul 2026, so network setup is no longer the barrier; volume leverage is.
- **Wedge:** One state, 6-10 member carriers and 2 MSOs, repair leg only, with a published cost-per-claim guarantee. First 10 customers: MGAs and regional mutuals that already outsource claims (they have a vendor budget).
- **Path to $100M:** Assume carriers outside the top ~10 write 35-40% of personal-auto claims (my assumption; I did not pull NAIC market-share data), so about 5.5M of the 15M claims, 70% repairable with a shop (3.9M), half DRP-eligible (1.9M). Fee per claim $30 plus 10% of $330 savings (6% of $5,500) = $63. Pool revenue is $120M at 100% of the addressable base. **$100M needs about 80% of the addressable base unless the TL, rental, salvage and glass legs are added (another $40-60 a claim). With all five legs it still needs 40-50% of tier-2/3 volume. That is heroic.**
- **Who does it today:** Innovation Group (managed network of 2,500+ shops for tier 2/3 carriers), Sedgwick managed DRP, CrashBay (Branch, Jul 2026), CCC's DRP and program guidelines, Driven Brands (1,000+ DRPs), carrier in-house shop-management teams.
- **Why they lose:** Innovation Group and Sedgwick manage network quality but do not pool volume across carriers or carry a savings guarantee. CCC sells to the carriers and cannot commit one carrier's volume against another's. Top carriers will never join a pool that dilutes their own tiers. Nobody else has the cross-carrier volume.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Scale economies (pooled volume lowers every member's rate) plus a cornered resource (MSO volume commitments).
- **Earned secret:** MSOs already price by volume tier, not by who the carrier is. A 2%-share carrier cannot promise a 25% share of a market, but forty of them can, and the MSO only needs one counterparty that pays in 10 days.
- **First 90 days:** Get 3 MSOs to quote a pooled tier in one metro. Recruit 4 MGAs. Build the severity-per-claim baseline from their last 12 months. Settle the antitrust and insurance-regulatory advice (buying-group rules) before any term sheet.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 4.
- **Risks to kill it:** Antitrust treatment of a carrier buying group; MSOs unwilling to hold the tier when a member carrier's volume falls; incumbents copy the pooling.

## 3. Cycle Close (lens: pattern, outcome-priced replacement of a high-volume labour function, with a total-loss adjuster's secret behind it)

- **One-liner:** An outsourced total-loss finishing desk that is paid per closed TL file, with a days-to-payment guarantee: it takes the file from "owner accepted the valuation" to "lien paid, title/POA in hand, salvage released", which is the part that takes weeks.
- **Customer:** Tier-2 and tier-3 personal-auto carriers, MGAs and insurtech carriers whose TL queues are staffed by a handful of adjusters, plus carrier-owned rental/subscription fleets later.
- **Payer and signer:** The carrier's physical-damage claims vice-president; the budget is the claims outsourcing / TPA line, and the saving (rental and storage days) lands in loss. Signer: VP or director of physical damage claims.
- **Problem:** TL is now 23-27% of claims. FNOL-to-release runs about 19-22 days (LexisNexis, Hi Marley), and in LexisNexis's illustrative timeline the lienholder is not even confirmed until about day 13. Every day costs about $54-105 in storage and rental. Valuation takes minutes; what is slow is a bank payoff department that answers fax and phone, an owner who has not signed, a title that lives in another state. Tools exist, but someone still has to run the file.
- **Solution:** A desk of licensed adjusters and title specialists, backed by software, that owns the post-valuation leg: lien payoff and letters of guarantee by named contact, e-title/ELT and power-of-attorney e-sign, owner outreach by text then phone, state tax and fee checks, salvage assignment, payment release. Priced per closed file with a credit if it misses the day-count promise. It plugs into the carrier's existing CCC/Mitchell valuation; it does not replace it.
- **Why now:** TL share rose from 16% to 27% in three years (J.D. Power), carriers cannot hire adjusters fast enough, and the NAIC examination of TL decisions makes a documented, human-reviewed process attractive. Copart/IAA volumes and storage yards are full of cars waiting for paperwork.
- **Wedge:** The lienholder-and-title leg only, at $65 a file, for one regional carrier with about 500 TLs a month. Measure days saved on 1,000 files, then take the full file. First 10 customers: MGAs and regional mutuals that already hand work to TPAs.
- **Path to $100M:** About 3.5M TLs a year (my arithmetic). Assume carriers outside the largest ten plus MGAs hold about 40% (my assumption), so 1.4M files. A full-file price of $175 (my assumption; no price source found) is $245M at 100% of that base. **$100M needs about 570,000 files: 41% of the addressable base, about 16% of all US TLs. That is heroic.** The price passes the value test: cutting 6 days at $54-105 a day saves the carrier $324-630 a file.
- **Who does it today:** CCC Total Loss and Total Loss Care (lien payoff, letters of guarantee for insurers and lenders), Verisk and LexisNexis Risk TL connections to lienholders, Hi Marley Total Loss Assist (claims 30-40% cycle cut), Mitchell WorkCenter Total Loss, IAA's Total Loss Predictor (2026), TPAs and independent adjusters (Crawford, Sedgwick) billed by time, carrier in-house TL units. I did not find an SLA-priced finisher; I could not prove none exists.
- **Why they lose:** The software vendors sell tools per claim and carry no payment risk or day-count promise. TPAs bill hours and are paid more when files are slow. The carrier's own team is measured on accuracy, not days. Nobody is paid for the lender-to-DMV leg.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Process power (state-by-state title playbooks) and scale economies (a directory of lienholder payoff contacts and e-lien connections that gets better with every file).
- **Earned secret:** Valuation is not what makes a TL slow. The twelve days between "owner accepts" and "lien paid" are, because the lien lives in a payoff department and the owner is missing one signature. A desk that owns the lienholder relationship by name wins days, not accuracy. Carriers measure the wrong thing, so nobody manages it.
- **First 90 days:** Sign one regional carrier on the lien-and-title leg. Time-stamp 1,000 files end to end to prove the baseline. Build the top-200 lender directory and ELT state coverage. Get the adjuster-licence and E&O position settled (this is claims adjusting in every state).
- **Outside-the-box score:** 5.
- **Risks to kill it:** Adjuster licensing across 50 states; carriers keep TL in-house for customer-experience reasons; CCC/LexisNexis own the best lien data connections and could bundle an outcome price.

## 4. Same-Day (lens: pattern, marketplace-owned logistics and credit, applied to collision parts and sharpened by tariffs)

- **One-liner:** A collision-parts network that promises a delivery hour per part from pooled dismantler, distributor and dealer stock, picks the source on total repair cost (part price plus rental days plus comeback risk) rather than unit price, locks a quote for 48 hours so tariff swings do not land on the shop, and funds shops net-45 against the insurer-approved estimate.
- **Customer:** MSO procurement teams and independent shops buying about $15B of parts a year (my arithmetic: 8.5M repaired vehicles x $4,818 x an assumed 38% parts share).
- **Payer and signer:** The shop or MSO pays a fulfilment fee and the financing spread; suppliers pay a listing fee. Insurers indirectly pay through fewer rental days and can be charged a success fee. Signer: MSO VP of procurement or shop owner.
- **Problem:** A repair waits for its slowest part. OEM parts take about 9 days; aftermarket and recycled about 3. OEM is 52% of orders, and OEM prices are up 4.3% since the tariffs while aftermarket stayed under 1%. At roughly 15 rental days per claim (Enterprise) and $40-100 a day, six extra days on one part costs more than the price difference between the parts, but the estimating and procurement tools rank by price. Tariffs add about $100 of parts per repair order (PartsTrader), and prices moved between estimate and order, which shows up as supplements.
- **Solution:** (1) A delivery-hour promise on the top 300 fast-moving collision parts (bumper covers, lamps, grilles, mirrors: bumper covers are the fastest-inflating item) from a pooled stock of dismantlers, distributors and dealer counters with couriers. (2) A selector that shows total cost per part, including days of rental and the estimated comeback rate by supplier. (3) A 48-hour price lock backed by a small pooled inventory position. (4) Net-45 terms secured on the insurer-approved estimate and repaid by direction to pay. The financing leg is a hypothesis: I found no data on shop payables terms or insurer payment timing.
- **Why now:** The tariff divergence between OEM and aftermarket parts is the first time in a decade that the cheapest part and the fastest part have been different parts for most orders. Insurers are pushing alternative parts to cut cost (LKQ: alt-parts usage at a record).
- **Wedge:** 40 shops of one MSO in one metro, 4-hour delivery on 200 part numbers, with a measured cycle-time result. First 10 customers: PE-platform MSOs with central purchasing.
- **Path to $100M:** Parts flow of about $15.6B (my arithmetic). Revenue per dollar of flow: delivery/fulfilment 3% + credit and price-lock spread 1.5% + supplier listing 1% = 5.5%, or $860M at 100% of flow. **$100M needs about 11.6% of all collision parts flow, about $1.8B of GMV. That is heroic, but not absurd for a logistics network.**
- **Who does it today:** LKQ/Keystone (the largest distributor, courier network, its own recycled and aftermarket parts), PartsTrader (insurer-aligned RFQ marketplace, supplier-paid), OEConnection CollisionLink (OE parts ordering from dealers, integrated with PartsTrader), dealer parts counters, insurer programs with price-matched OEM parts (18% of orders).
- **Why they lose:** PartsTrader is paid by suppliers per transaction and run to insurer program rules, so it optimises price. LKQ cannot be neutral about source because it sells the parts and owns the recyclers. OEConnection is OE-only. None carries a delivery-hour promise or the shop's financing.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Network economies (more stock sources and more shops raise the hit rate on same-day) and scale economies in last-mile logistics density.
- **Earned secret:** A parts manager knows the price on the screen is the smallest number in the decision. The ETA the supplier gives is a guess, a wrong part comes back with a restocking fee, and the bay sits empty. The parts manager already phones three suppliers and trusts none, so a verified hour promise is worth more than a 5% discount.
- **First 90 days:** Instrument 1,000 recent orders for promised versus actual delivery. Sign 6 dismantlers and 2 couriers in one metro. Build the total-cost selector. Run the 4-hour pilot on 200 part numbers and report days saved per repair.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 4.
- **Risks to kill it:** LKQ copies the promise with its own courier fleet; the credit leg has no real demand; supplier ETA data is too weak to underwrite a guarantee.

## 5. Open Yard (lens: dilemma, what Copart and IAA structurally cannot do)

- **One-liner:** An asset-light salvage network that lists a totaled car the day it is declared, from the tow yard or shop where it already sits, and ships it once, straight to the buyer, so the insurer skips the second tow, the yard intake and the dwell time.
- **Customer:** Insurers' salvage and vendor-management teams (start with regional carriers and MGAs, which have little leverage in a duopoly), and the existing buyer base: rebuilders, dismantlers and exporters.
- **Payer and signer:** The insurer pays a per-unit seller fee that is lower than today's tow-in, intake, loading and storage stack; buyers pay a buyer fee. Signer: director of salvage or vice-president of claims vendor management.
- **Problem:** Copart and IAA hold about 80% of US salvage (one investor write-up; other sources split it 40-45% and 30-35%); insurers supply 80-85% of their units; Copart alone owns 275+ yards and a duopoly's moat is yard capacity plus bidder depth. A totaled car moves from the crash to a tow yard or shop, then to an auction yard to wait for a sale, then again to the buyer [M]. Total losses are at a record, so yards and storage bills are full. An insurer moved volume between Copart and IAA in 2025, which shows contracts are not sticky.
- **Solution:** Certify tow yards and body shops as pickup points with a standard photo and VIN-and-title capture; sell online to the same rebuilders, exporters and dismantlers, plus dealer wholesale buyers; release the car from the yard to the buyer's carrier; pay the insurer net of a lower fee. Buyers get escrowed payment and a misdescription guarantee, so the platform has to price condition risk it does not own on a lot.
- **Why now:** TL volume is up while claim volume (and therefore Copart's US insurance units: -9.5% in Q1 2026, -10.7% in Q2) is down. IAA is now a division of RB Global, so insurers want a third option. Vehicles are older and cheaper (72% of valuations are on cars 7+ years old), which makes the second tow a larger share of value.
- **Wedge:** One state, two regional carriers, 30 tow yards, only cars already sitting in a yard waiting for paperwork. Recruit 40 regional buyers from the exporters and dismantlers who already bid on Copart. First 10 customers: regional mutuals and MGAs.
- **Path to $100M:** About 3.5M TLs a year (my arithmetic). A platform take of $300 a unit (assumed: about $100 seller-side plus $200 buyer fee; I did not find either company's fee schedule) means $100M is about 333,000 units: about 9.5% of all TLs, or about 12% of Copart plus IAA's combined volume if they hold 80%. **That is a plausible share but a heroic execution problem: proceeds depend on bidder depth, which only comes with volume.**
- **Who does it today:** Copart, IAA (RB Global), small regional dismantler auctions. I searched for ACV Auctions or a startup running in-place insurer salvage and found nothing; I could not rule it out.
- **Why they lose:** Copart's returns are tied to owned yards and yard-centred fees, so a car that never enters a lot is revenue they do not earn and an asset they cannot redeploy. They cannot tell an insurer "do not send it to us". Their moat (275+ permitted yards) is exactly what Open Yard does not need.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Network economies (more buyers raise proceeds, which pulls more insurers) and counter-positioning against yard-heavy incumbents.
- **Earned secret:** The second tow exists to serve the auction company's yard, not the buyer. Most of a salvage unit's dwell time is waiting for title and lien paperwork, which a yard does not speed up. A shop or tow yard can hold the car as well as a Copart lot for free. [M, from general industry practice]
- **First 90 days:** Sign 30 yards to the capture standard. Pilot 300 TL cars with one regional carrier. Recruit 40 buyers. Set up escrow and the misdescription guarantee. Compare net proceeds per car with matched cars sold through Copart or IAA.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 6.
- **Risks to kill it:** Thin bidder depth lowers proceeds and the insurer leaves; fraud and title problems; carriers have volume commitments to Copart/IAA; stored-vehicle liability (including EV battery fires).

## 6. Dent Plan (lens: dilemma, what insurers structurally cannot price, with the shock behind it)

- **One-liner:** A prepaid repair membership sold through collision chains, dealers and credit unions for drivers who dropped or never had collision cover: a monthly fee buys a yearly repair credit at fixed menu prices, priced off the shops' slack capacity instead of insurance risk.
- **Customer:** Owners of older, cheaper vehicles who cut collision as premiums rose, and owners who now skip small claims ("keep the dent and pay the rent", PartsTrader Spring 2026).
- **Payer and signer:** The driver pays $19-29 a month. Distribution partners sign: an MSO vice-president of marketing, a dealer F&I director, a credit-union vice-president. The MSO pays a platform fee on the work it receives.
- **Problem:** Premiums rose about 46% from 2022 to 2024 (Insurify, via a secondary summary); CCC's 2026 report (via a secondary summary) found 29% of consumers cancelled or downgraded an insurance product. Repairable claims fell about 10% and personal-auto claim volume fell 5% and 3%. Customer-pay is only about 20% of shop repair orders, yet it is the highest-margin work. An insurer cannot sell sub-$1,000 cover without destroying its loss ratio, and surcharges exist to discourage small claims. The only subscription products I found (Deductify, launched Sep 2025: $10.99 a month for $1,000 of deductible cover) pay after an insurance claim, so they still need the claim and the surcharge.
- **Solution:** A 12-item menu (bumper scuff, door dent by paintless repair, headlamp restoration, windshield chip, mirror, wheel repair, trim, minor panel) at fixed prices with recycled or aftermarket parts, scheduled into slack days, with a yearly credit of about $1,500. No claim, no deductible, no surcharge. A stop-loss layer covers the tail. Structured as a service contract, or as insurance where a state requires it.
- **Why now:** Coverage drop-off and claim avoidance are measured in the same year that shop volume is falling. PE-backed MSOs (130+ firms) want volume that insurers do not steer and that is not capped by labour-rate contracts.
- **Wedge:** One MSO of 30-80 shops in one metro; sell $19.99 a month to its own past customer list and to 10 dealers' F&I menus; add 5 credit unions' auto-loan members. First 10 customers: MSO marketing heads and credit-union product managers.
- **Path to $100M:** Gross membership revenue at about $290 a year means 345,000 members for $100M. If about a quarter of roughly 250M registered vehicles lack collision cover (my assumption; I found no figure), that is about 60M vehicles, so 345,000 is 0.6% of them. On repair work, 345,000 members at half a claim a year is about 175,000 repair orders, about 8% of customer-pay orders (about 2.2M, my arithmetic). Contribution after repair cost is about 25-30% (my assumption), so the revenue is gross and the profit pool is a quarter of it. **A small share of a large pool, but the revenue figure is gross.**
- **Who does it today:** Deductify (deductible reimbursement), dealer-sold dent-and-ding, tire-and-wheel and appearance protection (F&I products, one-off and at dealer margin), Fidelis PPM (prepaid maintenance with deductible reimbursement, 2018, current status not checked), credit-union auto-protection plans that reimburse deductibles up to $500. I found no MSO selling a prepaid collision repair plan.
- **Why they lose:** Insurers cannot price small claims at frequency without wrecking rating plans, and their products are built around the claim. Deductify and credit-union plans depend on a paid claim. Dealer plans are product-by-product and priced at dealer margin. MSOs have the capacity but not the compliance, distribution or pricing engine; nobody has joined the two.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Scale economies (shop network density lowers marginal cost) and brand with the customer-pay segment; counter-positioning against insurers' claim-based pricing.
- **Earned secret:** A shop's only perishable inventory is bay and paint-booth time. Tuesday's empty booth costs the same as Friday's full one. Retail-priced customer work is the best margin in the building but few drivers can afford it, so a fixed-price plan that fills slack days at marginal cost builds a customer list insurers cannot steer.
- **First 90 days:** Pick one MSO. Price the 12 items from six months of slack-hour data. Sell to 5,000 past customers. Sign 3 dealers. Get legal opinions on insurance versus service contract in CA, TX, FL, NY and IL before any sale outside the pilot state.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 7.
- **Risks to kill it:** A state treats it as insurance (licences, reserves, rate filing); adverse selection when people join after the damage; a quality dispute with a retail customer becomes a regulatory complaint; MSOs will not jeopardise insurer referrals.
