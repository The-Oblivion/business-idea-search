# P096: Captives, group captives and fronting (round 2 ideas)

Pool: Marsh-managed captives wrote $79.1B of gross written premium in 2025 (Marsh benchmarking, published 2026-06-01). Other counts differ a lot by definition: the SOA's 2026 landscape report says about 8,000 captives and roughly $50B written worldwide (2024), while Luzern Risk cites about $240B through captives. The money that a new company can actually earn is the fee, spread and risk-bearing margin around that premium, not the premium. Generated 2026-10-14.

## What the search changed (read before the ideas)

Things I found that kill or reshape the obvious ideas:

- **The pool file said "no venture-funded startups." That is wrong for captive management.** Luzern Risk (formerly XN Captive) raised a $45M Series B in Sept 2026 led by Insight Partners (after a $12M Series A in 2025 and a 2023 seed), explicitly to make captives work for the middle market with AI-automated administration. Huscarl (YC, FRST) is building an "autonomous AI actuary" for captives. Piko Labs AI launched a reinsurance intermediary for captives and RRGs (Business Insurance listing, 2026-10-01). A generic "AI captive manager" or "AI actuary" is taken.
- **Group captives are not open.** Captive Resources (CR) says it advises 50+ group captives with 7,500 member companies and over $5.5B annual premium (Feb 2026 page); a Travelers executive puts all U.S. casualty group-captive premium at $6.5B across 90+ groups. CR reports 97-98% member retention versus high-80s to low-90s for the wider market, and projects $10B by 2030. CR itself says it serves 6,500 of about 70,000 potential casualty members and about 10,000 of about 200,000 potential medical stop-loss (MSL) clients.
- **Captive-focused fronts exist.** Concert Group (A- rating, $100M capital raise) and PRP Performa both sell fronting to captives. Fronting capacity is tight because MGAs and insurtechs need paper too (Concert CEO, Captive.com). AM Best says over $30B of premium flowed through fronting arrangements in 2025; Conning says $22B+ (up 17%), with five fronts exiting or de-emphasising and initial accident-year loss ratios developing adversely seven years running.
- **Freight-broker liability already has a Marsh product.** Marsh launched BrokerSafe in Aug 2025 (A-rated panel, up to $5M primary plus $5M London excess, with an analytics tool built with Oliver Wyman). Falvey sells a logistics liability package on Accelerant paper; TIA endorses Avalon's program; Higginbotham opened a 31-person transportation division in Oct 2026.
- **Gene-therapy pools, senior-living captives and 831(b) services are taken or too small.** Aradigm (pooled cell and gene therapy, partnered with Quantum Health in May 2026); Marsh McLennan Agency's Secure Harbor captive, Communities of Faith RRG and Healthstar RRG for senior living (and EPIC says senior-living liability rates are flat to down in 2026); micro-captive services have a tiny fee pool and legal risk (Drake Plastics, S.D. Tex., 2026-04-15 vacated the "listed transaction" label but "transaction of interest" disclosure survives).
- **Where the pressure is real and recent.** Montgomery v. Caribe Transport II (U.S. Supreme Court, 9-0, 2026-05-14): freight brokers can be sued for negligent carrier selection. Segal's 2026 stop-loss data: premiums up 12.7%, $1M+ claimants growing about 25% a year for four years, gene-therapy exclusions and wider lasering. U.S. private-equity hold periods have stretched to about 7 years on average.

## 1. [insider] Clean Exit: sponsor-level cell captives that are built to be sold with the company

**One-liner.** A sponsored-captive platform for private-equity sponsors in which every portfolio company gets a cell and every cell is born with its exit: buyer-ready accounting, a pre-priced loss portfolio transfer (LPT) at sale, and fund-level pooling above the cells.

**Persona.** Captive actuary with 15+ years, working alongside M&A insurance counsel; has watched sponsor captives get vetoed by the fund CFO at the sale.

**Customer.** Middle-market PE sponsors with 10-50 U.S. portfolio companies. Beachhead: healthcare services, dental, veterinary, behavioral and home-services platforms, where workers' comp, professional liability, EPL and cyber are material and the company is a roll-up of locations.

**Payer and signer.** The sponsor's operating partner or COO signs the program (insurance is an EBITDA lever the sponsor capitalises at exit); the fund CFO and general counsel clear related-party and LP-advisory-committee issues; each portfolio company's CFO opts in and pays premium from its insurance line.

**Problem.** Brokers pitch sponsor captives with savings of 25-50% (Horton Group; a broker figure, unverified), yet adoption is thin. Proskauer's own list of open questions for a captive covering separate portfolio companies is ownership, risk and return allocation, financing and exits. Captive.com notes that winding a captive down needs the domicile regulator's assurance that all claims are paid and no more will emerge. Long-tail lines need 5-7 years of collateral. My inference, not found in a source: the buyer's quality-of-earnings team treats retained captive earnings as non-recurring and the lender treats collateral as debt-like, so the sponsor's CFO vetoes the captive at the sale.

**Solution.**
1. A sponsored captive owned by the sponsor (or a fund-level vehicle), one protected cell per portfolio company, fronted and reinsured above an agreed retention, cell collateral in a Treasury trust.
2. An exit package written into every cell at formation: (a) transfer with the company to the buyer's program; (b) novation of the cell's run-off to the sponsor's core cell at an actuarial price; (c) a pre-priced LPT or adverse development cover from a legacy partner under a rate card (reserve plus load grid), so a sale does not wait for a bespoke deal.
3. A quality-of-earnings kit: premiums set by an independent actuary at market-equivalent rates, profits recognised in the core, a standard data room page for buyers and lenders.
4. Fund-level pooling above the cells so 30-50 companies diversify each other.

Legal premise to verify before building: tax treatment of related-but-separate insureds (risk shifting and distribution; premium deductibility), domicile rules for sponsored captives with unrelated participants, LP agreement limits on related-party fees. I did not verify any of these.

**Why now.** The mismatch between captive tails and PE holds has just closed. Average U.S. hold was about 7 years in 2025 versus about 6 in 2021, and about a third of the 13,325 unsold PE-backed companies have been held 7+ years (Solomon Partners citing PitchBook, March 2026). Exits below $1B of enterprise value are down about 28% since 2021, so sponsors need operating levers more than financial engineering. Woodruff Sawyer's 2026 outlook lists "portfolio-style programs gain momentum (again)", and the UK PRA/FCA captive consultations (CP11/26, CP26/29, closed 2026-10-14) put captives in front of sponsors' lawyers.

**Wedge / first 10.** Two or three sponsors with 15-30 healthcare-services or home-services platforms each. Thirty to forty cells in year 1 (workers' comp, PL/EPL, cyber, auto) at about $1.5M average premium is $45-60M of premium.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).**
- Pool (assumption, no source found): 13,400 PE-backed U.S. companies at about $1.5M of annual insurance spend (about 1% of a $150M-revenue company) is about $20B of premium.
- Program fee stack of 6-7% of premium (captive management and actuarial, reinsurance placement override, legacy structuring fee, tech).
- $100M needs about $1.5-1.6B of premium, roughly 1,000 portfolio companies (about 7-8% of the universe, about 8% of the premium pool), across 35-50 sponsors at 20-30 cells each. Share is not heroic; the sales cycle is. Realistic timing is year 6-7.

**Who does it today.** Brokers and captive managers sell sponsor captives case by case (Horton Group, Captive.com articles, Proskauer commentary). In my searches I found no dedicated sponsor-level facility or exit protocol launched by Marsh, Aon or Alliant in 2025-26 ("none found", not "none exist"). Insurance collateral funding providers pitch liquidity to PE. Legacy buyers (Enstar and peers, from memory) do large transfers but do not offer exit options up front. The UK regime is not live until mid-2027.

**Why they lose.** Brokers are paid on placements and annual renewals; a captive with a built-in exit takes the recurring brokerage away. Large captive managers price bespoke work for large clients. Legacy buyers prefer big deals and bespoke diligence. Nobody on the insurance side controls the sale process; a sponsor-aligned platform with deal counsel, a QoE playbook and committed LPT capacity covers the part the sponsor's CFO actually fears.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Switching costs (standard documents and equity accounts across 30-50 cells under one core); process power (an exit playbook accepted by QoE firms and lenders); cornered resource (pre-committed legacy capacity).

**Earned secret.** Captives do not fail at formation; they fail at the sale. Buyers' accountants read retained earnings as non-recurring and nobody wants a five-year tail, so the CFO quietly vetoes the captive the broker sold. Fix the exit and the savings brokers promise become bankable.

**First 90 days.** Interview three sponsor COOs and three QoE leads on how captive profits and collateral were treated in real sales. Pull five-year loss runs for 20 portfolio companies and run a cell-level feasibility. Sound out three legacy buyers for a rate card on sub-$25M reserves. Draft cell documents and the buyer data-room page. Get a tax and fund-document opinion before any pitch.

**Outside-the-box score.** 6

## 2. [insider] The 12th Member: a cross-agency fill-rate machine for group captives

**One-liner.** A neutral network that assembles new group captives from pre-scored members spread across hundreds of independent agencies, because the scarce input is the twelfth qualified, similar member in the room.

**Persona.** Group-captive agent or consultant, 15+ years, sponsor of several groups.

**Customer.** Independent agencies (the producer of record) and mid-market firms with $0.25-5M of casualty premium in industries too thin in any one agent's book to form a group. CR counts about 70,000 potential casualty members and about 200,000 potential MSL clients against 6,500 and about 10,000 served.

**Payer and signer.** The company CFO or owner (premium moves from a carrier to the captive; the fee sits inside that spend); the agency principal (flat fee plus profit share); the captive board.

**Problem.** A group needs homogeneity and critical mass. Practitioner sources say 6-12 qualified insureds and $5M+ of premium at inception (a 2004 article); Utah's regulator says at least $1M of annual premium. Each group is sponsored by one agency and filled from its book, so thin niches never form. Large consolidators earn carrier profit-sharing (my recollection, not verified here) and have no reason to steer clients out; Marsh, Aon and Gallagher serve large clients; CR is capacity-bound as a consultant.

**Solution.**
1. A free "captive fit" engine: five years of loss runs, payroll and class codes, FMCSA and OSHA records, telematics where available, producing an expected-performance score against group benchmarks.
2. An anonymous cross-agency ledger of prospects by class, size and geography.
3. A syndicate builder that composes 10-15 alike members, runs actuarial feasibility and sets terms.
4. A turnkey group captive on shared infrastructure (cells, front, reinsurance, TPA), or paid placement into an existing group.
5. The agency stays producer of record on a flat-fee schedule plus a share of profit.

Legal premise to verify: domicile licensing for group captives, producer-compensation disclosure rules, securities treatment of membership interests.

**Why now.** Casualty is still hard: Marsh Q2 2026 shows casualty up 7% and umbrella/excess up 15% (risk-adjusted) while overall U.S. commercial fell 2%; WTW says over a quarter of lead umbrella programs were restructured. Luzern's $45M Series B shows capital is now arriving for middle-market captives, but Luzern is a captive manager, not a member assembler. CR projects $10B of group premium by 2030.

**Wedge / first 10.** Twenty-five independent agencies in one vertical (regional food and beverage distributors, propane and fuel distributors, landscape and snow contractors, specialty contractors). Three syndicates of 12 members is about $40-50M of premium.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).**
- Fee stack of 4-5% of premium (formation, sponsor/program fee, share of management).
- About $2.2B of premium: roughly 2,000 members at $1.1M average (assumption), about 150 syndicates of 13, or about 3% of CR's 70,000-prospect estimate.
- **Heroic if the market does not grow:** $2.2B is about 22% of 2030 group premium ($10B). It works only if the network creates groups that would not otherwise exist, i.e. it reaches the roughly 63,500 unserved casualty prospects.

**Who does it today.** Captive Resources (consultant to agency sponsors), Roundstone and Strategic Risk Solutions, Marsh (Fleet Solutions Insurance Ltd. for transportation), Aon, Gallagher/Artex, Travelers Captive Solutions, Hylant's group captive services (MSL), Reliance Partners (transportation), Luzern Risk (digital captive manager). None found that assembles members across agencies (searched; "none found").

**Why they lose.** Every group is bound to its sponsor agency's book, and consultants are paid by sponsors, so no one is paid to move a prospect across sponsors. Consolidators have a commission conflict. Luzern runs captives; it does not fill them.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Network economies (more agencies fill groups faster, which attracts more agencies); a data asset (admission scoring built from loss runs across hundreds of agencies); switching costs once a group exists.

**Earned secret.** Capital and fronting are not the bottleneck; the twelfth qualified, similar member is. Every agent knows 40 good prospects and can name three of the twelve needed. Nobody can see the other nine because they sit in other agents' books.

**First 90 days.** Recruit 25 independent agencies in one niche. Collect 200 loss-run packages under NDA. Build and back-test the fit score against known group results. Pick one niche and sign a sponsor for the first syndicate. Line up a captive manager, a front and a reinsurer as vendors.

**Outside-the-box score.** 5

## 3. [pattern] Zero-Collateral Cell Reinsurer: a pooled $250M reinsurer that qualifies for the U.S. no-collateral rule

**Pattern transferred.** "Regulatory permission slip creates a licensed first mover" and "a federal licence that preempts a state-by-state patchwork" (digest). The NAIC's 2019 credit-for-reinsurance amendments let reinsurers from reciprocal jurisdictions post zero collateral if they hold at least $250M of capital and surplus. Bermuda, Japan and Switzerland joined the reciprocal list on 2020-01-01; all 56 NAIC jurisdictions had adopted the model by 2022; a lead-state approval can be passported through the NAIC.

**One-liner.** A Bermuda multi-cell reinsurer capitalised above $250M by hundreds of captives plus institutional money, so every group captive that is one of its cells cedes from U.S. fronts without regulatory collateral.

**Customer.** Group captives (casualty and MSL), mid-size fronted single-parent captives and rent-a-captive cells, and their sponsor agencies. Fronting carriers are the counterparty.

**Payer and signer.** Group captive board chair and member CFOs, with the sponsor's captive manager; budget line is the cost of collateral (LOC fees of 25-100 bps plus the carrying cost of cash and trust assets) and the fronting fee. The fronting carrier's chief underwriting officer signs the reinsurance.

**Problem.** Fronts usually require 125-150% of the projected loss fund as collateral, sometimes with a 20-75% buffer, set "at the sole discretion of the fronting company" and reviewed annually; release takes 3-7 years after policy expiry on long-tail lines (Captive.com, Captive International, Business Insurance; several sources are older). Regulation requires some collateral because a small captive is an unauthorised reinsurer; the front adds the rest. Fronting fees were quoted at 6-10% of premium in an older source. Individual captives cannot reach $250M, so the no-collateral route is closed to them.

**Solution.**
1. A Bermuda segregated-accounts reinsurer or Class 3A-type reinsurer, capitalised with $250M+ (member surplus contributions, surplus notes, insurance-asset-manager and ILS capital).
2. Each group captive or mid-size captive is a cell with its own loss fund and member risk-sharing; the platform's general capital backs fronts for cell default (a credit enhancement fee replaces collateral).
3. Lead-state approval, then NAIC passport, as a reciprocal-jurisdiction reinsurer; an AM Best rating (AM Best's Aug 2025 criteria opened a path to rate individual incorporated cells) so cells can meet "A- VII" contract and lender requirements.
4. Revenue: capacity fee of about 1.5-2% of premium (my assumption; set below members' net carrying cost of collateral, which I estimate at 2-4% of premium), cell management of about 2.5-3%, and a share of investment income.

Legal premise to verify (this is the whole business): whether a segregated-accounts structure's general-account capital counts toward each cell's cessions; whether a newly formed reinsurer faces seasoning or old-liability collateral rules (one summary says collateral still matters for newly formed reinsurers and old liabilities); whether fronts will cede without contractual collateral to a pooled reinsurer; state-by-state passport mechanics.

**Why now.** Fronting is the squeezed link: MGAs and insurtechs compete with captives for paper (Concert CEO, Captive.com); AM Best flags the growing reliance on offshore, unauthorised-captive and unrated reinsurers; five fronts have exited or de-emphasised; Conning says the sector is maturing into a softer market. The August 2026 CICR issue is asking whether insurance securities can replace excess cash collateral, so the question is live. Group captive premium is growing to a projected $10B by 2030.
*Why not here yet:* the framework is six years old and was built for large reinsurers; the $250M threshold is out of reach for any one captive; sponsors are agencies and consultants, not balance sheets; fronts like the discretion.

**Wedge / first 10.** Convert 6-10 existing group captives from two sponsor agencies ($400-600M of premium) into cells. Capital: $60-80M of member surplus plus about $170M of third-party capital.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).**
- Revenue of about 5% of premium all-in (assumption) means about $2B of cell premium.
- That is about 20% of 2030 group-captive premium ($10B), or about 10% of a broader $20B pool of fronted group, MSL and mid-size captive premium (my assumption). **Heroic on the narrow pool; plausible only if the pool is widened to MSL and mid-size single-parent captives.**
- Capital needed is at least $250M and it carries real tail risk if cells fail.

**Who does it today.** Traditional fronts (Old Republic, Sompo, Zurich and others, from memory) demand collateral at their discretion. Concert Group and PRP Performa are captive-focused fronts that are friendlier on terms, not collateral-free. Marsh's Mangrove cell facility (cells up 14% by number, 47% by premium in 2025), Gallagher/Artex and Aon cell programs lack a $250M balance sheet. Large RJ-qualified reinsurers (Swiss Re, Munich Re, from memory) do not write small first-loss cells. None found pooling captives into one RJ-qualified reinsurer (searched; "none found").

**Why they lose.** A front cannot waive collateral without taking unsecured credit risk on small captives, and it earns from the discretion; AM Best penalises unsecured recoverables. Broker cell facilities cannot raise $250M of risk capital from members they also place business for. CR is a consultant, not a risk-bearer.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Scale economies (capital efficiency); cornered resource (RJ approval plus rating); switching costs (cells hold members' equity); counter-positioning (fronts cannot copy without giving up collateral spread).

**Earned secret.** Collateral is a negotiating lever, not a risk measure: regulation sets a floor and the front adds the rest "at its sole discretion". The $250M threshold turns the question from "how much will this $20M captive post?" into "is the pool a reinsurer?", which no individual captive can answer and a pool can.

**First 90 days.** Retain Bermuda and U.S. counsel for a written view on cells under the RJ test. Pre-consult a lead state and the BMA. Get term-sheet-level responses from two fronts (including Concert and PRP) on ceding to a pooled RJ reinsurer with no collateral. Model capital and the guarantee fee. Sign LOIs with 8 group captives and their sponsors.

**Outside-the-box score.** 8

## 4. [pattern] The 97% Book: buy the agencies and managers that sponsor group captives

**Pattern transferred.** "Buy the services firm, then rebuild its margin (the balance-sheet path)" plus "roll-up multiple arbitrage in recurring services" (digest). No AI is required; automation helps margin but is not the thesis.

**One-liner.** A permanent-capital holding company that acquires the agencies, consultants and independent managers that sponsor and run casualty and MSL group captives, the one corner of commercial insurance with 97-98% retention and member-owned capital accounts that make leaving expensive.

**Customer / payer and signer.** Sellers: founders of sponsor agencies, consultants and independent captive managers (revenue $8-30M each). Revenue payers: group-captive member boards that sign multi-year management and program agreements. Buyer signer: the platform's investment committee; each captive board must consent to assignment.

**Problem.** Group-captive sponsor and manager fees are hidden inside agencies and valued like ordinary commission revenue; owners age; continuity and key-person risk worry members. Big consolidators are buying captive *managers* (Howden bought ARM Group in 2024, Holmes Murphy bought Global Captive Management, Davies bought Citadel Risk, Aon bought Nordic Mutual) but their retail agencies conflict with captives.

**Solution.** Acquire, in sequence, (i) sponsor agencies with 3-8 groups, (ii) independent captive managers and MSL specialists, (iii) group-captive TPA units. Consolidate reinsurance and front purchasing, back office and reporting. Write a covenant that the owner never sells members commercial insurance. Cross-launch new groups through the combined sponsor network (feeds on idea 2).

**Why now.** Group premium is growing about 11% a year to $10B by 2030 on CR's projection; retention is 97-98%; strategics are paying for captive management, which sets comparables; PE capital is looking for recurring revenue.
*Why not here yet:* the assets are private, relationship-based and tied to a consultant (CR) whose cooperation is needed; consolidators that could buy them sell commercial insurance to the same clients.

**Wedge / first 10.** First deal: a $15-30M-revenue sponsor agency or consultancy with 5+ groups. Equity of about $100M for the platform; 8-12 deals over five years at $8-15M of revenue each.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).**
- Sponsor and manager fee pool of 7-9% of premium (assumption; CR members pay professional fees rather than commissions): $455-585M on $6.5B today, $700-900M on $10B in 2030.
- $100M of revenue is about 11-14% of the 2030 pool, via 8-12 acquisitions plus about 10% organic growth. Enterprise value is roughly $400M at 4x revenue; funding is $300-500M of equity and debt.
- It is a financial-engineering path more than a product path. It is credible, late-cycle, and dependent on key people staying.

**Who does it today.** Marsh, Aon, Gallagher/Artex and WTW run groups and managers; Howden/ARM, Holmes Murphy/GCM, Davies/Citadel are consolidating managers; CR is family-held (private). PE-backed brokers (Hub, USI, Acrisure and others, from memory) buy commission books. I found no platform buying group-captive *sponsors* as a category (searched once; "none found").

**Why they lose.** Boards will not approve an assignment to a buyer that sells their members commercial insurance, which rules out the large brokers; the covenant is the counter-position. Sponsors who want independence prefer a partner with permanent capital and no cross-selling.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Switching costs (members leave their equity behind for 4-5 years after exit); scale economies in reinsurance and front panels; brand with member boards.

**Earned secret.** Group-captive revenue is the stickiest in commercial insurance, yet it is valued as ordinary agency commission revenue. The arbitrage is the multiple and the retention, not technology.

**First 90 days.** Build a map of the 90+ casualty groups and 50+ MSL groups (sponsor, consultant, manager, premium, fee schedule). Call 20 sponsors; get three LOIs. Read three management agreements for assignment and key-person clauses. Line up a $50M anchor equity commitment and a debt partner.

**Outside-the-box score.** 4

## 5. [dilemma] Pareto TPA: paid on the 7% of claims that carry 40% of the cost

**One-liner.** A claims administrator for owner-managed casualty programs that puts its best adjusters, nurses and defence strategists on the top slice of claims by dollar exposure and is paid a share of verified savings against the captive actuary's pick.

**Customer.** Group-captive boards and sponsor agencies, large single-parent captives, and large-deductible or self-insured workers' comp and liability programs.

**Payer and signer.** Risk manager or captive board claims committee; the budget is the TPA/ALAE/ULAE line paid from the loss fund.

**Problem.** NCCI data show a tiny fraction of claims drives much of the cost: claims of $100K-$500K were 7.3% of claims and 41.3% of costs (study of 2004-06 services, dated), and claims above $1M were under 0.5% of lost-time claims but up to 15% of costs; fast-emerging large claims were 59% of $1M+ claims in 2023 versus 27% in 2003. TPAs staff by claim count and bill per file or hour, so the best people are not on the claims that matter.

**Solution.**
1. A top-decile desk that takes over a claim at triggers (reserve above $50K, attorney involvement, surgery, ICU, venue).
2. LLM file review for missing records, causation, litigation budgets and settlement valuation against an outcomes database built from the programs served; senior humans decide.
3. Fees: a small base fee on desk claims plus 15-25% of verified savings against the actuary's expected ultimate.
4. Run as the owner's representative alongside the current TPA, which keeps the long tail of small claims.

Legal premise to verify: state adjuster and TPA licensing and any rules on compensation tied to claim outcomes (structure as consultant to the captive, not adjuster of record); conflicts with the fronting carrier's claims-control clauses.

**Why now.** Models can read ten-year claim files. Nuclear-verdict severity makes the top slice matter more. Captive claims committees control claims by design (Rough Notes). CLARA Analytics launched an "agentic AI claims workforce" for complex casualty; Veltha (YC S26, tiny seed) is an AI-native TPA for workers' comp; both sell productivity to carriers and TPAs, not savings-share to owners.

**Wedge / first 10.** Five group captives (trucking, construction) with $30-100M of premium each hand over their open top-decile claims for a 12-month, fee-on-savings pilot.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).**
- Effective revenue of about 5.6% of claim dollars under management (4% base plus 20% of an assumed 8% saving).
- $100M needs about $1.8B of top-decile claim dollars under management. Assumptions: Marsh-managed captives' $79.1B of premium at a 55% loss ratio is about $43B of losses, half long-tail ($21.5B), top slice 45% (about $9.7B a year); group captives add about $1.75B (60% loss ratio on $6.5B, top slice 45%).
- That is about 15% of the captive casualty top slice, or 5-7% if large-deductible and self-insured programs (not sized here) are as large. **Heroic on captives alone.** Attribution of savings is a standing dispute risk.

**Who does it today.** Sedgwick, Gallagher Bassett, Crawford, CorVel, ESIS (Chubb), Optum (all per-claim or per-hour); AI claims vendors CLARA ($60M raised in total), Five Sigma ($24M), Agentech ($3M seed), Veltha. Litigation-management and claim-audit boutiques (from memory). None found selling savings-share on the top decile to owners (searched; "none found").

**Why they lose.** Per-claim fees and carrier customers who buy speed make outcome fees a pay cut for the TPA, and automating adjuster hours cuts its own billable base; a savings-share invites audits of its reserving. Fronts that bundle claims earn on volume.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Process power (playbooks per injury class and venue); a data asset (settlement outcomes across programs, which no captive has alone); switching costs on open claims.

**Earned secret.** Claims are staffed by count and cost is by dollars. About 7% of claims make about 41% of the cost and the fee schedule pays the same for each file, so nobody puts the best adjuster on the 7%.

**First 90 days.** Pilot with one captive manager and two groups: review 200 open claims; fix the savings baseline as the actuary's inception pick; build the outcomes database; get a licensing opinion.

**Outside-the-box score.** 4 (borderline cliché "AI claims"; the difference is the owner as customer, shared-savings pricing and senior people on the top slice)

## 6. [dilemma] Retained-Risk Bureau: the NCCI of captives, self-insureds and large deductibles

**One-liner.** A member-governed data cooperative that pools loss triangles, claim outcomes and safety data from captives, TPAs and self-insured programs and sells development patterns and proof of selection to reinsurers, carriers and MGAs.

**Customer.** Buyers: reinsurers, excess carriers, MGAs, fronts. Contributors and subscribers: captive managers, TPAs, sponsor agencies, captive boards.

**Payer and signer.** Head of casualty reinsurance underwriting or chief actuary (external data and analytics budget); MGA founders; sponsors' CEOs for member-facing tiers.

**Problem.** ISO and NCCI collect statistical data on insured business; the retained layers (captives, self-insured, large deductible) are invisible to everyone. Advocates say captives outperform, but I found no rigorous independent study: figures such as "15-30% lower premiums" come from brokers and captive managers, and AM Best's recent view is that captive results came under pressure in workers' comp and RRG commercial auto. Excess reinsurers therefore price off market loss costs plus an uncertainty load, and captives have no neutral benchmark for collateral release or treaty renewals.

**Solution.**
1. A give-to-get data charter with anonymisation, run by a neutral operator; ingestion by LLM extraction from loss runs and bordereaux.
2. Products: captive loss-development factors by line, class and size (for collateral and reserve talks); a selection-effect index (how group-captive members perform against insured-market expectation); admission scores for sponsors; TPA benchmarks; API access for reinsurers and MGAs.
3. Over time, an advisory loss-cost product that reinsurers cite in treaties.

Legal premise to verify: antitrust (information exchange among competitors; use a neutral aggregator with safeguards), ownership of data as between captive, front and TPA, HIPAA for MSL data (non-PHI only), McCarran-Ferguson treatment of statistical agents.

**Why now.** Group-captive premium of $6.5B and third-party captives of $4.4B (Marsh) produce enough data; Conning finds fronting programs' early loss ratios have developed adversely seven years running, so reported data are under suspicion; reinsurers are pushing fronts to retain more; and the data bureaus' pricing power is visible (Verisk's underwriting segment reported $569M of Q2 2026 revenue, per a secondary source).

**Wedge / first 10.** One segment where data are richest: trucking auto liability and workers' comp in group captives. Ten groups contribute; deliver a selection-effect report to two reinsurers.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** Mature revenue (assumptions): 40 reinsurers at $300K-$1.5M ($30M); 150 excess carriers and MGAs at $100-400K ($30M); 2,500 captives, sponsors and managers at $15-40K ($60M); 20 fronts at $250K ($5M): about $125M. $100M would be roughly 4% of the underwriting-analytics spend that Verisk's segment implies (about $2.3B a year, secondary source). **Adoption assumptions are heroic;** contributions and trust take years.

**Who does it today.** Verisk/ISO and NCCI (insured-market statistics); AM Best captive composites (dated); Marsh, Aon, Gallagher/Artex benchmarking (client-only); Milliman, Pinnacle and other actuaries (bespoke); NAIC annual-statement data for RRGs (free); Luzern and Huscarl will accumulate data as proprietary advantage. No neutral cross-captive bureau found (searched; "none found").

**Why they lose.** ISO and NCCI answer to carriers and receive data from insurers, not captive owners, and carriers gain from opacity about the retained world. Brokers' benchmarks are sales tools tied to their own clients. Managers hold data but will not hand it to competitors.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Network economies (more contributors, better factors, more value); a cornered resource (contractual data rights); switching costs once embedded in collateral talks and treaty pricing.

**Earned secret.** Everyone says captives outperform and nobody can prove it, because the data live in 8,000 sets of spreadsheets and front bordereaux. Whoever can prove it, or disprove it, sets the price of every excess layer and every collateral-release schedule.

**First 90 days.** Draft the charter and antitrust review. Get five managers or TPAs to share anonymised triangles for 50 programs. Compute a first selection-effect result for trucking. Show it to two reinsurers and ask for a price.

**Outside-the-box score.** 8

## 7. [shock] Broker Mutual: a member-owned liability RRG that underwrites every freight load

**One-liner.** A risk retention group owned by mid-size freight brokers and 3PLs that insures negligent-selection claims after Montgomery and prices each tendered load on the carrier, truck and driver behind it.

**Customer.** U.S. freight brokers and 3PLs ranked roughly 40th to 1,000th by gross revenue ($50M to $2B), plus asset-light agent-network brokerages. Not the top 25 (C.H. Robinson carries $135M of auto liability limits above a $10M per-claim deductible).

**Payer and signer.** The broker's CFO or owner. Budget: contingent auto liability, third-party liability and E&O. TIA's president puts a brokerage's total insurance at 1-3% of revenue and says the figure he hears most often is a 5x increase.

**Problem.** On 2026-05-14 the Supreme Court unanimously held that negligent-selection claims against brokers are not preempted. The only federal broker financial requirement is a $75,000 surety bond. TD Cowen reports about 10 underwriters in broker auto liability, expected to fall to eight (two exits already); a top-10 broker's liability premium roughly tripled; a large broker's excess above a $15M primary went from $3M to $10M; few brokers carry above $100M outside the largest; the Lipe verdict against C.H. Robinson was reported at $600M in late July. About 22,000-28,000 brokers exist; the top 100 hold 84% of the market. The federal carrier minimum is still $750,000 (since 1980) against a median trucking nuclear verdict of $36M (ATRI, as reported).

**Solution.**
1. A Vermont-domiciled RRG: under the Liability Risk Retention Act an RRG with one state licence can write liability (not workers' comp) in all states for owner-insureds engaged in similar business. Behind it, an A-rated reinsurance tower with excess for large losses.
2. Underwriting per load: the member's TMS sends each tender to the RRG's API; the RRG scores the carrier (authority age, safety rating and inspection history, crash history, live insurance verification, identity and double-brokering flags) and, where available, truck and driver data; premium is a base charge plus a per-load charge; high-risk loads are priced up or blocked.
3. An immutable selection file for every load, which is the evidence of standard of care in discovery.
4. Dividends in good years; GL, E&O and cargo fronted through a partner.
5. Year 3+: shared balance sheet with idea 3 for an excess mutual for the top 100 and carriers.

Legal premise to verify: that contingent auto liability is "liability" under 15 U.S.C. 3901 for RRG purposes; member-ownership and similar-exposure tests; state registrations (panelists at Vermont's 2026 conference described multistate delays); Vermont Act 77 (effective 2026-07-01) restrictions on RRG loans or investments to members; shipper contracts that demand an A-rated insurer, which a new RRG cannot meet at launch (so launch behind a fronted or rated layer).

**Why now.** The ruling is five months old. The FMCSA broker financial responsibility rule took full effect on 2026-01-16. Underwriters expect to price carrier selection as an exposure and to ask for vetting records (Higginbotham, Lockton, FreightWaves). Reinsurer appetite for this class has fallen (TD Cowen). A Landstar-style proof exists: Landstar's insurance segment earned about $26M of revenue and $10M of operating income in Q1 2025 (about $15M external) running a captive for its independent trucks.

**Wedge / first 10.** Twelve to fifteen founding brokers ($100M-$800M gross revenue) with documented vetting who have been quoted 2-3x; $15-25M of initial capital pooled from members plus a surplus-note investor; endorsement through TIA; first-year premium $20-30M.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).**
- Tier of about 900 brokers outside the top 100 with $50M+ of gross revenue (assumption). Average member: $300M gross revenue at about 13% net revenue is $39M of net revenue; TIA's 1-3% of revenue implies $0.4-1.2M of insurance; after repricing, about $0.9M for liability (assumption).
- 110 members at $0.9M is about $100M of premium: about 12% of that tier's roughly $800M pool.
- **Revenue here is insurance premium. Fee-only revenue (manager, program and tech at 12-15%) would be $12-15M.** Year 5+ option: carriers on the approved list buy primary auto liability through the RRG, adding $60M at 2,000 carriers and $30K each (assumption).

**Who does it today.** Marsh BrokerSafe (Aug 2025); Falvey (Accelerant paper, $1M/$2M primaries); TIA-endorsed Avalon program; Reliance Partners and TCB Insurance Programs (non-admitted); Higginbotham transportation division; USA Risk Group reportedly seeking a Delaware transportation RRG (undated source); Aon's Small Fleet Advantage RRG (directory listing only). Big brokers self-insure (C.H. Robinson, Landstar's captive). Carrier-vetting software (Highway, MyCarrierPackets, Carrier Assure, from memory) feeds underwriting but is not a risk-bearer. No broker-owned RRG found (searched; "none found").

**Why they lose.** Marsh's facility is placed capacity: Marsh does not own the underwriting result, cannot hold members to a tendering standard, and sees only annual submissions. The shrinking pool of ~10 underwriters prices on revenue and loss history, not per-load selection. Members who own the dividend have a reason to vet better.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Counter-positioning against annual-submission underwriting; switching costs (TMS integration plus the evidence file plus member equity); a cornered resource (cross-broker carrier-selection data); later network effects as the RRG's carrier list becomes the market's approved list.

**Earned secret.** After Montgomery, a claim is decided in discovery by one question: what did you know about this carrier when you tendered the load? Underwriters and plaintiffs ask it from the same eight data points. Whoever holds the time-stamped selection file can price the risk and defend it, so the record is the product, and it can only be built inside the TMS.

**First 90 days.** Get LOIs from TIA and 12 brokers. Retain counsel for an LRRA and Vermont opinion and a Vermont RRG manager. Collect loss runs and TMS exports. Build the load-scoring model from FMCSA bulk data and test it on historic loads with known claims. Get soft quotes from Lloyd's and Bermuda reinsurers. File the business plan and a $15-25M capital term sheet.

**Outside-the-box score.** 7

## 8. [shock] The TPA Captive: stop-loss captives sponsored by the people who see the claims first

**One-liner.** Give independent third-party administrators their own medical stop-loss group captive in a box, so employers with 50-1,500 lives stop buying lasers and annual repricing from carriers who see the same claims six to twelve months later.

**Customer.** Independent TPAs (about 4,300 once; now hundreds after consolidation, per a Cognizant/TriZetto piece), each administering 50-500 self-funded employers; and those employers. KFF 2025 (as reported): 67% of covered workers are in self-funded plans, 27% at small firms.

**Payer and signer.** Employer CFO or head of HR (benefits budget; stop-loss premiums are up 12.7% on average for 2026 in Segal's 225-plan data); the TPA's CEO co-signs (retention and differentiation against carrier ASO bundles).

**Problem.** Segal 2026 data (via actuary.info): stop-loss premiums up 12.7%; $1M+ claimants grew about 25% a year for four plan years (2.44x since 2022); carriers laser beyond oncology and transplant and are adding gene-therapy exclusions; median medical trend of 9%; GLP-1 allowed PMPM for covering employers rose from $2.41 to $22.59 between 2022 and 2024; employers raise specific deductibles from $150K to $250K or more, which pushes variance into the aggregate layer. Stop-loss premium was $35.5B in 2023 (Oliver Wyman). CR says about 10,000 MSL members are served out of about 200,000 potential, and calls MSL "a real target."

**Solution.**
1. A platform that lets each TPA sponsor a cell or its own group captive: front, reinsurance panel, captive manager, actuary, collateral trust, investments and employer onboarding.
2. The TPA's live claims feed (prior authorisations, case management flags, large-claim alerts, Rx) drives underwriting continuously, enabling "no new lasers", multi-year rate corridors and a pooled catastrophic layer.
3. The employer retains the first $100-250K per person; the captive pools the next layer; reinsurers take above $1M.
4. TPAs earn admin fees plus a sponsor share of 3-5% of premium; the platform earns about 6.5% (captive management about 2%, reinsurance override about 2%, underwriting and tech about 2%, other 0.5%; assumption).

Legal premise to verify: state stop-loss rules for small groups (minimum attachment points), ERISA and MEWA status, and tax treatment of captive-funded stop-loss (one source notes the IRS has not definitively addressed it). Target employers of 50+ lives.

**Why now.** The structural break above; carriers adding gene-therapy exclusions; employers dropping GLP-1 coverage (6% of 500+ employers for 2026), while Lilly Employer Connect (launched 2026-03-05, Zepbound KwikPen at $449 per dose as reported) and Novo's Waltz Health and 9amHealth programs (Jan 2026) open direct-to-employer drug channels that TPAs can plug into.

**Wedge / first 10.** Ten independent TPAs with 100+ groups each and renewal pain; start with groups of 100-1,000 lives and a two-year rate corridor; first 100 employers at about $400K of stop-loss premium is $40M.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).**
- About $1.5B of stop-loss premium in TPA-sponsored captives at 6.5% is about $98M: 80 TPAs placing 40 groups each at $470K.
- That is about 3,200 employers (1.6% of CR's 200,000 prospects, a third of CR's 10,000 served) and about 3% of an estimated $45B stop-loss market in 2026 (my extrapolation from $35.5B in 2023).

**Who does it today.** Captive Resources (50+ groups, MSL a stated target), Strategic Risk Solutions' MSL Captive Solutions, RMC (fronting and reinsurance facility for captive managers and MGAs), Sun Life, QBE and Skyward Specialty's AH Captive Solutions (carrier-run group stop-loss programmes), Hylant GCS, Roundstone, Zenith Risk Strategies (small MGU), Yuzu Health (General Catalyst-backed TPA infrastructure for small employers, per a newsletter headline; unverified), Aradigm (gene-therapy pooling, May 2026 Quantum Health deal). Carrier-affiliated TPAs (Meritain/Aetna, Cigna, UnitedHealthcare) hold large shares. None found giving independent TPAs a captive platform (searched; "none found").

**Why they lose.** Stop-loss carriers see claims through TPA submissions with a lag, reprice annually and laser: that is their profit lever, and they sell ASO bundles that compete with independent TPAs. Benefit brokers earn commissions on stop-loss and cannot recommend a captive that removes them. CR's model is built around agency and consultant sponsors, not around the party that holds the claims feed.

**Moat (7 Powers).** A cornered resource (live claims data from many TPAs); switching costs (employer equity accounts in the captive, 97-98% retention in CR's groups); scale economies in reinsurance.

**Earned secret.** The stop-loss carrier is the last person in the room to learn about a $1M claimant; the TPA knew at prior authorisation, months earlier. Adverse selection is a data-latency tax the employer pays. Whoever owns the claim feed can underwrite without it.

**First 90 days.** Recruit five independent TPAs through their associations and sign data-sharing agreements. Back-test three years of claims on 20 groups against actual stop-loss premiums and lasers. Choose a domicile with MSL experience and sign a front and a reinsurer for the $1M+ layer. Get an ERISA, state stop-loss and tax opinion.

**Outside-the-box score.** 5

## Honest ranking

**Highest ceiling if the legal premise holds:** 3 (Zero-Collateral Cell Reinsurer) and 6 (Retained-Risk Bureau). Both change a rule everyone else treats as fixed (collateral; who owns retained-risk data). Both are heroic on adoption and both can be killed in the first 90 days by a legal or data-sharing answer.

**Best evidence and timing:** 7 (Broker Mutual) and 8 (The TPA Captive). Both have dated catalysts and hard numbers. Idea 7's path to $100M is an insurer-revenue path (premium), not a fee path; idea 8 faces strong captive-consultant competitors.

**Most financeable with the least invention:** 4 (The 97% Book) and 1 (Clean Exit). The first is a capital-markets business; the second needs sponsors and tax and fund-document opinions.

**Weakest:** 5 (Pareto TPA), because "AI claims" is close to a cliché, outcome fees may collide with claims-practice rules, and the share needed on captives alone is heroic. 2 (The 12th Member) is sound but needs the market to expand to reach $100M.

Synergies: 3 and 7 can share a balance sheet (7's excess mutual would be a cell); 2 and 4 feed each other (sponsors acquired by 4 are 2's supply of prospects); 6 would give 3 its collateral-release evidence; 8's MSL cells fit inside 3.
