# P022 - US consumer auto-loan interest and fees, with the subprime servicing, collections and repossession layer under stress (round 2 ideas)

Written 2026-10-18. Eight ideas, two per lens. Every number is tagged: (S) = sourced, (A) = my arithmetic or assumption, (U) = unverified (from memory, a vendor claim, or a trade-press relay of primary data I could not open).

Method note: about 29 web searches and 6 page fetches. The pool is "contested", and every idea below has a named incumbent or near-incumbent. Ideas 4 to 8 (captive, rail, servicer, registry, exchange) can be assembled into one "trusted pipe" stack. They are separated because they have different payers and mechanisms and should be judged separately. Four of the eight need a share of their sub-pool that I call heroic (over 20%) in the text; I say so rather than shrink them.

## INSIDER lens

### 1. Last Mile: a locate-first, net-recovery operator for the default chain

**One-liner.** A forwarder paid on dollars recovered per defaulted dollar instead of per truck roll: it finds the car before the file is assigned, offers cooperating borrowers a scheduled hand-back, and routes each recovered unit to its best sale channel.

**Customer.** Non-captive lenders with 8k-30k repossessions a year: independent subprime lenders, credit unions with $1B+ auto books, regional banks, private-credit-owned portfolios.

**Payer and signer.** Lender's SVP Collections/Recovery signs; the CFO co-signs. Paid from the existing recovery line (forwarder fees, transport, storage, auction and title costs). It re-bundles spend that already exists.

**Insider.** A 20-year repo forwarder and remarketing manager.

**Earned secret.** Lenders think repossession is a muscle business. It is a timing and information business. In 2025 RDN saw about 2.4M assignments in a quarter against a 29% recovery ratio, so most assignments do not end in a car on a truck (some are cures, the rest are cars that moved, hid, were sold, or sit behind a bankruptcy stay). The lender never sees the failure rate because it pays only on success ($750-900 billed, $325-375 to the agent; dated), so the forwarder prices failure into the success fee. Every link is paid per activity: forwarder per recovery, agent per grab, yard per day, auction per sale, collection agency per deficiency dollar. Nobody is paid on net proceeds per defaulted dollar, and the file is usually assigned at day 60-90, after the car has moved. A cooperating borrower who hands the car back on a scheduled day costs a fraction of a field repossession and the car arrives cleaner (A).

**Problem.** Non-prime ABS recoveries averaged 38.2% in 2024 (S), the lowest since the financial crisis according to KBRA's framing. Repossessions are at their highest since 2009-2010 on some measures. The default chain leaks at every handoff and no one owns the net number.

**Solution.** (1) At day 30-45 delinquent, pre-locate the vehicle and verify lien, insurance and bankruptcy flags. (2) Offer cooperating borrowers a scheduled voluntary surrender with a defined deficiency and credit-reporting outcome. (3) Dispatch agents only to located units at a fixed price plus a first-attempt bonus. (4) Route each recovered unit by condition, mileage and title status to digital wholesale, dealer direct, export or parts. (5) Decide the deficiency path (pursue, sell, release) by state law and borrower profile. Price: a base fee below the lender's current forwarding bill plus 20-30% of net recovery above a baseline back-tested on the lender's own prior vintages.

**Why now.** Repossession volume is at multi-year highs (Cox ~1.8M expected in 2025; RDN ~2.95M annualized), non-prime recoveries are weak, ABS investors and private credit now ask for recovery reporting, and digital remarketing channels exist to plug into. Lenders under cost pressure will trade a fee cut for a recovery lift.

**Wedge and first 10 customers.** One lender, a 90-day randomized pilot on 1,000 defaulted accounts (treatment: pre-locate plus managed hand-back; control: current process). First ten: credit unions with $1-5B auto books (they lack forwarding scale and are member-sensitive) and independent subprime lenders.

**Path to $100M (A).** Lender pays today about $1,450 all-in per recovered unit (forwarding ~$825 plus transport ~$250, auction/seller fees ~$300 and title ~$75). We bill about $1,000 for the services we control (base $700, remarketing coordination $150, lift share $150 on average), pay agents about $350, and net about $650 per unit. $100M of net revenue is about 154k units a year; $100M gross is about 100k units. That is 5-9% of annual repossessions (net basis; 1.8M Cox-type count to 2.95M RDN-type count), or 8-12 mid-size lenders at 12-18k repossessions each. Lender test: +3 points of recovery on a $17k average defaulted balance is about $510 per unit, and our lift share is under a third of it. Share needed is high but not above 20%.

**Who does it today.** Primeritus (forwarding, skip tracing, remarketing; formed 2012 by Kinderhook; sold its direct recovery business to Connect 1 Recovery; 2026 status unverified), the RDN assignment network, MBSi (remarketing and VINchex), OPENLANE and Manheim digital repo programs, Servicing Solutions, regional forwarders, and in-house recovery at the largest lenders. Motorola's DRN plate-read data is the likely locate source (U).

**Why they lose.** Forwarders earn per assignment and per success, so they gain from volume and cannot afford to underwrite outcomes; the RDN network is a pipe, not an operator; auctions earn on lane sales and fees, not on net proceeds; inside a lender, collections, recovery, remarketing and legal are separate cost centers with separate vendors.

**Moat.** Process power (per-unit outcome data becomes a recovery-pricing engine), counter-positioning against activity-priced forwarders, and scale economies in dispatch density.

**Legal check.** State repossession-agency licensing; UCC 9-609 breach of peace; FDCPA 1692f(6) on wrongful dispossession; DPPA permissible use for plate data (U); "voluntary" surrender must be genuinely voluntary.

**First 90 days.** Pull the public RDN/Cox series; hire a former forwarder operations head; contract 50 agencies with a standard compliance pack; sign one mid-size lender for the 1,000-account pilot; measure recovery ratio, days to sale and net proceeds against control.

**Outside-the-box: 4/10.** Operationally sharp, conceptually incremental.

### 2. Downshift: a lender-approved short-payoff and trade-down exchange

**One-liner.** Before the repossession, a delinquent borrower is matched with a dealer that buys the car near retail value and puts them in a cheaper one, with the lender pre-approving the short payoff.

**Customer.** Lenders' loss-mitigation teams (subprime, credit unions, captives' used books) and independent/BHPH dealers who want inventory and customers.

**Payer and signer.** Lender SVP Collections/Loss Mitigation pays a per-completed-case fee out of recovery and charge-off budgets; the dealer pays a car-acquisition fee. Lender's CRO or Head of Servicing signs the short-payoff policy.

**Insider.** A buy-here-pay-here dealer principal, 20 years.

**Earned secret.** "The best loss mitigation is selling the customer a cheaper car, not taking back the one they cannot afford. I buy their car, put them in a $9k car at $60 a week and keep them current. A bank can't, because the loan is upside down, it has no way to accept a short payoff, it has no replacement inventory and it has no retail channel." The pool numbers fit: about 31% of new-car trade-ins are underwater (Edmunds via a vendor; pool file), BHPH loans are in active repossession at 16.63 times the rate of traditional lenders (S: Fed note) because BHPH dealers repossess and resell constantly, and rolling negative equity into a new loan is reported to more than double the odds of repossession within two years (secondary report of a CFPB finding; U). Auto "short sale" exists only as an informal, discretionary request; I found no lender-integrated marketplace.

**Problem.** A repossession destroys value: non-prime recovery averages 38.2% (S), the borrower loses transport and income stability, and the lender books a deficiency it rarely collects.

**Solution.** A standard short-payoff product: the lender pre-authorizes a window (accept up to X% of appraised value as payoff in full or partial, with the residual released or sold as an unsecured balance). The platform matches the borrower to a partner dealer that appraises, buys the car, pays off the lien, and sells a lower-payment vehicle financed by the dealer's own lender. The platform handles lien payoff, paperwork, credit-reporting codes, Form 1099-C where debt is forgiven (U), and a compliance audit trail.

**Why now.** Record serious delinquency, record negative equity, 35.55% of new loans longer than six years (pool file), a used market firm enough that cars hold value, and private-credit owners of loans who want fewer deficiencies.

**Wedge and first 10 customers.** Two lenders with in-house repo programs plus five BHPH/independent dealers in one metro; run 200 swaps against matched repossession cohorts.

**Path to $100M (A).** Balance $18k; repossession at the 38.2% recovery rate returns $6.9k, less about $1.45k of costs, so about $5.4k net. Short payoff: the dealer pays about $8.0k (it can retail the car), the lender pays a $700 fee, so about $7.3k net: a gain of about $1.9k per case. Platform revenue per case is $700 from the lender plus $300 from the dealer = $1,000. $100M needs 100k completed cases a year, 3.4-5.6% of annual repossessions (1.8M-2.95M), or about 11% of the roughly 0.9M loans 60+ days delinquent (A: 6.9% x 13M). Share needed is under 20%, but the volume is hard to source.

**Who does it today.** Informal BHPH dealers; "we buy any car" retailers (Carvana, CarMax) that pay off liens but make the borrower cover negative equity; lender hardship teams (extensions, deferrals, modifications); no packaged marketplace found. I did not search lender policy documents, so this is "not found", not "does not exist".

**Why they lose.** Lender loss-mitigation systems are built for modifications and extensions, not asset sales; the big used-car buyers have no reason to absorb a lender's loss; ABS servicing agreements limit modifications, and a short payoff may need servicer or trustee consent (U), which is exactly the standardization a platform sells.

**Moat.** Network economies (lender pre-authorizations on one side, dealer inventory and appetite on the other) and switching costs once the lender's policy engine integrates.

**Legal check.** Whether ABS servicing standards permit short payoffs without trustee sign-off (U); state deficiency and notice law; 1099-C; UDAAP and fair-lending risk on the replacement loan; adverse selection by dealers; dealer licensing.

**First 90 days.** Get one lender's actual repossession-versus-swap economics from its own data; line up five dealers; draft the short-payoff policy with the lender's counsel; run the 200-case pilot.

**Outside-the-box: 7/10.** A mortgage short-sale analogue that auto finance lacks.

## PATTERN lens

### 3. Policy Escrow: the car's insurance inside the car payment

**One-liner.** Mortgage lenders solved uninsured collateral with escrow; auto lenders never did. A licensed, carrier-neutral agency collects the premium with the loan payment, keeps coverage continuous, and feeds policy status to the lender.

**Pattern transferred.** "Control the repayment channel": the lender owns the one recurring payment the borrower protects, so riders on that payment (insurance, registration) get paid first. Mortgage escrow is the proven analogue.

**Customer.** Non-prime auto lenders, credit unions and BHPH dealers, plus non-standard carriers that want low-lapse distribution.

**Payer and signer.** Carriers pay commission (10-15%; U). The lender pays nothing and gets lower collateral risk, a policy-status data feed and a replacement for clunky tracking/force-placement; its Head of Collateral or CRO signs the integration.

**Earned secret.** Told from a subprime collections manager's seat (the causal claim is mine to test, U): the insurance cancels first, then the car payment bounces, and the lender learns the car was totalled and uninsured only when the borrower calls. Subprime portfolios run 35-40% uninsured against 10-15% for prime (S, dated vendor data). Lender-placed coverage is expensive and narrow, and the vendors that sell it are paid more when borrowers lapse.

**Problem.** The two biggest bills of a subprime borrower, the payment and the premium, compete; the one that breaks first takes the other with it.

**Solution.** Optional, multi-carrier policy escrow: at origination or at the first 30-day miss, the borrower can fold a quoted non-standard policy into one monthly draft. The agency pays the carrier, auto-reinstates on late premium by drawing the next payment, and gives the lender real-time coverage status. Premium financing and the payment draft run on state-compliant rails.

**Why now.** Insurance costs are surging, subprime delinquency is at a record, and embedded insurance is moving: Deloitte forecasts up to a fifth of personal auto insurance embedded in vehicle sales by 2030, with Root-Carvana cited as an example (S).

**Wedge and first 10 customers.** One credit union or BHPH group with its own force-placement data; start with the 30-day-delinquent "insurance-first" outreach, then move to origination.

**Path to $100M (A).** About 13M non-prime loans x ~$3,000 premium (U) = ~$39B of premiums; at 12% commission (U) the pool is ~$4.7B. $100M of commission is ~$830M of premium, ~277k policies, about 2% of non-prime loans. Three to five lenders with 100k+ loans each, or a few hundred BHPH/credit-union partners.

**Who does it today.** Gaya (tiny: $50K raised; sells through insurance agents), Root-Carvana embedded, Ford Credit's commercial program, lender-placed insurers (Assurant, Allstate's National General Lender Services; U), insurance-tracking vendors (Verifacto and others), and non-standard carriers selling through agents or direct.

**Why they lose.** Carriers cannot sell into the lender channel without a fight with their own agents; force-placement vendors profit from lapses; lenders cannot sell insurance, cannot require a particular insurer, and have no licensed agency.

**Moat.** Switching costs (the premium lives in the payment stream), counter-positioning, and a cornered resource in lender integrations plus the policy data feed.

**Legal check.** Agency licensing in every state; premium-finance and tying rules; premium trust accounting; whether the combined draft changes the Reg Z finance-charge picture (U); optional and multi-carrier by design.

**First 90 days.** Get anonymized force-placement and lapse-to-delinquency timing from one lender; test the "insurance lapses before the payment" claim; partner with an existing agency/MGA instead of getting licensed; pilot with 5,000 borrowers.

**Outside-the-box: 6/10.**

### 4. Driveway in a Box: a captive finance arm for mid-size dealer groups

**One-liner.** Lithia and AutoNation proved a dealer group can capture the finance spread it gives away to banks; build the rest of the top 150 groups their own captive (licensing, underwriting, servicing, funding) as a service.

**Pattern transferred.** "Concentrated-operator vertical platform": a few dozen operators control many locations and repeat the same function.

**Customer.** Dealer groups with 10k-60k retail units a year.

**Payer and signer.** Group CFO/president signs; paid from the group's finance profit pool (reserve and F&I it already earns, plus spread it newly keeps).

**Evidence.** Lithia's Driveway Finance Corporation: $840M originations in Q1 2026, 18.0% penetration, average FICO 750, $5.0B average managed receivables, finance operations income $21.3M (+70.4%) and interest margin $58.9M in the quarter (S, primary). AutoNation Finance: 11.1% penetration (up from 10.0%), a portfolio reported at $2.67B, +52% (U). Penetration and profit are both rising.

**Earned secret.** A captive is not a credit-skill business; it is a cost-of-capital, customer-data and servicing business. All three can be rented. Dealers already run producer-owned reinsurance companies on F&I insurance (U), so the structure is familiar. And the group keeps the customer, the repossessed car and the resale (BHPH-style loss recovery) that a distant lender loses.

**Problem.** A group sends each financed customer to a bank for a reserve of roughly 1-2 points of rate (U) and loses the 4.7% annualized interest margin (A from DFC's $58.9M quarterly margin on $5.0B).

**Solution.** Multi-tenant captive stack: sales-finance licensing, decisioning, funding (pooled warehouse and ABS shelf plus forward-flow from private credit), servicing (via idea 6), collections, compliance. Dealer group keeps 60-70% of the spread and a first-loss slice for alignment; the platform charges a servicing/platform fee plus a share of finance operations income.

**Why now.** Proof from two public groups, abundant private-credit capital (TPG-OneMain forward flow), record penetration, and credit-box tightening by banks.

**Wedge and first 10 customers.** Three to five 20-60 store groups; model their actual funded deals from the DMS.

**Path to $100M (A).** DFC earns finance operations income of $85M a year (1.7% of $5.0B). Platform take: 0.70% of managed receivables plus 20% of finance operations income (0.34%) = ~1.0%. $100M needs ~$10B of managed receivables, about two DFCs. A 25k-unit group at 15% penetration and $30k average originates $112M a year and carries ~$280M at a 2.5-year life, so ~35 such groups. Against a universe I could not source (U; about 150 groups with 10k+ units a year), that is roughly 20-25%: heroic, and the weakest part of the idea. It works if the first customers are larger groups.

**Who does it today.** In-house captives (Lithia DFC, AutoNation Finance, CarMax Auto Finance, Carvana, DriveTime/Bridgecrest); DMS and origination vendors (Dealertrack, RouteOne, CDK, Reynolds) that route to lenders; servicing software (Fiserv, Dealertrack Servicing; U).

**Why they lose.** RouteOne (captive-owned; U) and Dealertrack (Cox) earn when dealers send deals to lenders and cannot help dealers disintermediate their own customers; banks pay reserve to keep dealers in the agency role; DMS vendors avoid credit and licensing.

**Moat.** Scale economies in pooled funding and servicing, switching costs (licenses, systems, portfolios), counter-positioning.

**Legal check.** State sales-finance licensing; ECOA/FCRA adverse action; reserve disclosure; ABS risk retention; capital and funding covenants; Lithia-style dealers may license their own platform (U).

**First 90 days.** Pick three groups; run the P&L capture model on their last 12 months of funded deals; get a forward-flow term sheet; hire ex-DFC/ANF/CarMax leaders.

**Outside-the-box: 6/10.**

## DILEMMA lens

### 5. Payday Rail: lender-paid, payday-aligned payments with portable mandates

**One-liner.** A payment layer that asks for the car payment the day the paycheck lands (request-for-payment or pay-by-bank), retries around payday, carries no borrower fee, and keeps the payment mandate portable if the servicer changes.

**Customer.** Non-prime lenders, credit unions and captives.

**Payer and signer.** Lender's Head of Payments/Servicing Operations pays per payment processed plus a share of failed-payment recovery; General Counsel is the champion because of pay-to-pay litigation.

**Earned secret.** From a collections manager: delinquency here is partly a calendar problem. Bridgecrest's own prospectus says its delinquencies vary by day of week because many borrowers pay bi-weekly (S). Lenders fix the due date and retry on a schedule that ignores payday, Nacha limits re-presentments to two within 180 days (U), and each bounce triggers a call, a fee and a worse next month. Predicting the debit date that clears matters more than the credit score. The tell is a spike mid-week in delinquency tapes.

**Problem.** Missed or bounced debits and pay-to-pay fees hurt both sides. Pay-to-pay fees are now in court: the Eleventh Circuit affirmed liability against Ocwen over Speedpay fees (Feb 2025), courts are split, the CFPB withdrew its position in May 2025, and NACA sued GM Financial in May 2026 (S).

**Solution.** Pay-by-bank/RTP/FedNow request-for-payment to the borrower on or just after a payroll-derived payday, with smart retry, account validation, card account-updater, a fee-free default path and a hardship hook. Mandates sit in a neutral layer so a servicer switch does not stop autopay. Revenue: a lender-paid fee per payment plus a percentage of recovered failed payments plus float on funds in transit.

**Why now.** Request-for-payment is live on FedNow and RTP (S, secondary: a Digital Transactions article; participation counts unverified); pay-by-bank is growing; pay-to-pay litigation is moving to state consumer-protection law; Tricolor showed what a payment-collection failure looks like.

**Wedge and first 10 customers.** One mid-size lender; shadow-test payday-aligned presentment for 10,000 borrowers using payroll connections (Pinwheel/Plaid-type).

**Path to $100M (A).** About 1.0B scheduled payments a year (85M loans x 12) plus more for weekly/bi-weekly borrowers. At $0.65 lender-paid per payment, $100M needs ~154M payments, about 15% of all scheduled payments. Add recovery fees: 60M non-prime payments with 12% failing x $550 = ~$4B failed; improving 7-day cure by 20 points pulls ~$0.8B forward; a 3% fee is ~$24M. So the path is roughly 9-15% of payment volume. Fee compression is the main risk because lenders pay ~$0.20-0.30 per ACH today (U).

**Who does it today.** ACI Speedpay, Paymentus (markets an auto-finance product), PayNearMe, Fiserv, Jack Henry, servicers' own portals, pay-by-bank providers such as Trustly and Plaid (U).

**Why they lose.** Speedpay-style economics rest on fees borrowers pay and that lenders often share (U); removing them removes revenue and the litigation exposure is borne by the lender, not the processor. Legacy servicing platforms fix due dates and retries by schedule, not by payday.

**Moat.** Switching costs (mandates and integrations), data (a payday-cadence model per borrower), counter-positioning against fee-funded processors.

**Legal check.** Nacha and Regulation E authorization for recurring and RfP debits; TCPA for payday prompts; state payment-fee laws; UDAAP if debits are timed to cause overdraft. RfP requires borrower approval.

**First 90 days.** Run the shadow test; sign a credit union and one subprime lender to a pay-by-bank pilot; get counsel's opinion on pay-to-pay exposure to use as the sales hook.

**Outside-the-box: 4/10.**

### 6. Independent Servicer of Record ("Cenlar for auto")

**One-liner.** A bank-grade, multi-tenant subservicer for non-bank originators, dealer-group captives and private-credit-owned loans, so the backup servicer is already the servicer when an originator fails.

**Customer.** Dealer-group captives (idea 4), fintech originators, private-credit funds and forward-flow buyers, small lenders that want to stop running servicing.

**Payer and signer.** The originator's COO/CFO or the fund's Head of Asset Finance, from the servicing and operations budget; investors and warehouse banks increasingly require it as a covenant.

**Earned secret.** Told from an ABS investor's chair: after Tricolor, deals ask for a "hot" backup servicer. A backup is a name on a page. Vervent was designated and still needed 8 business days to operate inside Tricolor's legacy system and 14 to board the loans (vendor claims; S), and a backup servicer does nothing about falsified data. What works is a servicer that already holds a mirror of the data and the borrower relationship every day.

**Problem.** Originator-servicers cannot be independent of themselves. Servicing fees (in non-prime ABS often several percent of balance; U) are the originator's profit and its control over collections.

**Solution.** A multi-tenant servicing platform and licensed operations: payments, collections, hardship, title, compliance, investor reporting, with each portfolio mirrored. Charge a per-loan monthly fee, with a lower rate for hot-standby-only. The successor event is a feature: a failed originator's portfolio moves with no migration.

**Why now.** Dealer-group captives, fintech originators and forward-flow buyers are all new buyers of servicing, and Tricolor made independence a requirement.

**Wedge and first 10 customers.** Two or three fintech/dealer-group portfolios of 20k loans; a hot-standby contract on one warehouse facility.

**Path to $100M (A).** Blended fee of $7 per loan per month (not sourced; test in RFPs) = $84 a year. $100M is ~1.2M loans, ~1.4% of 85M open loans. Pipeline: DFC ~200k loans ($5.0B at $25k), AutoNation Finance ~105k, a Tricolor-sized event ~100k, plus fintech and private-credit portfolios: about 12 portfolios of 100k. Thin margins (10-15% EBITDA, A) and a long sales cycle.

**Who does it today.** Vervent (backup-first, capital-markets services), Loan Portfolio Servicing (tiny, 2012-era), Bridgecrest (DriveTime's captive servicer, not pursuing outside clients at the time of the article), Westlake (U), software vendors (Fiserv, Dealertrack Servicing), BPOs, and Salient (AI loan servicing, $60M Series A, July 2025; paywalled, U).

**Why they lose.** Originators cannot outsource their own borrowers; software vendors will not take regulatory servicing liability; BPOs lack lender-grade licensing and compliance; Vervent's brand is a backup-servicer brand.

**Moat.** Scale economies in cost per loan, switching costs (servicing transfers are painful), process power (compliance across states).

**Legal check.** State servicing and collection licenses; ABS servicing-agreement consents; FDCPA/Reg F if acting as a debt collector; data security.

**First 90 days.** List the 30 largest new auto originators that do not own servicing; price a 20k-loan pilot; secure state licenses.

**Outside-the-box: 3/10.** The most conventional idea here; included because it is the most reachable $100M.

## SHOCK lens

### 7. Pledge Registry: a neutral cross-lender collateral ledger with an insured certificate

**One-liner.** After Tricolor, a neutral registry where warehouse lenders, ABS trustees and forward-flow buyers register hashed VINs and e-chattel-paper control so a double pledge is caught before it is funded, plus an insurer-backed certificate for registered paper.

**Shock.** Tricolor (Sept 2025): about $2.2B of purported assets pledged against ~$1.4B of real collateral, with a founder indicted (S, allegations). First Brands, in the same weeks, hit receivables lenders. Banks are scrutinised for lending to non-bank financial institutions.

**Customer.** Warehouse banks, private-credit funds, ABS trustees and originators.

**Payer and signer.** Originators pay (the way a buyer pays mortgage title insurance) to win a higher advance rate; banks and funds make registration a covenant. Signers: Head of Asset-Based Finance/CRO at the lender, Treasurer/CFO at the originator.

**Earned secret.** From an ABS analyst: trustees, raters and custodians each check their own slice and rely on servicer reports. For motor vehicles the lien is perfected by notation on the title, not a UCC filing, so there is no central place to look, and the ABA article itself notes that lien searches miss possession or control perfection. The only way to catch a duplicate is a neutral party that sees all lenders at once. Nobody was paid to be that party.

**Problem.** Controls proposed after Tricolor (lien searches, custodians, cash sweeps, verification agents) are each lender-specific and none sees across facilities.

**Solution.** (1) Hashed-VIN registry using private set intersection so lenders share nothing but duplicates; (2) continuous matching of the named lienholder against state ELT records through the ELT providers; (3) registration of "control" for e-chattel paper across vendors' vaults (eOriginal, Dealertrack, SecureClose), like the MERS eRegistry does for mortgage eNotes (U); (4) monitoring of lien releases, title-absent status and bank-statement versus servicer-remittance reconciliation; (5) an insurer-backed Collateral Integrity Certificate priced like title insurance.

**Why now.** Tricolor, a live indictment, Wolters Kluwer and Rithm arguing for continuous asset-level verification and shared registries, and a new monitoring seed round (CORA, Sept 2026) show the budget exists.

**Wedge and first 10 customers.** A one-time hashed-VIN overlap test run by three warehouse banks and two originators; the overlap count is the sales headline.

**Path to $100M (A).** About $250B of auto ABS outstanding (2-year life on $127B a year) plus ~$150B of warehouse, forward-flow and bank-pledged paper = ~$400B, ~20M loans at ~$20k. Registry fee $3.50 per loan a year = $70M. Certificate: 0.30% (A, no market price exists) on 40% of ~$150B newly pledged non-bank balances = ~$180M of premium; our 20% share = ~$36M. Total ~$106M needs more than half of pledged loans on the registry and 40% certificate uptake: heroic, and plausible only if a bank-led covenant sets a market standard. Adding other serial-numbered collateral (RVs, boats, equipment) makes it realistic but is outside this pool. Verdict: borderline.

**Who does it today.** Custodians and e-vaults (Wolters Kluwer's eOriginal, Dealertrack's vault, SecureClose), ELT providers (Dealertrack, VINtek, DDI, PDP, Secure Title Administration), MBSi/VINchex (lien checks for repossession), Black Book vehicle verification, CORA (monitoring), field-exam firms and accounting firms. I found no cross-lender registry and no insurer product for double-pledged collateral.

**Why they lose.** Each sees only its own customers and its own slice; vault vendors are competitors and cannot register each other's control; rating agencies and trustees are issuer-paid; bureaus see people, not collateral. Cross-lender sharing needs a neutral party trusted by lenders who will not hand each other their VIN lists.

**Moat.** Network economies (each added lender raises the chance of catching a duplicate; a late entrant cannot copy the coverage) plus a cornered resource in neutrality and data. The MERS eRegistry is the precedent.

**Legal check.** UCC 9-105 control and 9-311 title-notation perfection mean a registry detects, it does not perfect; antitrust and confidentiality (hashed, aggregate-only); insurability of a certificate (title insurance for personal property and receivables is uncommon, U).

**First 90 days.** Run the overlap test; sign letters of intent with two vault vendors; get a Lloyd's or MGA view on pricing; get a legal opinion on registering control.

**Outside-the-box: 7/10.**

### 8. Seasoned-Paper Exchange: sell the loans that survived a year

**One-liner.** BHPH dealers and small lenders originate at 20%+ APR, season each loan for 12 months, and sell the performing ones, verified and registered, to credit unions, banks and private credit, recycling the dealer's capital.

**Shock.** Non-prime ABS issuance fell 27.8% early in 2026 and banks pulled back after Tricolor (S), while private credit has capital but no way to underwrite the BHPH long tail.

**Customer.** Sellers: BHPH dealers and independent non-bank lenders. Buyers: credit unions (refinance the borrower at a lower rate), banks, private-credit funds.

**Payer and signer.** The buyer's Head of Indirect/Consumer Lending or ABF desk pays about 1% of balance; the dealer or lender CFO pays about 0.5% and gets liquidity, a bounty and the customer relationship.

**Earned secret.** From a BHPH principal's cash-flow seat: the first year decides the loan. A loan that has made 12 payments is far safer than one that has made two, but the dealer's capital is tied up at 12-15% (U) and cannot recycle into the next sale. Cumulative-loss curves in non-prime pools are front-loaded (U). BHPH balances tripled to $32B (S) and BHPH loans default at about 1.9 times, and enter active repossession at 16.6 times, the rate of traditional lenders (S), so it is the seasoned slice that is worth buying.

**Problem.** Banks and funds will not underwrite a single BHPH dealer; dealers cannot raise cheap capital; borrowers who have proven themselves stay at 24-29% APR.

**Solution.** Verification and registration (idea 7), standardized servicing data (idea 6), dealer-level seasoning scorecards, and two exits: assignment to private credit at a premium, or refinance into a credit-union loan with a dealer bounty.

**Why now.** The funding squeeze above, forward-flow deals proving that private credit wants non-prime auto (TPG-OneMain $2.4B), and ad-hoc trades like Westlake's ~$100M sale to HFC Acceptance (Jan 2026).

**Wedge and first 10 customers.** Ten BHPH dealers with 1,000+ active accounts and two credit unions.

**Path to $100M (A).** BHPH originations ~$18-22B a year (assuming a ~1.5-year balance life on $32B); loans current at month 12 are ~40% of originations at ~70% of balance = $5-6B. Add ~$90B of other independent non-bank balances, ~$15B of graduates. Flow ~$20B a year. Take 1.5% (1.0% buyer, 0.5% seller) = $300M at 100%; $100M needs ~$6.7B sold, about a third of the flow. Heroic. It improves if fintech originators' seasoned paper is included.

**Who does it today.** Pagaya (AI network and forward flow, buys fresh volume from partners), forward-flow programs (TPG-OneMain), ad-hoc whole-loan sales, everchain exchange (deficiency paper), BHPH dealers' own bank lines.

**Why they lose.** Forward-flow buyers want fresh volume from scaled originators; banks cannot diligence BHPH dealers; credit unions cannot reach them. No one sells a standardized, verified, seasoned product.

**Moat.** Network economies (dealer supply and buyer demand) and a cornered resource in dealer-level seasoning data.

**Legal check.** Lender or broker licensing; Reg Z assignment and refinance disclosures; FCRA; UDAP around "captive" BHPH customers; dealers may resist losing customers.

**First 90 days.** Collect 12 months of payment history from five dealers; build the seasoning scorecard; get an indicative bid from one credit union and one private-credit buyer.

**Outside-the-box: 5/10.**

## Considered and killed

- OBBBA car-loan interest reporting (lender information returns for the new deduction): pool ~$30-60M a year (A, ~6M eligible loans a year at $2-5 a return) and the deduction sunsets after 2028 (U). Round 1's expired-why-now trap.
- Early-intervention priced per saved loan (pool hint 2): a 5-point cure lift on ~2.5M defaults a year is ~125k saves, so $100M at $500 each needs ~200k saves, more than any plausible coverage. Round 1 died here; I repeated the arithmetic and it fails again.
- AI collections voice agents (Prodigal, Salient): funded and a listed cliche.
- OEM-telematics collateral intelligence (replace aftermarket GPS/starter-interrupt): the borrower can revoke consent just before default, so the data disappears when it matters; subprime cars are also older and less connected (U).
- Gig-driver vehicle loans repaid from platform payouts: a graveyard (Uber's own subprime lending sold to Fair in 2018, after a $20M settlement) and platform-dependent (S, via search).
- Contract-in-transit instant dealer funding: ~$5.5B average CIT (A: ~$675B dealer-originated x 3 days / 365) x 8% carry = ~$440M a year of dealer cost; a provider taking a quarter of it at 60% of savings earns ~$66M. Below $100M.
- Digital-twin servicing SaaS: at $1-2 a loan a month it needs 4-8M accounts.
- AI-native creditors' rights law firm (Arizona ABS): bankruptcy auto-claim fees ~$120M a year at best (A), over 50% share.
- BHPH roll-up: revenue is vehicle sales rather than a profit pool, capital intensive, and the sector is wounded (NABD "best performer" bad debt is 25% of sales; S).
- Dealer-risk exchange and dealer-holdback exchange: path needs near-universal adoption.
- Generic auto-refinance marketplace: the obvious idea, contested.

## Stack and overlaps

Ideas 4 (captive), 6 (servicer), 7 (registry), 8 (seasoned exchange) and 5 (rail) share customers and could be sequenced as one company; on their own, 7 is the one with a distinctive wedge (the overlap test), and 1 and 2 are the two that attack the same defaulting borrowers from different ends (collateral return versus borrower rescue).
