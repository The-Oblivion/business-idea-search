## 1. Ready-to-Build: the constraint-clearing layer for line crews (INSIDER: line-construction superintendent, 20+ years)

- **One-liner:** Sell contractors and utilities a live "ready backlog" in which every job is gated on the six things that actually idle a crew, so crew-hours stop leaking.
- **Customer:** Line contractors with 40-500 crews on unit-price or time-and-equipment MSAs, and utility construction and distribution operations departments that run in-house crews. First 10: independent contractors with 40-150 crews serving two or three IOUs (not Quanta or MasTec, who build in-house).
- **Payer and signer:** Contractor VP Operations or COO from operating budget; on unit-price work every recovered hour is margin. For utilities, the Director of Construction or Distribution Operations from the contractor-oversight and construction-management line. MYR's 10-K says MSA work is billed unit-price, time-and-materials or time-and-equipment, and time-and-equipment pays for crew hours; I could not confirm what share of hours are standby (U).
- **Problem:** A crew of 3-6 people plus 2-3 specialty trucks is a fixed-cost asset (A: $0.6-1.0M a year). It earns only when six gates are open on the same day: an approved stake sheet, staged materials, an outage or clearance window, live 811 locates, access and permits, traffic control. Six different people in four systems own those gates, and the foreman holds the status in his head and a group text. Bain found utility crews' wrench time averaged about 40% (S). Transformers at 128 weeks (S) make the materials gate worse. Outage windows are scarcer as load grows (U, insider).
- **Solution:** Read jobs from the utility work-management system or the contractor ERP. Track the six gates with owners and due dates and chase the owners by text and voice ("locates expire in 4 days", "transformer not at the yard"). Publish a daily ready list ranked by crew qualification (hot-line, rubber-glove), travel and outage window. Re-plan the same day when a gate fails by swapping in a ready job. Keep an audit trail of standby caused by utility-side gates versus contractor-side gates, which settles disputes and feeds prudence reviews. Scalable assets: per-utility gate rules and a cross-utility benchmark of outage-window cancellations and gate-failure rates.
- **Why now:** Record backlogs (Quanta $53.4B, Dycom $9.5B) and capex up about 29% make each idle crew-day dearer than ever. Transformer lead times and thinner experience (over half the workforce under 10 years) remove the slack that tribal knowledge used to cover. Quanta's four acquisitions leave independents hunting for an edge.
- **Wedge:** A "Standby Autopsy": 60 days of timecards and field tickets from 10 crews, sorted by cause, sold as a $15k diagnostic that becomes a $2,500 per crew-month subscription. Start with outage-window and materials-staging gates on distribution rebuild programs.
- **Path to $100M:** (A) about 33k US crews (131.9k BLS workers / about 4; half in-house at utilities). At $2,500 per crew-month ($30k a year, about 4% of a crew's annual billings and about a third of the value of a 10% productivity gain), 3,300 crews = $99M. That is about 10% of all crews, reached through roughly 50 organizations with 60+ crews each. Gross margin 65-70% (A).
- **Who does it today:** Locusview/Itron (as-built capture and billing; about 4,000 crews a day), utility work-management systems (Maximo, SAP, Oracle, IFS; features not verified), Assignar and Planera (general construction scheduling), Utilen (materials coordination), Quanta's internal tools, and spreadsheets plus group texts. I found no product built around the six gates for T&D crews.
- **Why they lose:** The utility's system cannot see the contractor's yard or crew qualifications; the contractor's system cannot see the utility's outage calendar; Locusview records work after it happens; general-construction schedulers do not know clearances or 811 tickets. The biggest contractors will never sell their own tools to rivals. Risk: Itron bundles readiness into Locusview.
- **Moat:** Switching costs (it becomes the daily dispatch routine) plus a cornered resource (cross-utility outage and gate-failure data).
- **Earned secret:** "Standby is billed, so nobody inside the utility sees it as waste until an audit. The costliest idle hour is the 5:30 a.m. call when system operations cancels the outage window, because the three people who knew it was fragile were never told." (insider claim; the share of hours is U)
- **First 90 days:** Sign three superintendents as design partners. Run the Standby Autopsy by hand for 10 crews. Build read-only imports from Maximo/SAP exports and 811 ticket status. Start a human "readiness desk" chasing gate owners by phone. Close two paid pilots at $2,500 per crew-month.
- **Outside-box:** 6.

## 2. Zero-Redline: outcome-priced distribution work packages (INSIDER: distribution designer, 15+ years)

- **One-liner:** A vendor-neutral design pod that delivers construction-ready distribution work packages, priced per package accepted with zero redlines, using the incumbents' tools plus each utility's standards compiled into checks.
- **Customer:** Co-ops and munis with design backlogs, mid-size IOU customer-driven teams, and line contractors that bid design-build MSAs. First 10: 4 co-ops or munis, 3 design-build contractors, 3 mid-size IOU teams.
- **Payer and signer:** Utility Director of Distribution Engineering or VP Engineering from the capitalized design line; for contractors, the VP Preconstruction. Design is already bought: 8-15% of construction cost (S, analog).
- **Problem:** The software exists (SBS, Itron, GSI, Bentley) but the designers do not. Vendors estimate 10-15% of projects need field correction or redesign at 4-8 hours each (U); the causes a designer sees cluster in stale GIS, unrecorded customer changes and revised standards. Engineering firms bill hours, so cutting hours cuts revenue. Contractors earn on units, so a leaner design shrinks their scope.
- **Solution:** A package is field-verified (imagery, LiDAR, street-level plus a foreman photo check), standards-checked (rules compiled from the utility's construction standards), complete with BOM and compatible-unit list, permit and easement flags, and PE-stamped where needed. Priced per package accepted, with free rework on any redline. Drafting pods (US lead plus nearshore) run licensed vendor tools. Over time the standards library and a "why was it rejected" corpus become the checker, and first-pass acceptance becomes the product metric.
- **Why now:** Capex up about 29% against flat engineering headcount (vendor-sponsored claim, U). The design-software vendors have all been bought (SBS by Enverus, GSI by private equity, Locusview by Itron), so no neutral party sells outcomes.
- **Wedge:** One high-volume package type (single-phase overhead and underground service extensions) in two utilities' standards, sold to design-build contractors first because they feel redlines in their margin. Track first-pass acceptance on the first 500 packages.
- **Path to $100M:** (A) T&D capex $100-120B x 9-12% design = $9-14B; about half outsourced = $4.5-7B. At $900-1,000 per package (construction value of about $15k x 8-10%, undercutting hourly pricing), 100-110k packages = $100M. That is 1.5-2.2% of outsourced design dollars, or about 5% of an assumed 2M design work orders a year (A, not sourced).
- **Who does it today:** SBS AUD/Enverus, Itron Distribution Staker, GSI Distribution Design Studio (PE-owned), Bentley, Hexagon; hourly firms Burns & McDonnell, Sargent & Lundy, Black & Veatch, POWER Engineers, Ulteig, Kimley-Horn; offshore design BPOs (U); AI-native entrants Unlimited Industries, Tiros, AZX, Piq, Gridsight.
- **Why they lose:** Software vendors sell seats and cannot carry rework risk; hourly firms lose revenue when hours fall; contractors lose units when designs shrink; AI-native startups chase hyperscaler and industrial EPC where tickets are larger. Risk: Enverus adds services to the SBS base.
- **Moat:** Process power (a rejection-learning loop that raises acceptance rate) with secondary switching costs once a utility's standards live in the checker.
- **Earned secret:** "The designer's real job is predicting what the crew will find in the field. Redlines come from three causes: stale GIS, an unrecorded customer change, a standard nobody told the contractor about. Fix those and you can price by outcome." (insider claim; to be measured on the first 500 packages)
- **First 90 days:** Pick two utilities' standards and compile the rules for one package type. Recruit 2 design-build contractors. Deliver 100 packages at cost with a rework guarantee and log every redline by cause. Set the price from the measured acceptance rate.
- **Outside-box:** 4. This is the pool's own obvious wedge; it stays in because the outcome-priced, vendor-neutral form is not on sale anywhere I found.

## 3. Spec Passport: shared product qualification, paid by the suppliers (PATTERN: free tool paid by the vendors who want the profession's attention)

- **One-liner:** Run the vendor-neutral layer that tests a distribution product once and lets many utilities accept it, free to utility standards engineers, paid for by the manufacturers who want in.
- **Pattern and why not yet:** OpenEvidence is free to physicians and paid by pharma for the moment of attention. Here the profession is utility standards and materials engineers, and the buyer is any manufacturer trying to get onto approved-materials lists. It has not happened because no one is paid to write product failures down in a form another utility can use.
- **Customer:** Free side: standards and materials engineers at utilities. Paid side: manufacturers and importers of transformers, cutouts, arresters, connectors, insulators, composite and steel poles, crossarms and conductor. First 10: 3 new US transformer plants, 4 importers, 3 hardware makers.
- **Payer and signer:** Manufacturer VP of utility sales or director of product qualification, from the budget that already pays for samples, lab tests, trial installs, travel and consultants.
- **Problem:** Each utility keeps its own approved-materials list. Trials run a year or longer (EWEB) and wait on lists (Queen Creek) (S). I found no utility that accepts another's test data (S gap). With 128-week transformer lead times (S), utilities want alternates but cannot qualify them fast, and a new supplier cannot afford 50 separate qualifications. The Nordics share supplier pre-qualification (SELLIHCA) and the UK has UVDB (S), but product-level reciprocity is missing.
- **Solution:** (1) Accredited-lab test protocols per category, covering IEEE/ANSI/NEMA plus the extra requirements of the top 25 utilities; (2) a free Utility Console: side-by-side comparison against the utility's own spec, pooled field-trial results from member utilities, alerts when a product fails elsewhere; (3) a Passport credential that utilities opt to accept in place of repeat lab tests; (4) manufacturers pay for protocols and the Passport (fees buy testing and listing, never ranking); (5) later, a sourcing desk that routes urgent RFQs to passported alternates.
- **Why now:** Shortages (transformers at 128 weeks), steel, aluminum and copper tariffs, and new US capacity entrants who need many utilities to say yes at once; utilities' own staff are thinner than ever (over half under 10 years' experience).
- **Wedge:** Distribution transformers and cutouts, where the need for alternates is acute and test methods are standard. Anchor with 5 co-ops, one G&T and 2 IOUs that agree to read the data, plus 10 paying manufacturers on a $25k pilot.
- **Path to $100M:** All inputs are (A/U). (a) Qualification fees: assume about 1,500 manufacturers and importers sell to US utilities, each spending about $400k a year on qualification (U) = $600M pool; capturing 10% = $60M. (b) Sourcing desk: distribution materials spend about $24B (A: 40% of about $60B distribution capex); routing 10% at 1.5% = $36M. (c) Utility data subscriptions: 100 utilities x $100k = $10M. Total about $106M. Needs 10% of an unverified spend pool plus 10% of materials, so treat it as the least certain size.
- **Who does it today:** Every utility's own AML and trial process; UL Solutions (cable procurement specs), Tecnalia (EU lab qualification), SELLIHCA and UVDB (supplier-level), Avetta/ISN/Veriforce (contractor safety, not products), Utilen (project and materials coordination), distributors with supplier programs (Wesco, Graybar, Border States). Free substitute: EPRI, EEI and NRECA standardization work (not checked).
- **Why they lose:** Utilities cannot afford to publish their failures or test for others; distributors and OEMs are not neutral; prequalification schemes certify companies, not products; no one owns the cross-utility field-performance record.
- **Moat:** Network economies (more utilities accepting means more manufacturers paying, and the reverse) plus a cornered resource (the pooled field-performance dataset).
- **Earned secret:** "An approved-materials list is mostly institutional memory, such as why a product failed at one co-op in 2011. Utilities do not share it because nobody is paid to write it down in a form another utility can use. The first party paid to do that, by the vendors who want in, becomes the record."
- **First 90 days:** Interview 20 standards engineers on what evidence they would accept in place of a repeat test. Compile 8 utilities' transformer and cutout requirements. Contract an ISO 17025 lab. Sign 5 manufacturers to a paid pilot. Launch the free console for the 5 co-ops.
- **Outside-box:** 8.

## 4. Capacity Upgrade Desk: limiting-element-first reconductoring, funded by the load (PATTERN: a deep-pocketed buyer's demand shock re-rates a stalled technology)

- **One-liner:** Find the single span or terminal that actually limits each congested line, fix it, and reconductor only where it pays, with the data-center or industrial customer funding the work in exchange for deliverable capacity.
- **Pattern and why not yet:** Advanced conductors have existed for about 20 years (more than 90,000 miles deployed worldwide 2003-2023) yet the US rebuilds or reconductors only about 1% of lines a year, because utilities earn their return on capex either way and no one is paid to recommend the cheaper fix. Hyperscalers now pay for speed (Google solicited advanced-conductor projects in Jun 2025).
- **Customer:** Transmission owners with congested 69-230 kV corridors serving load pockets, and the developers who need deliverability. First 10: 5 utilities in constrained pockets (PJM, ERCOT, MISO, Southeast) and 5 funding developers.
- **Payer and signer:** The funding developer's VP Energy or Infrastructure, from interconnection capex (as a contribution in aid of construction or a capacity-contribution agreement); the utility's VP Transmission Planning signs the scope and the PUC approves recovery.
- **Problem:** About 700,000 miles of lines (S). New lines take 10-15 years; reconductoring 1-2 years (vendor-sourced, U) and typically costs under half of new build (advocacy source). Advanced conductor costs 1.5-5x conventional (DOE). FERC Order 1920 requires transmission providers to consider advanced conductors and dynamic ratings, and CA SB 1006 studies were due 2026-01-01 (S), but studies rarely reach construction. On many lines the limit is not the conductor but a terminal, a clearance crossing or a structure (insider, U).
- **Solution:** LiDAR and imagery line survey with ratings reconciliation; a limiting-element model across sag, clearance, structure and terminal equipment; a ranked list by MW gained per dollar and per month; design and bid packages that fix the limiting element and reconductor only where needed, with conductors specified from several makers; outage plans that use Ready-to-Build (idea 1); a commercial template in which the developer funds the upgrade, receives capacity rights, the utility owns the asset and a rider recovers cost; infrastructure funds pre-underwrite because the cash flows are contractual.
- **Why now:** 1,066 GW of requested data-center load, only 12% with firm service (S); FERC's 2026-06-18 show-cause orders ask RTOs to explain why they are not using alternative transmission technologies (S); NJ S674 and the REWIRE Act of 2026 are pending (S).
- **Wedge:** A $150-400k limiting-element audit per corridor, sold to a developer and a utility together; the first 10 customers are 5 audits plus 5 follow-on design packages.
- **Path to $100M:** (A) 7,000 miles a year x 25% that is capacity-driven and addressable = 1,750 miles. Fees at about 12% of a roughly $1M-per-mile project (audit, engineering, program management) need about $830M of project value, or about 830 miles = 12% of all miles. If it books turnkey EPC revenue, $100M is about 100 miles at $1M per mile (net margin 10-12%).
- **Who does it today:** Quanta (and Quanta Technology), MYR, Pike, Burns & McDonnell, Sargent & Lundy, POWER Engineers; conductor makers CTC Global, TS Conductor, Southwire, Prysmian; dynamic-rating vendors LineVision, Heimdall Power, Ampacimon; Google as a buyer rather than a provider.
- **Why they lose:** EPC firms are paid by the mile of construction, so telling a utility to fix a $2M terminal instead of a $40M reconductor cuts their scope; conductor makers sell wire; rating vendors sell sensors; utilities earn a return on the larger capex.
- **Moat:** Counter-positioning (neutral advice against EPC economics), then process power and a limiting-element library by utility.
- **Earned secret:** "On most lines I worked, the conductor was not what limited the rating; one old terminal or one clearance crossing did. The cheapest megawatt on the system is at the weakest span."
- **First 90 days:** Recruit two transmission planners and a line engineer. Offer one free limiting-element audit to a utility in a hyperscaler load pocket, using existing NERC facility-rating data and a LiDAR subcontractor. Draft the developer-funded capacity template with a utility lawyer and an infrastructure-fund contact.
- **Outside-box:** 6.

## 5. Accredited Delivery Partner: customer-funded connections at the customer's speed (DILEMMA: utilities cannot sell speed)

- **One-liner:** Be the utility-accredited third party that designs, permits and builds customer-funded connections to the utility's standard, so customers who would pay to go faster can.
- **The dilemma:** A regulated utility works a first-in-first-out queue under non-discriminatory tariffs, earns little on customer-contributed assets, and cannot sell a priority lane or a fixed date. Engineers and contractors each sell a piece, and the utility's standards and inspection gate ownership acceptance. The UK solved part of this with independent connection providers (Ofgem's Feb 2026 call for input proposes more self-build of high-voltage connection assets; independent providers held only about 8% of contestable electric connections in the 2008 debate and Ofgem found effective competition in 42 of 126 segments in 2010-15) (S).
- **Customer:** Developers and owners with customer-funded extensions and upgrades: commercial and industrial campuses, 1-75 MW data-center and EV depots, subdivisions, municipal projects. First 10: 10 projects of 1-20 MW in California (applicant design under Rules 15/16) and Ohio (third-party installation to utility specs).
- **Payer and signer:** The customer's VP Development or construction director, from the utility-service and interconnection line of the project budget (a contribution in aid of construction the customer already pays).
- **Problem:** PG&E's own applicant-design guide says at least 5% of projects may take up to 357 days, and the CPUC's 2024 energization targets only report and shame (S). SDG&E's 2026 guide lists "customer dependencies" (approvals, payments, easement signatures, permits) that no one manages (S). A delayed campus loses revenue every month.
- **Solution:** Accreditation per utility (as the Ohio rule and NH statute contemplate for approved contractors). A fixed-fee, fixed-date package: pre-application scoping, utility form filing, design to the utility's standard (using idea 2's library), easement and permit management, long-lead procurement, construction by accredited crews, utility inspection and an energization checklist. The partner books the full contract value, subcontracts crews and keeps an assumed 12-18% design and management margin. Over time its cycle-time data per utility becomes the reference regulators can point to.
- **Why now:** CPUC D.24-09-020 (2024) and D.26-02-025 (2026, flexible service connection offering); data-center and industrial load; Ofgem's 2026 reform direction; FERC's June 2026 orders cover transmission-level only.
- **Legal premise (checked):** Supported by CA Rules 15/16 and PG&E's applicant-design process, Ohio OAC 4901:1-9-07 (to utility specs and inspection), and NH RSA 370:12 (private property, utility-approved contractor, utility design). Not found in Florida, where the extension remains utility property. It is a state-by-state patchwork, so reaching $100M needs three to five states or utility-side accreditation programs, not a single rule change.
- **Wedge:** California customer-funded 1-10 MW projects with a signed will-serve letter, sold as "energized by date X or we pay".
- **Path to $100M:** (A, no national figure found) distribution capex about $60B x 25-30% customer-driven = $15-18B; customer-funded and contestable where third parties are allowed today about 15-20% of that = $2.5-3.5B. Gross revenue of $100M is 3-4% of that pool, and the net design and management margin would be $12-18M, so it only works if the margin pool grows with more states and bigger loads.
- **Who does it today:** Customers' civil engineers plus local line contractors, PG&E's applicant-design program, and in the UK the independent connection providers. Quanta and MasTec do not chase small customer-funded jobs. No national accredited-partner brand found (U).
- **Why they lose:** Utilities are barred from selling speed; engineers and contractors sell parts; the customer coordinating five parties is the bottleneck.
- **Moat:** A cornered resource (accreditation by utilities) plus counter-positioning against the utility's own constraint.
- **Earned secret:** "Most delay is not engineering. It is the customer dependencies, such as easement signatures, permits, inspections and panel readiness, that nobody manages because the utility says it is the customer's job and the customer does not know how."
- **First 90 days:** Get onto PG&E/SCE/SDG&E and one Ohio utility's approved-contractor or applicant-design lists. Sign three customers to a fixed-date package. Record the days spent at each step and each dependency.
- **Outside-box:** 7.

## 6. Pooled Crews: a contract vehicle with reserved crews for co-ops and munis (DILEMMA: big contractors and hourly engineers cannot serve small utilities)

- **One-liner:** Pool about 2,800 small utilities' line construction, engineering and materials demand into a pre-competed multi-year vehicle with reserved crew-months, so small utilities stop being last in line.
- **The dilemma:** Quanta ($53.4B backlog), MasTec, MYR and Dycom optimize for large multi-year programs; a 15-pole co-op job does not justify mobilization. Hourly engineers have minimums and cannot hold staff for a six-crew co-op. A co-op cannot reserve crews alone because paying standby invites board and lender scrutiny. Cooperative purchasing exists (Sourcewell's electrical IDIQ awards include T&D services) but is generalist and does not reserve capacity (S).
- **Customer:** About 1,998 public power utilities and 864-905 co-ops. First 10: ten co-ops in one G&T system, signed through the G&T or statewide association.
- **Payer and signer:** Co-op GM and board, from the capital construction budget (RUS or CFC-financed work plans). The vehicle's fee is embedded in contractor pricing, as in other cooperative purchasing, plus an engineering subscription.
- **Problem:** Co-ops own 42% of line miles on 8 consumers per mile (S), with thin staff, a young workforce, 128-week transformer lead times and last place in allocation. Contractors say yes to anyone who can commit crew-months a year ahead.
- **Solution:** Competitively bid multi-state MSAs with 3-5 qualified contractors including mid-size independents, unit-price rate cards, and crew-months reserved against aggregated member demand. A shared engineering pool (standard RUS units, staking, work plans, loan packages). Pooled materials buying and a shared transformer spares pool. Common inspection, safety and storm mutual-aid terms. Fee of 2-3% of routed construction plus a $50-150k engineering subscription.
- **Why now:** Contractor backlogs, capex up about 29%, data-center and industrial load arriving at co-ops, and lead times that punish small buyers.
- **Wedge:** One G&T system's 10 co-ops: 36-month pipeline, 3 contractors, 20 reserved crew-months.
- **Path to $100M:** (A, no sourced co-op and muni capex total) about $20B a year of capital spend, of which $12B is construction, engineering and materials that could route through the vehicle. At 2.5%, $100M needs $4B routed (33% of $12B). Adding 300 members x $100k = $30M of engineering subscriptions lowers it to $2.8B routed, or 14-20% of the pool. I count this as heroic.
- **Who does it today:** Sourcewell/OMNIA/TIPS (generalist), NRECA, CFC and statewide associations (education, lobbying, finance), APPA mutual aid, engineering firms with co-op practices (not verified), and each utility's own RFP.
- **Why they lose:** Associations are non-profits that cannot underwrite crew capacity or take performance risk; general vehicles do not reserve crews; contractors cannot reserve for unknown demand.
- **Moat:** Scale economies in purchasing and a cornered resource (reserved crew capacity), with counter-positioning against the big contractors.
- **Earned secret:** "A co-op's constraint is not money. RUS and CFC lend. It is crews. The manager who calls three contractors in February gets none until October."
- **First 90 days:** Sign a G&T or association sponsor. Compile a 36-month pipeline from 10 co-ops. Issue an RFP for reserved crew-months and collect bids. Settle the legal form and who is the lead agency.
- **Outside-box:** 5.

## 7. Pre-Accepted Substations for non-hyperscaler load (SHOCK: the AI load wave created a buyer who must build utility-grade assets)

- **One-liner:** Sell developers of 20-300 MW campuses a customer-owned substation and gen-tie package designed in advance to their utility's standard, with equipment slots reserved, so interconnection takes 12-15 months instead of 24-36.
- **The shock and new buyer:** Over 10 MW usually means the operator designs, builds and maintains its own substation, with core equipment of $3-7M and total interconnection of $20-100M+ (vendor estimates, U). The buyers are neoclouds, colocation firms, powered-land developers, converted miners and industrial reshoring projects without in-house utility engineering. Study fees of $100-300k and security of $50k/MW in Texas's draft rule, FERC's June 2026 show-cause orders and 768 GW of phantom load raise the cost of slipping (S).
- **Customer:** Developers outside the top six hyperscalers with signed will-serve letters. First 10: sites in ERCOT, AEP and Dominion territory, Georgia and Arizona.
- **Payer and signer:** Developer's Chief Development Officer or VP Infrastructure from site power capex (A: $30-70k/MW for the customer-owned scope), often with lender sign-off.
- **Problem:** Each utility has its own substation standards for relay schemes, SCADA protocols, bus configurations, clearances and metering. Designs get rejected or reworked for months, and transformers at 128 weeks must be ordered before design settles. EPC majors prefer larger programs.
- **Solution:** A library of 25-30 utilities' customer-owned-substation requirements compiled into rules; 4-6 pre-engineered families (100/200/300 MW, ring or breaker-and-a-half, 115-230 kV) with utility-reviewed one-lines and protection schemes; reserved transformer slots with two or three makers (Spec Passport, idea 3); factory-built control houses; construction by accredited crews (idea 5) scheduled with Ready-to-Build (idea 1); a utility acceptance package. Revenue is a design and management fee of 8-10% plus margin on procurement and EPC, booked gross.
- **Why now:** WoodMac's 12% firm / 17% likely split means only real projects need execution, and the developers with firm service cannot wait for custom engineering.
- **Wedge:** Three 100-150 MW sites with signed will-serve letters in one utility territory, as proofs.
- **Path to $100M:** (A) WoodMac's roughly 28% serveable of 1,066 GW is about 300 GW over 6-8 years, or 30-45 GW a year. About 40% is outside the top-six hyperscalers = 12-18 GW; at $50k/MW for the customer-owned scope = $0.6-0.9B (up to $1-2B with gen-ties). $100M gross is 5-10% of that, at a net margin of 10-15%.
- **Who does it today:** Quanta, MYR, Burns & McDonnell, Black & Veatch, Sargent & Lundy, POWER Engineers; Hitachi Energy, Siemens Energy and GE Vernova turnkey; Powell and Eaton e-houses; Unlimited Industries (a16z; claims pre-construction engineering cut from six months to weeks); Tiros; hyperscalers in-house.
- **Why they lose:** EPC majors are capacity-limited and favor large programs; OEMs sell equipment, not utility acceptance; AI-native EPCs optimize layouts but do not hold 30 utilities' acceptance rules. Risk: utilities change standards; Unlimited builds the same library.
- **Moat:** A cornered resource (pre-approvals and equipment slots) and process power.
- **Earned secret:** "The substation is rarely late because of engineering. It is late because the utility's standards group rejects it on page 140 of its own standard, after the transformer is already on order."
- **First 90 days:** Pick two utilities' standards, compile them, draw the 100 and 200 MW families, get an informal utility pre-review, reserve transformer slots and sign LOIs with three developers.
- **Outside-box:** 5.

## 8. Make-Ready Rail: the bulk pole-application exchange (SHOCK: FCC timelines and the BEAD pole wave)

- **One-liner:** Be the single bulk-application, loading-analysis, estimating and make-ready rail between telecom attachers and about 2,000 pole-owning utilities, paid per pole by the attacher, so shot clocks are met and fiber gets built.
- **The shock:** The FCC's 2025-07-25 order sets 90-day surveys and 29-day estimates and requires bulk applications. BEAD builds touch about 3.9M utility-owned poles across 2,053 electric utilities. California providers have waited a year or more (CPUC D.26-05-007) (S).
- **Customer:** Fiber and cable attachers, BEAD subgrantees and their contractors; pole-owning utilities that cannot meet the clock. First 10: six BEAD-funded regional ISPs in California, two cable operators, two co-ops that own poles.
- **Payer and signer:** The attacher's VP Network Engineering or Construction from the make-ready and engineering line of build capex ($500-5,000 per pole by industry range).
- **Problem:** Each utility has its own application format, survey method and fee schedule (CPS Energy charges $2 to review, $25 for a loading analysis and $55-64 for make-ready engineering per pole). The pole-attachment desk is a handful of people and the fees do not fund hiring.
- **Solution:** One bulk package (KMZ or GIS routes) normalized to each owner's form, with loading analyses run by licensed engineers; field data from utility-approved contractors under the FCC's self-help provisions; make-ready estimates from each owner's unit-price schedule; every pole tracked against its FCC clock with a complaint-ready evidence package; dispatch of utility-approved crews for communications-space make-ready. The asset is a "pole truth" database: survey once, reuse for the next attacher, the utility and insurers.
- **Why now:** FCC order (2025), FCC 26-6 definitions (2026), BEAD, AT&T and Verizon each above 10% of Dycom's revenue, Dycom FY2027 backlog about $12B (one aggregator).
- **Wedge:** California bulk applications for 20-50k poles for three to five BEAD subgrantees.
- **Path to $100M:** (A) pole-related cost averages about $320 per BEAD pole ($1.25B / 3.9M) and up to $1,190. Net fee model at about $80 per pole (review, loading analysis, engineering, management) needs 1.25M poles a year. I estimate 6-12M poles a year need attachment applications (BEAD about 1M a year plus AT&T, Verizon/Frontier, Lumen, Charter/Cox and regional builds, all A), so that is 10-20% of poles. Gross booking of make-ready field work reaches $100M at roughly 170-300k poles (3-5%) but nets about 15%.
- **Who does it today:** Utilities' own desks and engineering vendors (Osmose, Katapult-type loading software; not verified), telecom contractors Dycom, MasTec and Quanta, and attachers' in-house teams. ikeGPS PoleForeman reached about NZ$11M ARR in two years (secondary), which shows demand for pole software. I found no funded startup doing bulk make-ready.
- **Why they lose:** Utilities cannot standardize across each other; attachers each solve it alone; contractors are paid for field work and prefer fewer, bigger crews.
- **Moat:** Network economies (once the major owners accept the rail, attachers must use it) plus a cornered resource (the pole database).
- **Earned secret:** "Pole owners are not stonewalling. Their pole desk has four people and the survey fee does not cover a loading analysis. Hand them a complete, engineered application and the clock starts working for you."
- **First 90 days:** Take three BEAD subgrantees' routes in California and file 20k-pole bulk applications with two PE firms running loading analyses. Track every pole against the FCC clock and publish cycle times.
- **Outside-box:** 5.

## Ideas killed in research (and why)

- **Common App / "credit bureau" for large loads (planner's idea).** The insight is real: 768 GW of phantom load and duplicate filings are verified, and no standard exists for retail load. But the fee pool is small: about 3,500 live requests, a $15k packet fee, a $75k verified tier and utility subscriptions top out at about $110M at 100% share (A). FERC's show-cause orders, ERCOT's batch process and Texas's duplicate-disclosure rule are free substitutes. Surety on $50k/MW security adds only $10-25M of commission (A). Dead as a $100M business.
- **Billing-grade as-built capture (ambient capture).** Locusview, now owned by Itron, already covers as-built capture, close-out and invoicing for about 4,000 crews a day. Racing a bundle with a form-based incumbent is too hard.
- **Storm-capacity options and damage assessment.** The cost is real (FPL contractors $625M for three storms) but lumpy; a vendor taking 15% of a 7% saving on a $8-12B annual pool (A) needs about all of it.
- **Lineworker training academy.** About 10,900 openings a year (S) caps the pool; at $15k a placement it needs more than half of all openings.
- **Right-of-way and easement factory.** My arithmetic puts the services pool at $100-300M a year (A), so $100M would need 30-100%.
- **Contractor receivables finance.** About $64M of interest at 16% of invoices, mostly funding cost (A).
- **Joint-trench exchange (power plus fiber).** UMass finds co-undergrounding 39% cheaper, but my sizing of the shared civil cost pool is $225M and a 6% fee gives about $13M (A).
- **Crew lodging platform.** About $2B of spend (A); an 8-15% take needs half of it.
