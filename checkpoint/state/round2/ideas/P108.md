# P108 - US prison and jail health care (state corrections departments and counties paying contractors) (round 2 ideas)

Written 2026-10-18. Eight ideas, two per lens. Every number is tagged: (S) = sourced, (A) = my arithmetic or assumption, (U) = unverified claim from a secondary source, a search summary, or an insider hypothesis I could not confirm.

## Pool facts used (checked this session unless marked)

- (S) Size: Pew put state-prison health spending at $8.1B in FY2015 (peak $8.2B in FY2009, $7.7B in FY2011). (A) About $11B today at 3%/yr, per the pool file. Jail health and the federal BOP are not sourced; (A) jails are roughly $4-6B (667,700 average daily population x $6-9k per person-year), BOP roughly $1.5-2B (U).
- (S) Population: jails held an average of 664,800 people and processed 7.6M admissions in the 12 months to June 2023 (BJS), rising to 667,700 and 7.9M in 2023-24; average stay 32 days. State and federal prisons held 1,254,200 at yearend 2023; 193,000 were 55 or older (Prison Policy Initiative citing BJS).
- (S) Where the money goes: off-site hospital care was 27% of Virginia's and 23% of New York's prison health budget in 2015, and about 20% across ten states in 2007-11 (Pew 2018). Pew also found that pure capitation closes states off from savings on off-site care (2017 report, via search snippet). Correctional systems mostly pay fee-for-service at non-negotiated rates, and double and unbundled billing are common (NCCHC, Oct 2021).
- (S) Deaths: 1,200 local-jail deaths in 2019, 355 of them suicides (BJS). Settlements in 2026 alone: Huerfano CO $9.19M plus $1M from the vendor foundation; Choctaw OK $4M; Cleveland OK $20M ($2M insurer, $18M county over three years); Franklin WA $2.5M through a pool with a $25k deductible.
- (S) Insurance squeeze: an Oklahoma sheriff reports jail liability rates up as much as 300% in four years and cover hard to find; one county pool voted to exclude the jail; a 2021 Oregon RFP response shows a contractor cutting its professional-liability limit from $10M to $8M because of market capacity.
- (S) The shock: Wellpath filed Chapter 11 on 2024-11-11 with $644M of debt, emerged May 2025 owned by its lenders; unsecured creditors got a negotiated settlement worth possibly more than 50% of claims. In Georgia alone 750+ medical and EMS providers claimed $75.6M. Lane County OR had to file an indemnity claim in the bankruptcy. YesCare filed 2026-05-08, said on May 21 it was ceasing operations, and had been serving about 20,000 people a day; Alabama DOC ended a roughly $1B YesCare contract and moved to an emergency agreement with NaphCare from May 3; Polk County FL and Orange County NY moved to CFG; Maryland moved to Centurion ($1.7B state prisons, $723M pretrial). (U, AI-assisted source) Wellpath is closing its Shelby County TN jail health operation by Sept 30, 2026, cutting 119 jobs. (U) Placer County CA picked a new vendor for a seven-year, $131M contract in May 2026. Georgia's 2024 move to Centurion was an emergency procurement without an RFP (a Fulton County judge dismissed Wellpath's challenge).
- (A) Contract sizes: Placer about $18.7M/yr ($131M / 7), Monterey (Wellpath) about $14.8M/yr ($44.3M / 3), Mississippi VitalCore about $119M/yr ($357M / 3), Maryland about $340M/yr for state prisons. So one state contract is a $100M+ business; a mid-size county is $10-19M.
- (S) Ownership: Wellpath was owned by H.I.G. Capital (formed 2018 from CFMG and Correct Care Solutions); YesCare was BlueMountain-owned, then the Flacks Group from 2020 (PESP); Centurion is a Centene subsidiary. (U) Over 60% of jails outsource health care (one report); NaphCare says TechCare EHR is in 220+ facilities (vendor claim).
- (S) Staffing: North Carolina prison nursing vacancy above 44%; contract nurses $64/hr vs $25.40 for permanent (2023); NC prison health spend $400M in FY2024. South Dakota: LPN vacancy 53%, RN 40%, travel-nurse spend $4.6M against a $38.2M clinical budget request. California audit: $592M of "vacancy savings" from unfilled health posts over six years, and no tracking of missed minimum staffing. Wellpath said it had 40% annual turnover in Georgia prisons.
- (S) California mental health: a federal court put CDCR mental health under a Receiver effective 2025-09-01 (Collette Peters); mental-health vacancy rate was 29% in April 2024; the LAO (Feb 2026) recommends letting out-of-state tele-mental-health providers work in prisons; $100M+ of accumulated fines is being used for clinician bonuses and supplies.
- (S) Reentry money: 19 states have approved reentry 1115 waivers (KFF). California's first year (10/2024-9/2025): 42,000+ people eligible, 160,554 claims paid, only $16M reimbursed to correctional facilities; all CA facilities go live by 2026-10-01. In a Wisconsin program Medicaid coverage in the month of release rose from 39% to 69%. A Connecticut study found enhanced primary care for people recently released was cost-neutral for Medicaid but saved the justice system money; monthly Medicaid cost was $1,356-$1,737 per person.
- (S) Medicaid inpatient exception (hospital stays of 24 hours or more can draw federal match for incarcerated people): reported savings for FY2014-15 were $2.8M (AR), $10M (CO), $16.4M (KY), $19M (MI).
- (S) Hepatitis C: states treated under 1% of known infected inmates in 2015 (KFF Health News); Kentucky 159 of 1,670 identified; New Mexico 123 of about 2,800 diagnosed (2019); a ~$24,000 course in 2022, below $20,000 per patient in Colorado's 2024 settlement; the Cure Hepatitis C Act of 2026 (HR 9682) is pending, not law.
- (S) Competency restoration: Texas federal injunction (July 2026) orders admission of jailed incompetent defendants within 21 days, four years to comply; about 1,700 were waiting an average six months at trial; HHSC already contracts an average of 747 private beds a day and 292 of its 2,770 beds were offline. Colorado fines $100-500/day past 28 days; FY24 paid $12M (the cap) against $65.2M uncapped; requests 94 new beds. Washington: $400M in fines issued, 100M+ paid, a new 350-bed forensic hospital not due until 2028. Oklahoma: $928,400 in one month of fines. Jail-based programs have run $278/day (CA, 2012) to roughly $380/bed-day (Placer 15 beds, A) against about $500/day for a state hospital bed.
- (S) MOUD: about 30% of the nation's 3,535 jails provide buprenorphine and 20% methadone (JCOIN). Opioid settlement terms list correctional MOUD as an approved use; some West Virginia counties used settlement funds to pay jail bills instead.

## 1. Custody Mutual (insider)

**Persona:** county risk manager and public-entity pool claims director, 15+ years, who has paid the last 20 jail-death claims.

**One-liner:** An insurance program for jails (an MGA with fronted paper and reinsurance) that prices and discounts coverage using a handful of clinical-process indicators from the medical vendor's record, and that also covers the county when a bankrupt vendor's indemnity turns out to be worthless.

- **Customer:** county governments and sheriffs; public-entity risk pools. About 3,000+ county jails (the JCOIN survey counts 3,535 jails).
- **Payer and signer:** the liability line in the county risk-management budget, or the pool's annual premium. Signer: county risk manager or administrator plus the board; for pools, the executive director and board.
- **Problem:** Jail-death suits settle for $2.5M to $20M. Cover is shrinking (rates up as much as 300% in four years in one Oklahoma sheriff's account (S); a pool dropping the jail (S); vendor limits cut from $10M to $8M (S)). A vendor's indemnity is a promise from the party most likely to be insolvent exactly when the claim lands: Lane County had to file a claim in Wellpath's bankruptcy (S). And counties cannot see the clinical data that predicts losses, because it sits in the vendor's EHR.
- **Solution:** (a) Excess and contractual-liability layers that respond when the vendor cannot pay; (b) price keyed to a short list of process indicators (time to receiving screen, withdrawal scoring, sick-call response time, medication-administration omissions for chronic meds, provider escalation, continuity of community meds, cell-check logs), fed by the vendor or county EHR under contract language the county requires; (c) monthly dashboards and quarterly nurse audits as loss control; (d) premium credits for data sharing and for hitting thresholds.
- **Why now:** (S) Wellpath Chapter 11 and YesCare's liquidation have just proved the indemnity gap in public; pools are excluding jails; verdicts are rising; NaphCare's TechCare is becoming a procurable standard (U: a NASPO vehicle opens Sept 16, 2026).
- **Wedge and first 10 customers:** "Indemnity-gap excess" for counties tied to Wellpath or YesCare (Lane OR has an open death suit and a bankruptcy claim; Orange NY, Polk FL, Fayette and Louisville KY were YesCare customers), plus a free "clinical autopsy" of five years of closed claims for two or three state county pools. First ten: two or three pools and six or seven counties of 500-2,000 average daily population with at least $2M in jail losses.
- **Path to $100M:** (A) 1,200 jail deaths a year x 30-40% producing a claim x $2-3M all-in = $0.7-1.4B; add 50% for non-fatal neglect and injury claims = $1.1-2.2B of annual loss cost; at a 65-70% loss ratio that is $1.6-3.3B of premium-equivalent, of which perhaps 40-60% is insurable rather than retained by self-insured counties, so $0.6-2.0B. $100M of gross written premium is 5-15% share by year five or six. This is the least certain sizing in the file; the first-90-day test is to pull pool loss runs.
- **Who does it today:** county risk pools (for example Maine's county Risk Pool charges Somerset about $220k a year for liability, S); excess and surplus placements marketed by wholesalers (S, marketing pages only); the vendors' own professional-liability carriers (narrowing, S); brokers. Funded startups: none found for jail liability specifically (searched; I may have missed one). Free substitute: self-insurance by taxpayers.
- **Why they lose:** pools are consensus-governed and price by headcount and claims history, so they cannot differentiate between a good and a bad medical vendor; carriers have no clinical data and exit after a bad year; vendors cannot hand over data that would be used against them in discovery.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** cornered resource (the only dataset linking process indicators to claim outcomes across vendors) plus switching costs (data feeds written into contracts, premium credits), with scale economies in reinsurance later.
- **Earned secret:** the large losses are not random. A short list of process failures shows up in timestamps days before the death, and the county never sees it because the vendor holds the record. The insurer that can see it can price it.
- **First 90 days:** sign two pools or counties as design partners; run the clinical autopsy on 50 closed claims and check whether the indicators separate bad from average outcomes; pick a fronting carrier and a reinsurer for the indemnity-gap layer; draft the data-feed rider; get counsel on structuring the analysis as privileged patient-safety work product (U: a Patient Safety Organization under PSQIA).
- **Legal premises to check:** compensatory damages for constitutional claims are generally insurable but punitive damages are not; HIPAA permits disclosure for health care operations and legal and risk review under a business-associate agreement, but verify; a data feed that shows the county knew of failures can become evidence of deliberate indifference if nobody acts, which is why the privilege structure and a county action protocol are part of the product, not an afterthought; state MGA and fronting rules.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 6.

## 2. Outside Care Plan (insider)

**Persona:** state DOC health services director or CFO, 15+ years, who has defended the "outside medical" line to three legislatures.

**One-liner:** A risk-bearing benefit manager that takes all off-site care (hospital, emergency, specialty, dialysis, oncology) for a state DOC or large jail system at a fixed per-member-per-month price, with referral-timeline guarantees, independent utilization review, Medicaid match capture and case management for the sickest 1-3%.

- **Customer:** state DOCs (36 outsource at least part, 14 are in-house per the pool file, U) and the largest county systems.
- **Payer and signer:** the DOC's off-site or "outside medical" line, or the pass-through portion of a capitated vendor's contract. Signer: DOC commissioner or CFO with the health services director; legislature approves.
- **Problem:** Off-site care is 20-27% of prison health budgets (S). It is paid at near-chargemaster rates because the DOC has no network leverage (NCCHC, S). With pure capitation, the on-site vendor is both gatekeeper and payer, so delayed referrals are its profit and its lawsuit (Pew, S). Hospitals are now unpaid by failed vendors (750+ Georgia providers claiming $75.6M, S), so access itself is degrading. The Medicaid inpatient exception is under-claimed. People over 60 are 14% of California's prison population and 27% of its health spend (U, advocacy summary of CCHCS data).
- **Solution:** a per-member-per-month carve-out contract with (1) Medicare-based network rates (federal precedent: the Marshals Service re-prices claims against Medicare, S 2004); (2) independent utilization review with published timelines (specialist in 14 or 30 days), so the price comes from rates and not denial; (3) eConsult to avoid trips (a jail study found 20% of specialty cases handled on-site, S); (4) Medicaid eligibility and inpatient claims for expansion adults; (5) case management for the top 1-3% (dialysis, cancer, cirrhosis, dementia); (6) 15-day hospital payment, which is what buys the discount.
- **Why now:** vendor failure has turned off-site access into a visible crisis; emergency procurements show states will restructure fast; Safety Net Connect's July 2025 expansion shows the category waking up (S).
- **Wedge and first 10 customers:** a shadow bid in one or two states with in-house or lightly capitated health and Medicaid expansion; also county systems with their own hospital contracts. First ten: three states and seven large counties.
- **Path to $100M:** (A) off-site is about 20-27% of $11B = $2.2-3.0B for state prisons, plus perhaps $1B for jails. $100M of premium needs about $125M of today's off-site spend, which is one big state (North Carolina's whole prison health budget was $400M in FY2024, S) or three or four mid-size states. About 12-15 states have off-site lines that large (A). Premium is gross; expected margin 5-8% (A) before reinsurance cost.
- **Who does it today:** Prime Health Services (shared-savings repricing for county sheriffs; claims $88M of cumulative documented savings, no risk taken, S marketing); Florida Sheriffs Association's inmate medical cost program; CorrectCare Integrated Health (jail claims payer, S directory); HealthSmart (TPA); NaphCare (off-site management for the BOP); Safety Net Connect (eConsult); the big vendors' own networks; Centurion's parent Centene, a Medicaid health plan. Repricing alone is small: $88M of savings since about 2011 implies tens of millions of fees at most (A), which is why this idea must take risk and guarantee access.
- **Why they lose:** repricers are paid only on savings and promise nothing on access; the on-site vendor is paid more when referrals are delayed; our per-member price is paid regardless of how many referrals are approved.
- **Moat:** scale economies (hospital contracts improve with volume and term), counter-positioning (incumbent vendors cannot give up gatekeeping), process power (utilization review plus case management).
- **Earned secret:** hospitals will take Medicare-plus rates only if they are paid in 15 days and can count on stable volume for five to ten years. A three-year capitated vendor can promise neither, so the DOC pays chargemaster. And the lawsuits come from the delayed referral, not from the price.
- **First 90 days:** public-records request for three years of off-site claims in three states; reprice at 140% of Medicare to size the gap; get one hospital system to sign a letter of intent; get a stop-loss quote from a reinsurer and a sizing from an actuary; present shadow bids to DOC finance directors.
- **Legal premises to check:** a government per-member-per-month service contract is usually a vendor contract, not insurance, but some states may require a health-plan or TPA license; state rate-cap statutes for inmate care (U: roughly a dozen states, unverified); the inpatient exception needs a 24-hour-plus admission to a medical institution and an active Medicaid file.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 5.

## 3. Clinical Corps (pattern: instant-pay spot labor x expert-labor network)

**Pattern transferred:** Timee's instant-pay spot-labor marketplace for a shortage economy and Mercor's vetted-expert network for a buyer with an urgent budget. **Why it has not happened here:** in a prison the scarce thing is not a licensed nurse, it is a cleared one. Background check, key control, PREA and security orientation take 4-8 weeks (A) per facility system, which kills any shift-by-shift gig model, and vendors recruit in-house.

**One-liner:** A W-2 pool of nurses and tele-psychiatry clinicians who hold standing clearance and correctional training for a state's facilities, dispatched in 24-72 hours, billed per verified hour with a fill-or-credit guarantee, replacing registry and agency nursing.

- **Customer:** state DOCs with in-house health (North Carolina, South Dakota), the California mental health Receiver and the CCHCS (the physical-health side), plus vendors that subcontract overflow.
- **Payer and signer:** agency and contract nursing lines. Signer: DOC deputy secretary for health or CFO; the Receiver's staff; the vendor's VP of operations.
- **Problem:** vacancy 44% (NC), 53% LPN and 40% RN (SD), 29% mental health (CA, April 2024), turnover 40% (Wellpath in Georgia). Contract nurses cost $64/hr against $25.40 for state staff (S). California counted $592M of savings from empty posts and does not track missed shift minimums (S).
- **Solution:** recruit (including the displaced Wellpath and YesCare workforce: 119 laid off in Shelby, 100+ unpaid in Fayette), clear and train them once per state, pay above state scale, and rotate them across five to twenty facilities. Add a virtual nursing and tele-psychiatry layer under Nurse Licensure Compact and state telehealth rules. Hours are verified by badge, so the buyer pays for filled shifts only.
- **Why now:** published vacancy crises; a court Receiver with authority to contract outside civil-service pay scales (S); the LAO recommending out-of-state tele-mental-health (S); thousands of experienced correctional nurses suddenly between employers.
- **Wedge and first 10 customers:** two states with published crises (North Carolina, South Dakota) for a pilot of 10 FTE-equivalents at $54/hr against $64; then the Receiver's tele-psychiatry gap in California; then vendors' overflow. First ten: five DOCs, the Receiver, four vendors.
- **Path to $100M:** (A) if agency and contract labor is 8-12% of state-prison health budgets (South Dakota's travel-nurse spend is 12% of its clinical budget request, S) that is $0.9-1.3B a year, plus jails and vendor overflow. $100M of revenue is 8-11% of the state-prison slice, for example 15-20 states at $5-7M. Gross margin typical of staffing, 20-25% (A).
- **Who does it today:** AMN, Cross Country, Aya and other travel-nurse firms (U on corrections share); the vendors' own recruiters; per-diem marketplaces such as ShiftKey and Clipboard Health (U on corrections); Sourcewell has a staffing contract that lists clinical staffing (S); CFG's CareLink telehealth (S, Feb 2026); overtime and state float pools as the free substitute.
- **Why they lose:** generalist agencies will not pre-clear and train for each state; vendors cannot pay above their per-diem; states are held by civil-service pay scales and pay 2.5x through agencies instead.
- **Moat (weak):** switching costs and process power from standing clearances and a correctional-ready credential; scale economies in credentialing. A competitor can copy it with 6-12 months of work.
- **Earned secret:** the scarce asset is a cleared, trained, willing correctional nurse, not a licensed one. Whoever holds the pool of already-cleared nurses owns the only scalable supply, and states lose a month every time they onboard.
- **First 90 days:** clear 40 nurses in one state; sign a pilot priced per verified hour; get compact-state licensure sorted; call the Receiver's office and the LAO analyst about tele-mental-health pilots.
- **Legal premises to check:** W-2 employment (no 1099 games); state staffing-agency licensing; union and civil-service rules on contracting out; security-clearance reciprocity.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 3. This is the file's most obvious idea and probably a feature of ideas 5 and 8.

## 4. Piggyback Prime (pattern: wrapper on pass-through flows, the Carahsoft vehicle)

**Pattern transferred:** Carahsoft sells nothing it makes; it holds the contract vehicles that agencies can buy from without a new RFP, carries compliance, and subcontracts delivery. **Why it has not happened here:** jail health is bought by sheriffs one county at a time through bespoke RFPs, and the operators are competitors, not subcontractors. My search found cooperative contracts covering jail supplies (NASPO, OMNIA), corrections products (Sourcewell), generic clinical staffing (Sourcewell 061324-INF) and an EHR (NaphCare TechCare, usable from Sept 16, 2026), but none for jail medical services (S as a search result; the search might have missed one).

**One-liner:** Win a cooperative-purchasing master agreement (or a large state contract with a political-subdivision piggyback clause) for jail medical services, then let any county join in days, with uniform quality standards, a pooled insurance and indemnity backstop, and regional operators or public hospitals delivering the care.

- **Customer:** counties and states that need a vendor fast: Georgia (2024) and Alabama (2026) both sole-sourced in emergencies, and a Fulton County judge had to dismiss a losing vendor's challenge to Georgia's emergency procurement (S). Mid-size counties with no bidders.
- **Payer and signer:** the county jail-medical line. Signer: county administrator and sheriff, via a participating addendum.
- **Problem:** replacement takes 9-12 months of RFP (A); failure takes days; counties with one bidder have no leverage; vendors with thin balance sheets cannot back their indemnities.
- **Solution:** the prime holds the master agreement, a rate card, SLAs and the insurance (through a captive or idea 1's program); it subcontracts delivery to regional operators (CFG, Turn Key, Mediko and similar, U), public hospital systems, or Clinical Corps clinicians; counties get one accountable party and a switching clause.
- **Why now:** the failure wave of 2024-26; the TechCare NASPO award shows buyers will use cooperative vehicles in this sector (S).
- **Wedge and first 10 customers:** win one lead-agency award or a state contract with a piggyback clause; then recruit counties with expiring contracts. First ten: a lead agency and nine counties.
- **Path to $100M:** (A) gross: 6-10 mid-size county contracts at $10-19M each (Placer, Monterey scale), or one mid-size state. Net: a prime keeps perhaps 8-12%, so $100M of gross is $8-12M of net revenue. $100M of net would need roughly $1B of gross, about 20% of an estimated $5B jail pool: heroic. Treat this as a $100M-gross business only.
- **Who does it today:** nobody found for care delivery. NaphCare holds the EHR vehicle and could extend it; Sourcewell's staffing vehicle is a partial substitute; counties' bespoke RFPs are the incumbent process.
- **Why they lose:** operators will not subcontract to a rival; cooperatives like clean product catalogs and avoid professional-services liability; sheriffs fear losing local control.
- **Moat:** cornered resource (the master agreement itself, which takes a competitive solicitation to obtain) plus scale economies in pooled risk. Thin.
- **Earned secret:** emergency procurements are the real purchasing event in this industry, and they go to whoever is already on paper with ready capacity, not to the best bidder.
- **First 90 days:** read the cooperative portals' solicitation calendars; ask Sourcewell, NASPO and OMNIA whether a services solicitation exists or is planned; draft a rate card from published awards (Placer, Monterey, Maryland); sign two regional operators as conditional subcontractors.
- **Legal premises to check:** whether each state's procurement code allows piggybacking for professional and clinical services, not just goods; licensure of the prime where it does not itself deliver care; sheriff and county charter limits.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 4.

## 5. Day 91 (dilemma: reentry care that starts inside)

**What incumbents structurally cannot do:** the custody-health vendors are paid by the inmate-day inside the walls, so they have no reason to care what happens on day 91; Medicaid plans cannot get inside, and do not know a release date until the person shows up in an emergency room; community providers have no security clearance and no way to bill.

**One-liner:** A reentry care organization that works inside facilities for the 90 days before release (paid by Medicaid under 1115 waivers), then holds a per-member contract with Medicaid plans for the first year after release, with a medical, behavioral and community-health-worker team.

- **Customer:** state Medicaid agencies and Medicaid plans (Centene, Molina and others); DOCs and jails are the access partners, not the payer.
- **Payer and signer:** the 1115 pre-release benefit and targeted case management (state Medicaid director); the plan's complex-care or enhanced-care-management budget (plan VP of complex care). Warning from round 1's wrong-payer lesson: the Connecticut study found the savings landed in the justice budget, not Medicaid (S), so the contract has to be paid by the Medicaid benefit itself or share state justice savings, not rely on plan savings.
- **Problem:** Medicaid coverage in the month of release was 39% before a Wisconsin enrollment program (S). Post-release months are the highest-risk period for overdose and death (U here; well known in the literature). Medicaid per-person cost for people recently released with a chronic condition ran $1,356-$1,737 a month (S).
- **Solution:** in-reach teams (a community health worker with lived experience, a nurse, and tele-prescribing) inside facilities; eligibility reactivation; a 30-day medication bridge in hand at the gate; MOUD and long-acting injectable continuity; appointment within seven days; a 12-month community care team; data feed of release dates from the DOC.
- **Why now:** (S) 19 states have approved reentry waivers; California is live statewide by Oct 1, 2026; Washington runs it through plans; New Mexico's Just Health Plus pilot started July 2025 and Louisiana treats plans as central. This is the first time Medicaid has paid for anything inside custody except hospital stays.
- **Wedge and first 10 customers:** New Mexico, Washington or Louisiana, where plans are central; start with one plan plus one DOC or large jail. First ten: two plans, three DOCs, five large jails.
- **Path to $100M:** the pre-release benefit alone is too small: California reimbursed $16M in its first year with 42,000 eligible people (S), so (A) even 19 states may reach only a few hundred million at steady state. The business must reach post-release. (A) Care management at $200-300 per member per month for 12 months is about $3,000 a person: 33,000 members a year is $100M, about 7% of an estimated 500,000 high-need releases a year in expansion states (state prison releases roughly 400,000 plus a share of millions of jail releases; high-need share assumed 15%). Under full risk at $16-20k a member-year, about 5,000-6,000 members.
- **Who does it today:** Wellpath, NaphCare, CFG building reentry offerings; Transitions Clinic Network (nonprofit); Safety Net Connect's Collaborate platform; HealthTap with NaphCare for halfway-house patients (S); Reconnect ($3.3M raised, U) and Total Reentry Solutions (pre-seed, U); plans' in-house teams. My search did not confirm any reentry work by Cityblock, Waymark or Pair Team.
- **Why they lose:** none of them combines facility access and security clearance, Medicaid billing and a risk contract. Vendors are paid per in-custody day; plans are blind to release dates; community organizations are non-clinical and cannot bill.
- **Moat:** cornered resource (facility access agreements and the release-date data feed) plus process power (an in-reach protocol that improves with every cohort) and switching costs with DOCs.
- **Earned secret:** the DOC knows the release date months ahead; the plan finds out in the emergency room. Whoever owns the release-date pipe owns the cohort.
- **First 90 days:** pick one state; sit down with the Medicaid agency and one plan; get a data-sharing agreement for 24 months of release and claims history; price a PMPM from real claims; find a DOC or jail willing to host a 50-person pilot.
- **Risks and legal premises:** federal Medicaid cuts and work requirements starting 2027 (U on how recent releasees are treated); waivers can be rescinded; the Medicaid inmate exclusion remains outside the waiver window.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 6.

## 6. Cure Carve-Out (dilemma: the per-diem cannot hold a $20,000 cure)

**What incumbents structurally cannot do:** a capitated vendor pays each hepatitis C cure ($12-24k), each month of long-acting buprenorphine (list around $1,800, U) and each HIV regimen out of its own margin, so it rations; courts mostly side with the patients (S). It cannot become a pass-through without giving up the margin on those drugs.

**One-liner:** Take hepatitis C cure, HIV, long-acting MOUD and later GLP-1 drugs out of the per-diem and run them as a funded program (testing, treatment, pharmacy, cure verification, release continuity), paid by the state per cure or per patient-month with drugs at cost.

- **Customer:** state DOCs and big jails, especially those under settlements or consent decrees (Colorado, Indiana, Kentucky and others).
- **Payer and signer:** a separate pharmaceutical or program line. Signer: DOC medical director and CFO; the legislature (HR 9682 would add federal grants and subscription purchasing but is not law, S).
- **Problem:** under 1% of known infected inmates were treated in 2015; Kentucky treated 159 of 1,670; New Mexico 123 of about 2,800; hundreds untreated in Mississippi under a $357M three-year contract (S). Opt-out testing at intake is rare.
- **Solution:** opt-out testing, test-and-treat, drug supply through a 340B partner or manufacturer subscription (Louisiana and Washington used subscriptions, U), adherence, cure verification, and handoff at release; same chassis later for HIV and long-acting MOUD.
- **Why now:** courts have kept ruling for patients; prices fell to roughly $20k or less; pending federal legislation; opioid settlement dollars are earmarked for correctional MOUD (S).
- **Wedge and first 10 customers:** a state under a hepatitis C settlement. First ten: four such states and six large jails.
- **Path to $100M:** (A) if 100,000-170,000 prisoners are infected (KFF Health News cites 1 in 7, S; I did not verify the base) at $12-20k net, that is $1.2-3.4B one-time, or $250-700M a year over five years. HIV (15,000-20,000 patients at about $30k) adds $450-600M a year (A) and long-acting MOUD at 10% of about 200,000 people with OUD adds about $420M a year (A). $100M of gross is 6-10% of the roughly $1-1.7B a year of drug flow, but the operator's net fee would be 6-10% of that. Honest verdict: mostly pass-through, a finite wave for hepatitis C, probably a feature of idea 2.
- **Who does it today:** the vendors' own pharmacies and PBM relationships (I could not verify ownership or markups); manufacturer-direct deals (U); state programs that treat in-house. No funded startup found.
- **Why they lose:** each cure costs them the margin on several hundred patient-months.
- **Moat (weak):** scale in subscription and 340B purchasing, counter-positioning. Replaceable by the vendors once a state forces a pass-through clause.
- **Earned secret:** the litigation exposure from rationing a $20k cure exceeds the cure, and the capitation contract hides that from both sides until a judge shows up.
- **First 90 days:** read the Colorado, Indiana and Kentucky orders; price a test-and-treat program per cure; line up a 340B hospital partner and a pharmacy license; ask Washington and Louisiana for the subscription terms.
- **Legal premises to check:** HRSA's 340B patient-definition rules for people in custody; state pharmacy licensure; the drug subscription terms.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 3.

## 7. Day 21 (shock: forensic capacity for court-ordered competency restoration)

**The shock:** a July 2026 federal injunction orders Texas to admit jailed incompetent defendants within 21 days (four years to comply), on top of fines or consent decrees in Colorado, Oklahoma and Washington (S). The people waiting are the sickest in a county jail, and the county pays the cost while the state pays the fines.

**One-liner:** Secure psychiatric capacity that can come online in 6-12 months: jail-based restoration units inside county jails and modular or converted licensed units, paid per bed-day by the state at 30-45% below a state hospital bed, with an outpatient track for low-risk defendants.

- **Customer:** state health agencies (Texas HHSC, Colorado CDHS, Washington DSHS, Oklahoma ODMHSAS); host counties (Dallas County sheriff has sued the state over 300+ detainees waiting more than 45 days, S).
- **Payer and signer:** the state's forensic or private-bed contract line. Signer: HHSC deputy executive commissioner for state hospitals, or the equivalent.
- **Problem:** about 1,700 people waited an average of six months in Texas jails (S); Texas HHSC already contracts 747 private beds a day and 292 state beds were offline (S); Colorado's uncapped fines would have been $65.2M in FY24 (S); Washington's new hospital is not due until 2028 (S).
- **Solution:** license-ready modular units and jail-based programs, staffed by Clinical Corps clinicians, with a state contract at $380-450 per bed-day (A), against about $500 for a state hospital bed (S, 2012). Fines of $100-500 a day per waiting person (S) mean a bed pays for itself the moment it opens.
- **Why now:** the injunction, the Colorado decree expiring in 2027, and the fact that the state's own build timelines are years long.
- **Wedge and first 10 customers:** a 40-bed jail-based unit in a Texas county that is already suing the state, then a second Texas county, then Colorado's 94-bed request. First ten: three states and seven counties.
- **Path to $100M:** (A) $100M is about 600 beds at $450 a day. Texas alone needs perhaps 600-1,000 more beds than the state is building (my estimate; no HHSC number was published in what I found) worth $100-165M a year. Colorado's 94 beds are about $15M, Washington's 350-bed need about $55-60M if any is contracted. Nationally perhaps 3,000-4,000 unmet beds (A), so $100M is 15-20% share.
- **Who does it today (hidden incumbents):** Texas's own private-bed vendors (unnamed in my sources); Wellpath Recovery Solutions and its Early Access and Stabilization program with California DSH (S); Liberty Healthcare (San Bernardino jail-based program, 96 beds, $17.98M over three years, S); GEO Care, Universal Health Services and Acadia (U). Expect incumbents to bid.
- **Why they lose:** hospital builders take years and use civil-service staff; incumbents' beds are tied up; a 6-12 month deployable unit beats a 2028 hospital.
- **Moat:** cornered resource (licensed and permitted beds, county site agreements) with some switching costs on three- to five-year contracts. This is the thinnest moat among the shock ideas.
- **Earned secret:** the sickest 5% of a jail, people waiting 200+ days for a state bed, consume most of its one-on-one watches, medical emergencies and lawsuits. Nobody owns that problem: the county pays for the jail, the state pays the fine.
- **First 90 days:** talk to the Dallas County sheriff's office; read the injunction's benchmark plan; pre-file for a state license for a 40-bed unit; get a staffing commitment from a Clinical Corps pilot.
- **Legal premises to check:** state psychiatric hospital licensure and certificate-of-need rules; HHSC's authority to contract beds on a sole-source basis; county consent for jail-based programs; the Prison Litigation Reform Act (the judge ruled it did not apply, S).
- **Outside-the-box score:** 4.

## 8. Open-Book Health Authority (shock: counties that lost their vendor)

**The shock:** vendor collapse has produced a new buyer behavior. Barnstable County MA moved in-house in 2023 after the vendor could not meet staffing (S); Wellpath is leaving Shelby County (U); Monterey and Placer switched vendors in 2025-26; YesCare's customers scrambled (S). Counties now want control and visibility, not another black-box per-diem.

**One-liner:** A fee-only management company that runs jail health as a county or public-hospital health authority: the county is the employer and licensee, the company supplies the leadership team, recruiting backfill, pharmacy purchasing, EHR, telehealth, quality dashboards and Medicaid billing, and all costs are open-book.

- **Customer:** counties and sheriffs with 300+ average daily population that have lost or want to leave a vendor; public hospital districts and community health centers that want to run it but lack correctional know-how.
- **Payer and signer:** the jail-medical line. Signer: county executive or administrator with the sheriff and board.
- **Problem:** a fixed per-diem pays the vendor for vacancies and denials; the county cannot verify staffing; failures show up as emergency procurements.
- **Solution:** a management agreement: fixed fee plus a capped at-risk portion tied to measured outcomes (access times, medication continuity, deaths per 1,000, vacancy), pass-through costs, and savings kept by the county. Clinicians are county or hospital employees, which also helps with pensions, Medicaid and 340B eligibility (U).
- **Why now:** (S) Wellpath's restructuring and exits, YesCare's liquidation, Barnstable's in-house move; (U) NCCHC and DOJ pressure; the failures have put "who actually staffs the jail" on every county agenda.
- **Wedge and first 10 customers:** an emergency management bridge (60-90 days) for a county whose vendor just failed, converting to a three-year agreement; Clinical Corps provides the backfill. First ten: five bridge customers and five conversions.
- **Path to $100M:** (A) gross, including employer-of-record payroll: 6-10 county jails at $10-19M each (Placer $18.7M/yr, Monterey $14.8M/yr) is $100-180M. Net management fee is 6-10% of that ($8-15M). $100M of net would need the platform to add software and data revenue (idea 1's data, idea 2's claims) or a state contract.
- **Who does it today:** hospital-run jail health systems (Cook County Health, NYC H+H and similar, U); academic partners (UTMB and others, U); county in-house teams; consultants and RFP advisers; vendors on an ASO basis (U, none found). Barnstable's in-house move is the precedent (S).
- **Why they lose:** the incumbents' profit is the spread between the per-diem and the cost (vacancies, denials, fewer hospital trips), so they cannot sell management without selling that spread. Public hospitals cannot scale across counties.
- **Moat:** counter-positioning plus switching costs (the county's records and processes move onto our platform); weak on scale.
- **Earned secret:** a jail's biggest health costs are the ones the contract does not show: vacancy-driven agency labor, deputies on hospital watch at overtime, and liability. The vendor profits from the first, the sheriff pays for the other two.
- **First 90 days:** build a one-page open-book fee proposal from Placer and Monterey published contract values; interview the Barnstable sheriff and finance team; line up two public hospital partners; check the county employment and pension law in two target states.
- **Legal premises to check:** state law on county employment and contracting for medical care; sovereign-immunity and indemnity terms for the company; Medicaid and 340B eligibility of a county-employed clinic (U).
- **Outside-the-box score:** 5.

## Considered and killed (so the next reader does not redo them)

- **Reentry billing rail on its own:** California's biggest-in-nation program reimbursed $16M in year one. Even 19 states at a fraction of that is a few hundred million of gross claims; a 10% fee is not $100M. It is folded into idea 5 as the entry, not the business.
- **Medicaid inpatient-exception claims recovery on its own:** reported state savings are $2.8M to $19M each (S); a nationwide pool of perhaps $200-500M (A) at a 12-15% fee is $25-75M. Folded into idea 2.
- **Off-site repricing for jails on its own:** Prime Health reports $88M of cumulative documented savings on a no-savings-no-fee basis (S). That is a small fee pool. Only the risk-bearing version (idea 2) survives.
- **Medical-release placement network for older prisoners (federal Medicaid match for community nursing care):** the economics are attractive (CA cost per person by age $87k-$237k, U) but the binding constraint is parole law and politics, not beds. California found only about 60 placements, Wisconsin approves roughly eight petitions a year (S). Dead unless a law changes.
- **Booking-dock medical clearance hub:** the pool (maybe $100-160M at $250 a clearance, A) would need 65%+ share.
- **Neutral jail EHR / data layer:** TechCare is already in 220+ facilities and now has a NASPO vehicle (S); a standalone EHR is a $100-200M market at best (A).
- **Pharmacy GPO or pass-through PBM for corrections:** I could not find evidence of captive-pharmacy spreads in the large vendors, and a 3% GPO fee on about $1B is $30M.
- **ICE and other federal immigration detention health:** excluded for the political and reputational gate and because I did not research it.
- **Distressed roll-up of contract books:** H.I.G. (Wellpath) and Flacks (YesCare) already ran this play and it ended in bankruptcy; no sale process found. Kept as a possible scale path for idea 8, not an idea.
