# P137 US graduate-school borrowing displaced from Grad PLUS: 8 ideas (round 2)

Written 2026-10-18. About 28 searches and fetches (the brief said 8-15; I went over because the first answers kept turning up hidden incumbents: Ascent, Clasp, Juno, Sallie Mae-KKR). The NBER paper was read in full from a locally extracted PDF (sections IV.A-D). Everything else comes from search summaries, so it is flagged [S] where it is a secondary summary, [M] where it is from memory or my own assumption.

## Pool facts used

- **Size.** NBER WP 35683 (Turner, Aug 2026): the gap is about $8B a year across about 370,000 borrowers, so about $21,600 per borrower per year (my division). Current annual private graduate origination is only about $1.4B, so the market must grow 6-7x. Sallie Mae holds about 64% of all private student lending and originated $7.4B in 2025. Wells Fargo and Discover have left the business.
- **Capital.** Student loans carry a 100% bank risk weight (about 10.5% capital vs 50% for mortgages). Student-loan ABS issues only $6.5-9.3B a year, nearly all of it refinancing. Investors want performance data on the specific underwriting model, and lenders can only get that data by originating at scale. The paper says the fix (standard data reporting, pilots with built-in evaluation, early credit enhancement) is "undefined".
- **Where the gap sits.** About 80% of dentistry and 62% of medical borrowers exceed their cap (medians $115-173k four years out). Physical therapy, nursing and counseling psychology show flat earnings gradients. The paper gives them $3.2B, $2.9B and $1.6B of "displaced volume". Those three sum to $7.7B of the $8B total, so the paper's definitions do not reconcile in the text I read; treat the field figures as indicative only.
- **The gap may be smaller than $8B right now.** The $8B was computed under ED's narrow 11-field "professional degree" list. After Judge Howell's late-June stay, ED's interim list (GENERAL-26-42, 29 Jun, updated 10 Jul) treats DPT, OT, MSN/DNP, nurse anesthesia, PA, SLP, audiology and clinical/counseling psychology as professional ($50k a year, $200k aggregate) while the stay lasts. Social work and education are still capped at $20,500. MBA is not professional either. A separate suit in D. Mass. is pending. I could not find any news after mid-August, so the status is unverified. [S]
- **The ECOA barrier moved.** CFPB finalized a Regulation B rule on 22 Apr 2026 (effective 21 Jul 2026) that removes the disparate-impact "effects test" and says ECOA does not authorize disparate-impact liability. The NBER paper (Aug 2026) still says the statute is unchanged and state attorneys general and private plaintiffs keep their power; Earnest paid the Massachusetts AG $2.5M over an underwriting model that used cohort default rates. So program-level pricing is easier federally, not safe. [S]
- **Three school models** (NBER): an interest-rate buydown (school pays a per-loan fee for a program-wide rate), a risk-share (school absorbs part of defaults, sometimes via funded escrow), and lender of last resort (school pays an origination fee on dollars lent to otherwise-denied students). The paper says the legal and administrative costs are "non-trivial". Kansas Law (J-HELPS, 5%) and WashU Law (7.5%) lend from endowment. Perkins (1958-2017) ran through 1,500+ institutions at up to $2.8B a year. HEOA's code of conduct requires preferred-lender lists to have at least three unaffiliated lenders, disclosed criteria and rotating order. Harvard's 2026-27 lender process said it would consider risk-sharing, and Harvard's summary says only a few lenders offered a competitive fixed rate to all students regardless of credit. [S]
- **Incumbents found (all of them matter below).**
  - Sallie Mae: Q2 2026 graduate originations +29% y/y; management says PLUS reform is worth a potential $4.5-5B of extra annual originations. In Nov 2025 it signed a deal under which KKR buys a seed portfolio and at least $2B of new loans a year for three years, with Sallie Mae keeping the customer and the servicing. About 30% of originations are sold. A second partner is "advancing". One third-party read puts Q1 2026 gain-on-sale at 4.4% vs 9.4% a year earlier. [S]
  - Ascent Funding: says it is the only lender doing rated school risk-share deals (for 8 years), reports 2,300+ institutional partnerships and 100+ graduate partners, and closed a $45M Series C in Feb 2026. [S]
  - Clasp: employer loan repayment tied to tenure, committed before graduation. $20M Series B in Jan 2026; customers have allocated more than $130M across thousands of programs; no in-school lending mentioned. [S]
  - Juno: group-negotiated private loans; claims 14-15% of private in-school graduate lending in 2025. GradBridge: $20M Series A, second-look lending, reported 17.07% APR start plus a 5% fee. MPOWER and Funding U. State programs: CHESLA (5.50-7.99% fixed), RISLA, Minnesota SELF, MEFA. [S]
  - Legacy books: Discover's $10.1B went to Carlyle/KKR; Wells Fargo's $10B went to Apollo/Blackstone. Nelnet's Firstmark services them. [S]
- **Earnings test** (ED, June 2026): graduate completers' median earnings four years out vs the median for working adults 25-34 with only a bachelor's. Determinations go to institutions in early 2027, the first award year affected is 2027-28, and the first possible loss of Direct Loans is award year 2028-29. One failure triggers a student warning; two failures in three years cost the program Direct Loans for at least two years. Appeals are allowed only on ED's calculation. ED expects about 4% of graduate programs to fail (mental and social health services is the one large field where most fall below the benchmark). One source says 3,302 programs overall could fail, about 40% of which would close. The ED fact sheet itself returned 403, so all of this is from search summaries. [S]
- **Employer law.** California AB 692 (effective 1 Jan 2026) and New York's Trapped at Work Act (signed 19 Dec 2025; a chapter amendment reportedly delays it to 19 Dec 2026) ban stay-or-pay terms but allow tuition repayment for a transferable credential if it is a separate document, prorated, not accelerated on separation, and owes nothing if the worker is terminated other than for misconduct. IRC section 127 lets employers pay up to $5,250 a year of an employee's student loans tax-free; OBBBA made that permanent. [S]
- **Healthcare staffing** (SIA via summaries): about $38.7B by end-2026; 2025 travel nurse $15.8B, allied $9.9B, locum $9.6B, per diem $5.2B. Top five firms hold 40.7% (Aya 16.3%, CHG 7.4%). [S]
- **90/10 rule.** Private education loans count as non-Title IV revenue for proprietary schools. Whether a school guarantee or risk-share changes that is unresolved in anything I found; read 34 CFR 668.28 and Appendix C before building anything for a proprietary operator. [S]
- **First Marblehead / TERI.** TERI (guarantor of First Marblehead's trusts) was downgraded on 26 Mar 2008, hit a collateral-posting trigger and filed Chapter 11 on 7 Apr 2008. The securitization window shut. The National Collegiate trusts later could not prove ownership of loans (court filings cite more than $5B uncollectable). [S]
- **My book arithmetic (used below).** A lender's revenue accrues on a book, not on one year's originations. At an assumed 10% blended gross yield, $100M of interest revenue needs about a $1.0B average balance. In-school balances do not amortize and interest capitalizes, so about $300M a year of originations sustained for 3-4 years builds that. $300M is about 3.75% of the $8B gap or 3.2% of the $9.4B combined market. That is a revenue test, not a profit test: at a 6-7% cost of funds and 2-3% losses the net spread is thin.

## What the research changed

- **Ascent already does school risk-share lending**, so my first instinct (a standard school-funded reserve program) is not whitespace. It survives only as the capital layer behind such programs (idea 2) and as the neutral buyer-side agent for schools (idea 1).
- **Reg B weakened the ECOA story** I had planned to lean on, so the "lenders cannot price programs" dilemma is now a state-law and durability problem, not a federal ban.
- **Sallie Mae-KKR closes the "private credit has money but no origination" pitch for Sallie Mae's own flow.** The gap that remains is for everyone else.
- **The interim professional list may remove much of the flat-earnings-field gap** while the stay holds. Ideas that depend on PT/OT/nursing (idea 3) are partly an option on the stay being lifted.

## 1. Gap Ledger (lens: insider, a graduate financial aid director with 17 years of packaging, plus a law-school CFO)

- **One-liner:** A cap-gap ledger for graduate deans that shows, for every admitted student, the federal headroom left and the exact buydown dollars needed to keep them, then lets the school buy one fixed per-loan fee for a program-wide fixed rate from a competing lender panel.
- **Customer:** Graduate and professional programs priced above the caps: MBA (non-professional tier, $20,500 a year), law, medicine, dentistry, veterinary and pharmacy (professional tier, hitting the $200,000 aggregate), later doctoral health programs.
- **Payer and signer:** The school's institutional aid and discount budget, plus the enrollment-software line. Signer: the associate dean for admissions and aid, with the school CFO signing off on accounting treatment.
- **Problem:** Nobody holds one number per admit. Headroom depends on the tier, part-time proration, grandfathering, a $257,500 lifetime cap and a professional list that changed on 29 Jun and again on 10 Jul. Lenders must negotiate across thousands of institutions and the paper calls the legal and admin cost of buydown and risk-share deals "non-trivial". A guarantee lands on the CFO as a contingent liability [M: ASC 460]; a fixed fee is a known cost.
- **Solution:** Step 1 is the ledger: SIS/CRM feed, a mapping of the ED list to CIP codes, student-consented NSLDS data, and a gap per admit by week. Step 2 is a points desk: the school sets a capped fee per funded loan, a standard contract and an annual rate-card process among at least three unaffiliated lenders (HEOA), with the tail risk placed with the Seasoning Factory (idea 2).
- **Why now:** The first full admit cycle under the caps runs November to January. Harvard's own process shows even the best-funded buyer found few lenders offering a flat fixed rate.
- **Wedge:** Law schools and MBA programs outside the top 20 (the paper says top programs already attract lenders because earnings rise steeply with borrowing). First 10: programs whose aid offices run a lender RFP this winter. I have no signed customers and name none.
- **Path to $100M:** Leg 1, software: about 1,000 programs (law, MBA, medicine, dental, veterinary, pharmacy, doctoral health) at $50k = $50M. That is most of the professional and MBA universe, so it is heroic on its own. Leg 2, the desk: schools buy buydowns on about $2B a year of loans at 4 points = $80M of school fees a year, of which we keep about 15% = $12M, plus interest on any paper we hold ($1B at 10% = $100M, via idea 2). Extending the ledger to the Parent PLUS cap ($20,000 a year, $65,000 per student) adds undergraduate-serving institutions. Honest read: software alone tops out near $50M; $100M needs the desk and a held book.
- **Who does it today:** Ascent (rated school risk-share, 100+ graduate partners), Sallie Mae/SoFi/Citizens school programs, Juno (student side), the SIS vendors (Ellucian, Workday, Anthology) that will ship cap logic [M], and enrollment consultancies (RNL, EAB) on the undergraduate side [M].
- **Why they lose:** Ascent and every lender are conflicted sellers, and HEOA forces schools to list at least three unaffiliated lenders, so a lender-owned tool cannot be the school's agent. SIS vendors ship compliance logic, not pricing, RFPs or contracts. Juno works for students. Nobody is the school's neutral buyer-side agent.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Switching costs (the ledger becomes the record of aid gap data) and counter-positioning against lender-owned tools.
- **Earned secret:** Aid directors do not need more lender options. They need one number: what it costs to keep each admit. A fixed, capped per-loan fee fits the discount budget; a guarantee does not fit the CFO.
- **First 90 days:** Build the ledger for three programs from live admit data. Map GENERAL-26-42 to CIP codes. Draft a standard buydown contract and a three-lender RFP. Run one RFP for one law school. Get one CFO's written accounting view.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 4.
- **Risks to kill it:** Ascent or SoFi ships the same tool; HEOA treatment of vendor compensation; SIS bundling; schools will not pay for software before they pay for loans.

## 2. Seasoning Factory (lens: insider, an ABS structurer with 18 years in student-loan securitization)

- **One-liner:** A bank-partnered, on-balance-sheet buyer of outcome-based graduate loans from many small originators on one tape standard, funded by insurer term notes and a first-loss slice, which builds the performance record ABS investors say they need.
- **Customer:** Originators that can underwrite but cannot fund (Ascent-type risk-share lenders, second-look lenders, credit-union platforms, state authorities), and the insurers and asset-based-finance desks that want student-loan collateral beyond Sallie Mae.
- **Payer and signer:** Originators pay through the loan price (they sell at an agreed discount or premium). Capital comes from an insurer chief investment officer or an ABF head. Signers: the originator's CFO or treasurer, and the capital partner's investment committee.
- **Problem:** The paper's capital constraint: 100% risk weight, a modest ABS market that mostly refinances, and a chicken-and-egg on data. Sallie Mae fixed its own problem with KKR, so smaller originators are left without a buyer. Ascent's $45M Series C cannot fund a billion-dollar book.
- **Solution:** The Factory is the buyer. It sets a standard tape and takes chain-of-title custody. It runs forward-flow from 8-10 originators, funds with insurer term notes matched to a 10-15 year asset, holds a first-loss slice (equity plus originator and school retained pieces), and publishes quarterly program-level performance. Later, seasoned pools go to public or 144A ABS. Program-level price differences live in the purchase grid (an investor's choice), not in the originator's credit decision. That is a legal premise: it needs counsel under Reg B and state law.
- **Why now:** The market must grow 6-7x in 12 months. Reg B just removed the federal disparate-impact test. KKR's deal shows ABF desks will pay for flow, and the Discover and Wells sales left four big private-credit houses with roughly $20B of legacy loans.
- **Wedge:** Forward-flow for school-sponsored risk-share loans and second-look loans, with a 10% first-loss slice. Targets (not customers): Ascent-like lenders, second-look lenders, two or three credit-union service organizations, one or two state authorities, two insurers.
- **Path to $100M:** About $300M a year of purchases for four years builds a $1.0B balance. At 10% gross yield that is $100M of consolidated interest revenue, from 3.2% of the $9.4B market. Capital: about $100-120M of equity and first loss plus about $0.9B of term notes. This is revenue, not profit; the profit is the excess spread after funding and losses.
- **Who does it today:** Sallie Mae-KKR (vertical, its own loans only), SoFi, Earnest and College Ave securitizing their own books, Carlyle/KKR and Apollo/Blackstone holding legacy books, Nelnet's Firstmark servicing. I found no multi-originator student-loan aggregator. First Marblehead is the dead precedent.
- **Why they lose:** Sallie Mae buys only its own loans. ABF desks buy finished portfolios, not new programs from many small originators. Banks cannot hold at 10.5% capital. Small originators cannot produce investor-grade data alone.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Scale economies (cost of funds) and a cornered resource (the one pooled, standard loan-level dataset on outcome-based graduate loans); switching costs for originators (tape and custody).
- **Earned secret:** Investors price the first-loss holder and the document chain, not the borrower. First Marblehead failed on both: its guarantor TERI was downgraded and had to post cash, and its trusts could not later prove who owned the loans. Do it with no single guarantor and with custody from day one.
- **First 90 days:** Get a counsel memo on secondary-grid pricing under Reg B and state law. Collect letters of intent from three originators for tape standards. Close a $25M pilot through a bank partner. Get one insurer to size a $250M delayed-draw note. Set up custody with a trustee.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 6.
- **Risks to kill it:** Reg B reverses or a state AG moves; raising $100M+; Sallie Mae extends its KKR structure to buy others' loans; adverse selection from originators; rating-agency acceptance.

## 3. Offer-Backed (lens: pattern, the Brazilian payroll-first repayment rail plus outcome-priced labor-line conversion)

- **One-liner:** In-school graduate loans for clinicians, underwritten on a hospital's no-clawback hiring commitment, repaid first from the paycheck and priced off the hospital's credit, paid for out of the contingent-labor budget the hospital would otherwise give to staffing agencies.
- **Customer:** Health systems and large community hospitals that hire nurse practitioners, nurse anesthetists, PAs, physical and occupational therapists and pharmacists, and the students in those programs.
- **Payer and signer:** Health-system CFO with the CNO or chief people officer. Budget lines: contingent labor (SIA 2025: travel $15.8B, allied $9.9B, locum $9.6B, per diem $5.2B), sign-on and retention bonuses, and the $5,250 section 127 tuition benefit.
- **Problem:** Employers already pay for this, but late. Clasp's customers have allocated over $130M; Rochester Regional offers up to $180K per nurse anesthetist and Allegheny Health Network up to $110K. That money repays loans after hire. In school the student still borrows at 10-17%, and the hospital cannot guarantee the loan without risking a stay-or-pay claim.
- **Solution:** The hospital signs a non-binding commitment with no clawback. It pays the lender a monthly benefit (section 127, tax-free up to $5,250 a year) for each month the clinician is employed, plus a one-time program fee at hire. The student signs a loan; once employed, a voluntary and revocable direct-deposit split sends a share of pay to the lender first. Payroll-connectivity vendors (Pinwheel, Truv, Argyle) already run this plumbing for personal loans. The lender prices off the offer and the hospital's pledge. The design is carrot-only so it fits AB 692 and the New York carve-out.
- **Why now:** The caps; the stay may lift; AB 692 (Jan 2026) and New York's Act (Dec 2026) take away the stick; section 127 is now permanent; Clasp's Series B shows employers are buying the repayment half.
- **Wedge:** Private nurse-anesthesia, DNP and physical-therapy programs (high price, high employer demand). First 10: health systems that already allocate loan-repayment budgets. Clasp's published customers (Boston Children's, UMass Memorial, UNC Health Appalachian, OhioHealth) are the type of buyer, not my customers.
- **Path to $100M:** About 9,200 financed students a year at $38k = $350M a year of originations, about 7-8% of roughly 120,000 annual completers in hospital-relevant clinical graduate programs (my rough sum of NP, PA, DPT, OT, CRNA, PharmD and health-setting SLP/MSW; not sourced). A 3-4 year ramp builds a $1.0B balance; at 8.5% (hospital-credit pricing) that is $85M of interest. A $5,000 program fee on about 8,000 hires adds $40M. Hospital spend per student is about $21k ($15.75k of benefit over three years plus the fee), so one annual cohort costs hospitals about $190M, roughly 0.5% of staffing spend. If the stay lifts, the paper's field figures (PT $3.2B, nursing $2.9B) make this a much larger pool; if it holds, this is a pricing and pipeline product for programs above $50k a year.
- **Who does it today:** Clasp (repayment side), employer tuition-for-commitment (Rush nurse anesthesia), Sallie Mae/SoFi/College Ave in-school loans, tuition-benefit platforms [M: Guild, InStride], and the staffing agencies.
- **Why they lose:** Clasp is software with no balance sheet or lender. Lenders cannot underwrite an employer's promise. Staffing agencies profit from the shortage and cannot sell its cure. Hospital treasuries cannot hold consumer loans.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Cornered resource (pooled, rated employer commitments), switching costs (payroll and benefit integrations), counter-positioning against staffing agencies.
- **Earned secret:** The cheapest credit in the clinician pipeline is the future employer's balance sheet, but it sits in HR's recruiting budget, not in Treasury. A no-clawback pledge is legal in every state and still moves the rate.
- **First 90 days:** Counsel memo on AB 692, New York, section 127 and whether a pooled arrangement is an ERISA plan or an insurance product. Term sheet with one bank or credit-union partner. Three health systems and two nurse-anesthesia or DNP programs. Pilot 50 students.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 7.
- **Risks to kill it:** The stay outcome; Clasp adds a lender; hospitals will not pay without a clawback; revocable payroll splits (the loss evidence I found is vendor-supplied: Pinwheel claims 57% fewer late payments, Highline up to two-thirds fewer missed payments).

## 4. Operator Rail (lens: pattern, the concentrated-operator vertical platform that EliseAI ran in housing)

- **One-liner:** Sell graduate financing to the few operators that run most of the cap-exposed online and health-professions programs, fund it as a per-start conversion fee out of their marketing budgets, and become the lender of record behind their programs.
- **Customer:** Multi-brand operators and OPM-run portfolios. Adtalem alone reports 97,359 students (Sep 2025) across Chamberlain, Walden, and the Ross and AUC medical schools, revenue about $1.95B in fiscal 2026 [S]. Others on my [M] list, not sized: Grand Canyon, Strategic Education/Capella, Touro, Risepoint (Academic Partnerships), Noodle.
- **Payer and signer:** The operator's student-acquisition budget (marketing and recruitment), paid per funded start. Signer: CFO with the chief enrollment officer.
- **Problem:** The cap hits the operators' highest-priced programs, and they hold the funnel data, the call centers and the clinical-site networks, but they sell through whatever lender panel each brand has. Enrollment-to-start conversion is their P&L line, and a financing failure is a lost acquisition cost. [M: this is my insider hypothesis about what the enrollment dean sees; I did not find conversion data.]
- **Solution:** One operator-wide rate card for every student in its programs, paid as a per-start fee rather than a guarantee, with the tail placed with a third party (idea 2) so the operator is not the guarantor. Proprietary operators are the case where 90/10 matters: private loans count as non-Title IV, but the treatment of a school guarantee is unresolved, so the design avoids one.
- **Why now:** July 1 caps. Adtalem's own growth is uneven (Walden +13.6%, Chamberlain +2.2% in the first quarter), and analysts flag graduate exposure.
- **Wedge:** Graduate nursing and health programs at ground-based operators. The first deal is one brand of one operator.
- **Path to $100M:** About 16,000 funded students a year at $22k = $350M a year. With 35% of starts above the cap (my assumption), that needs about 46,000 graduate starts a year across four or five anchor operators, which I could not verify. Four years builds the $1.0B balance. This rests on three or four anchor contracts, so concentration is the main risk, not market size.
- **Who does it today:** Ascent (works with schools of all kinds), Sallie Mae, SoFi and MPOWER through operators' own lender panels, Climb for career training.
- **Why they lose:** Lenders sell school by school. A lender willing to be the operator-wide program lender, with the risk transferred, gets tens of thousands of loans from three or four signatures.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Switching costs and a cornered resource (operator contracts and funnel data).
- **Earned secret:** Operators already pay a conversion price (their acquisition cost per start). A financing fee is cheaper per start than another lead, and it is the only lever they control after the application.
- **First 90 days:** Read 34 CFR 668.28 and Appendix C with a lawyer. Model one operator's last two years of starts against the cap. Draft the per-start fee agreement. Get an introduction to one operator's treasurer.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 5.
- **Risks to kill it:** HEOA's three-lender rule keeps operators from steering exclusively; the 90/10 treatment; OPM scrutiny (the 2011 bundled-services guidance, GAO review, Minnesota's 2024 ban on public-college tuition-share contracts) [S]; the operators' own lender panels.

## 5. Program Score Exchange (lens: dilemma, the FICO-for-programs standard that the paper says is missing)

- **One-liner:** A neutral exchange where lenders contribute loan performance tapes, schools contribute consented alumni earnings, and ABS investors, rating agencies, schools and lenders buy program-level scores that no single lender can publish.
- **Customer:** ABS investors and rating agencies, ABF desks, lenders, and about 1,500 graduate-serving schools.
- **Payer and signer:** Investor data budgets, lender risk and analytics budgets, school institutional-research budgets. Signers: chief risk officer, head of structured finance, vice provost.
- **Problem:** The paper says the lack of "an industry-standard scoring framework" analogous to FICO limits scale and capital. The structural dilemma: lenders cannot publish program-level prices (state-AG exposure; Earnest paid $2.5M) or easily pool data; ED cannot publish forward-looking scores; rating agencies rate institutions, not programs; schools cannot grade themselves.
- **Solution:** Standard loan-level reporting in exchange for benchmarking. Program-level scores used by investors (purchase grids, reserves), schools (sizing a reserve or buydown) and lenders (loss reserves, pool selection). Documented business-necessity and less-discriminatory-alternative analysis for the lenders that choose to use it in underwriting.
- **Why now:** Reg B removed the federal effects test on 21 Jul; the market must grow 6-7x; the earnings test gives every program an official earnings label from 2027.
- **Wedge:** Benchmarking for the lenders that fund school-sponsored programs, sold with the Seasoning Factory's data. First 10: five lenders and three ABF desks.
- **Path to $100M:** Rating agencies and investors: 5 x $1.5M plus 30 x $0.5M = $22M. Lenders: 40 x $0.4M = $16M. Schools: 1,500 x $30k = $45M. Per-score fees: 3M applications x $3 = $9M. Total about $92M at near-full adoption. Honest read: heroic, and a second data source (idea 7) is needed.
- **Who does it today:** College Scorecard and PEER (free, stale: earnings data for only 12,700 of 41,000 programs), Lightcast, Gray, Ascent's proprietary outcomes model, FICO and the bureaus (individual-level only).
- **Why they lose:** Free federal data is four years stale; lenders' models are proprietary and legally exposed; bureaus score people, not programs.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Network economies (more lenders, more tapes, better scores) and a cornered resource (pooled loan performance by program).
- **Earned secret:** A score that no lender uses to decide individual credit, and that investors and schools use to price risk, keeps the exposure where the law is weakest.
- **First 90 days:** Counsel memo on FCRA and Reg B status of a program-level score. Sign three lenders to a reporting pilot. Draft the tape standard with a rating agency.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 5.
- **Risks to kill it:** FCRA treatment if the score reaches individual decisions; lenders will not share tapes; a later administration or court restores disparate impact; below $100M even at scale.

## 6. Employer-Cohort Cap-Fit (lens: dilemma, what OPMs and universities structurally cannot do)

- **One-liner:** Graduate programs priced at or under the $20,500 federal annual limit (so no private debt is needed), recruited through hospital and district employers instead of paid marketing, with the university granting the degree and the platform running curriculum, clinical placement and cohorts for a fee.
- **Customer:** Universities with spare graduate capacity and health systems and districts with cohorts of 20-50 employees (RN-to-MSN, MSW, school counseling, special-education licensure).
- **Payer and signer:** The university pays a services fee from tuition; the employer pays tuition or cohort fees from its pipeline budget. Signers: provost or dean, and the health system's CNO or chief people officer.
- **Problem:** The OPM model needs a $40-60k price to fund marketing and a 40-60% revenue share. 2U filed for Chapter 11 in July 2024 and Wiley's OPM unit was sold to Academic Partnerships (now Risepoint) for $150M [S]. Colleges are pulling programs in-house, Minnesota barred tuition-share contracts at its public colleges, and the incentive-compensation rules cloud revenue share. The caps now make $20,500 the price at which a non-professional master's needs no private loan.
- **Solution:** A flat fee for design, delivery support, clinical placement and cohort management, with the performance part tied to persistence and completion (not recruitment). Enrollment comes from employer channels with near-zero acquisition cost.
- **Why now:** The price anchor is new, the OPM model is broken at that price, and employers have the pipeline budget (idea 3).
- **Wedge:** RN-to-MSN and school-counseling cohorts at three regional universities with one health system or district partner each.
- **Path to $100M:** At 17% of $20,500 = $3,500 a student-year, $100M needs about 28,600 enrolled students, about 0.9% of roughly 3.1M graduate students [M]. That is 100+ programs. Honest read: this sits where the OPM industry has been losing, and Guild and InStride already sell employer-funded degrees.
- **Who does it today:** Guild, InStride (Strategic Education), Coursera degrees, Risepoint, Noodle, Georgia Tech's low-cost master's, public online programs [M].
- **Why they lose:** OPMs cannot serve a $20,500 price with paid marketing. Universities have no employer channel. Employers cannot grant degrees.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Cornered resource (employer cohort relationships) and process power (clinical placement operations).
- **Earned secret:** The expensive part of a graduate program is finding the student. If the employer sends the student, a price at the cap works.
- **First 90 days:** Sign one health system and one regional university for a 30-student RN-to-MSN cohort priced under $41k. Get a counsel opinion on a fee structure that complies with the incentive-compensation rules. Price clinical placement cost per student.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 4.
- **Risks to kill it:** OPM stigma and regulation; Guild and InStride; fee structure legality; universities keep margin and want the whole price.

## 7. Earnings Replica (lens: shock, the mandated earnings test as new forced spend)

- **One-liner:** For each graduate program, an alumni-consented replica of ED's earnings test, built from consented tax transcripts and payroll data 12-18 months before ED's own determination, with student warnings and program-restructuring support.
- **Customer:** Graduate-serving institutions, especially those with master's programs in counseling, social work, education, arts and humanities; later every program (the test covers undergraduate programs too).
- **Payer and signer:** Provost or CFO; budget: institutional research and compliance. A statutory consequence exists: a failing program must warn students and a second failure costs Direct Loans for at least two years.
- **Problem:** ED's determinations go out in early 2027 and appeals are limited to ED's calculation. The public data is stale (the Scorecard's earnings run only to about 2019-20 for most programs) and schools cannot see IRS earnings. The deadline passes first for the schools with no data.
- **Solution:** Alumni opt in through the school to share tax transcripts or payroll data. We compute the same median-vs-bachelor's test by program, report an early determination and a margin, generate the student warning, and benchmark restructuring options.
- **Why now:** Determinations early 2027; first applicable award year 2027-28; first possible loss 2028-29; ED expects 4% of graduate programs to fail and says counseling is the large field where most fall below the benchmark. [S]
- **Wedge:** Master's programs in mental and social health services and education at mid-size private and regional public universities. First 10: schools that already survey alumni salaries.
- **Path to $100M:** About 1,200 graduate-serving institutions at $50k = $60M, plus 1,500 institutions with undergraduate and certificate programs at $30k = $45M (the test covers every program), about $105M at 2,700 institutions. Heroic: that is near saturation, and SIS vendors and EAB will bundle. The exchange's investor and lender data (idea 5) adds revenue from the other side.
- **Who does it today:** Prentus (outcome tracking), Anthology, UMB-type bank advisories, Lightcast, Gray, EAB [S]. ED's own determination is free.
- **Why they lose:** They model from public data that is years stale or survey self-reported pay. A replica from consented transcripts is the only forward-looking, program-level view of the same data ED will use.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Cornered resource (consented alumni earnings by program) and switching costs once alumni data is connected.
- **Earned secret:** Schools hold what ED does not: alumni who will answer a request. Consented transcripts turn the school's relationship into a 12-month head start.
- **First 90 days:** Run a consent pilot with 300 alumni at two schools. Reproduce the test on a public subset to check method. Get counsel on the transcript consent mechanism (IRS Form 4506-C or payroll APIs) and FERPA.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 5.
- **Risks to kill it:** Alumni consent rates; the compliance whiplash filter (the rule could be delayed or narrowed); first loss in 2028-29 is late; bundling.

## 8. Verified-Pay Part-Timer (lens: shock, proration of limits for part-time working adults)

- **One-liner:** Graduate loans for working adults who are employed in their field, underwritten on verified payroll and repaid first through a voluntary direct-deposit split, with in-school interest paid from salary so balances do not capitalize.
- **Customer:** Part-time graduate students with jobs (RN to NP, teacher to counselor, analyst to MBA), many of whom hit prorated federal limits.
- **Payer and signer:** The borrower. The signer is the borrower, and some employers pay a part through tuition benefits.
- **Problem:** The paper's footnote says part-time students, including those continuing from before July 2026, are limited to lower amounts based on enrollment intensity. Lenders underwrite on credit score and cosigner, and the paper says program-level data is legally exposed. Individual verified income is the clean alternative, and payroll-linked repayment is proven in personal loans.
- **Solution:** Income verified by payroll connection, a loan sized to net pay, a payroll split (fixed dollars or a share of net pay), and in-school interest-only paid from salary. No program-level data is used in pricing, so the product does not depend on the Reg B outcome.
- **Why now:** Proration is new; payroll vendors (Pinwheel, Truv, Argyle) exist; section 127 lets some employers add a tax-free contribution.
- **Wedge:** RN-to-MSN and DNP students employed at one health system; teacher-to-counselor students employed at one district. First 10: employers that run a tuition benefit and will host the payroll connection.
- **Path to $100M:** If 20% of the 370,000 displaced borrowers are working part-timers (my assumption) = 74,000 x $21.6k = $1.6B a year. A 20% share = $320M a year, which builds a $1.0B balance in about four years. At 9-10% that is $90-100M.
- **Who does it today:** SoFi and Earnest (income and cash-flow underwriting), Funding U (individual academic performance), payroll-linked lenders (Kashable, Highline) for personal loans, Sallie Mae with a cosigner.
- **Why they lose:** Banks must underwrite ability to repay the highest amortizing payment (OCC Bulletin 2015-7) and securitize fixed cash flows, so they stay on score and cosigner. Payroll-connectivity vendors do not lend. Nobody sells both to the part-time graduate student.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Switching costs (payroll connection) and cheaper capital as loss data accumulates.
- **Earned secret:** Collection priority can matter as much as score. A borrower who has chosen to route pay to the lender first is a different credit than the same borrower on autopay.
- **First 90 days:** Counsel memo on revocable payroll splits, state wage-assignment law and UDAAP risk. A bank partner. 100 loans at one health system. Measure delinquency against a control.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 4.
- **Risks to kill it:** Revocable deposit splits protect less than they look; the loss-reduction claims I found are all vendor-supplied; consumer-protection risk of "pay the lender first"; Earnest and SoFi are close.

## Ideas I considered and dropped

- **School-funded reserve program (Perkins 2.0).** Ascent has run rated school risk-share deals for eight years across 100+ graduate partners. What remains is the capital layer (idea 2) and the neutral buyer-side agent (idea 1).
- **Program-risk reinsurer or surety for school risk-share ("Tail Co").** The logic is right: schools cannot hold the risk, lenders cannot price it, and an insurer can. But at about 2.5% premium on perhaps $3.5B a year of school-sponsored loans, 100% share yields $87M. Dead as a standalone; it works only as a captive piece inside ideas 1 and 2.
- **State graduate-loan platform with tax-exempt funding.** The funding edge is real (CHESLA 5.50-7.99% vs private rates to 17%), but qualified student loan bonds sit under the private-activity cap (Connecticut's SB 8 carve-out is $60M) until the pending Student Loan Bond Expansion Act (S.3761, H.R.2660) passes. Vendor fees of 1.5-2% of originations need $5-6B a year for $100M. Dead until Congress acts.
- **Income-contingent private loans.** Prepayment-penalty limits on private education loans [M] let high earners refinance away, which is the adverse selection that killed ISAs. The NBER paper itself says no private product can match federal IDR and PSLF.
- **Loan marketplace or group buying.** Juno's reported share (14-15% of about $1.4B) is about $200M of volume; even at 2% that is $4M of revenue, and lenders cannot lawfully pay schools.
- **Private-loan certification hub.** About 430,000 graduate certifications a year at $60 = $26M.
- **Tuition installment or BNPL at the bursar.** On the cliche list, and Nelnet, Flywire and Sallie Mae sit there.
- **Parent PLUS home-equity product.** A real gap ($11B in 2022, now capped at $20,000 a year) but undergraduate, not this pool. Mentioned only as an extension of idea 1.
- **Origination utility for private credit.** Sallie Mae-KKR closed the pitch for Sallie Mae's flow; the rest is idea 2.
- **Aid-office outsourcing with outcome pricing.** The pool is too small once limited to graduate loans.
