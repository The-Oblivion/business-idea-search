# C06: Level-1 Bench (refined from Overnight Yard Crews)

**One-liner:** A hire-train-deploy technician workforce company: six-week academy-trained W-2 Level-1 technicians placed by the hour into heavy-duty shops, dealers and yard fleets that cannot fill entry slots, converting to the customer's payroll for a fee.

**Revenue cases (5-7 years):** median $20M/yr; upside $100M/yr.

**Full refined pitch:** round2/deep/C06_refined.md · **Research dossier:** round2/deep/C06_dossier.md

## What the adversarial critics said (severity, then top objection)
- **Competition / incumbents** (fatal): INCUMBENT RESPONSE: the strongest rival is already doing this, is PE-funded, and is buying the same acquisition targets the idea's path to $100M depends on. Amerit Fleet Solutions' own July 23 2026 release claims over 3,500 technicians and over 350,000 managed assets, which is about 5-6x the roughly 640 field staff the idea needs at $100M. Amerit was over 2,500 field technicians in January 2025, so it added about 1,000 in 18 months, partly by acquisition. It has been majority-owned by New Mountain Capital (about $55B AUM) since January 2025, hired a VP of Corporate Development in February 2026 'to accelerate acquisition strategy', and bought Pro Reefer (May 2026) and Kirk NationaLease plus TTPS (July 2026; 19 shops; contract maintenance and mobile service). True North (Garnett Station) is on its sixth deal since November 2025. Ryder has bought Pit Stop and Truck Service Depot (January 2026). Cox runs a 24/7 mobile fleet service and brokers PM through FleetNet America's 60,000+ providers. Rush, National Fleet Management and Cox already do PM and DOT inspections at the customer's site outside business hours. The likely response to a traction signal is to price-match $115 an hour (Ryder, Amerit and Cox already pay for the same labor), bundle PM with parts and shop work, or buy the entrant. Counter-positioning is absent because the incumbents' cost structure is the same hourly labor. The dossier's own conclusion ('path to $100M runs through M&A') means the entrant would bid against these buyers for targets, or sell out to them at a regional-business multiple.
- **Customer / pricing / go-to-market** (fatal): The 50-2,000-truck buyer is either already locked up or has a cheap in-house substitute, so there is little open budget to win. Scope: this is fatal to the $100M thesis. It survives only as a $10-25M regional services business, which the dossier already says. Penske's Jim Lager says 50-60 power units once justified an in-house shop but larger fleets now outsource (Fleet Maintenance). The NPTC 2021 survey found 46% of private fleets outsource ALL maintenance and about 20% do it all in-house, so most of that segment already has a Ryder, Penske or dealer relationship. A 2019 FleetOwner/Informa survey went the other way: 55% of respondents do everything in-house and private fleets lean in-house (60% all or most). Either sample is bad for a newcomer, because the buyer is already served or is insourcing with a $16-22/hr lube tech as the substitute. Only 33% of fleets say they are understaffed (Fleet Maintenance Dec 2025), and the 65.5% ATRI figure counts shops, not fleets. Ryder's Torque targets a centralized fleet of 50-100 trucks, which is the idea's wedge customer, and sells with no long-term contract.
- **Execution / regulation / capital / timing** (serious): The labor model as pitched (4-6 week Level-1 hires plus a remote video master tech) is not deployable everywhere, and the fix costs the margin the dossier's model depends on. The dossier already shows 49 CFR 396.19 and 396.25 block a 4-6 week hire from being the inspector or brake-qualified person, and it says it found no state licensing regime for heavy-duty technicians. That second claim is wrong for Michigan, where I read the statute. MCL 257.1302(p) defines a heavy-duty truck as 14,000 lb GVWR or more. MCL 257.1310b(2) makes brakes, suspension and steering, drivetrain, diesel engine and electrical separately certifiable categories. MCL 257.1304(c) exempts only a business that repairs vehicles for a single establishment or commonly owned affiliates, so a crew company serving many fleets is a regulated repair facility. The Department of State manual (revised June 2026) lets uncertified workers hold a 2-year, non-renewable trainee permit but only 'under the direct supervision' of a certified mechanic in the same category, and the certified mechanic's name goes on the invoice. A video mentor is not obviously 'direct supervision'. That reading is untested with the state (U). I replicated the dossier's section 5 model and got 20.5% / 15.0% / 4.6% EBITDA at 72% / 65% / 55% utilization with a $24 Level-1 wage. If each crew gets an on-site certified lead at $36.50 (the FreightWaves median, burden 1.3) in place of the remote mentor, and the vehicle costs an all-in $46k a year instead of $25k (see objection 2), the margins fall to about 11% / 5% / -8%. Break-even utilization rises from 52% to about 61% (A). No published utilization benchmark exists for yard or mobile heavy-duty crews. The fix also turns '60 mentors' into about 290 certified technicians, drawn from a trade where ATRI says 19.3% of slots are unfilled. Michigan is one state, and I did not find others (absence of evidence).

## Second-round critic: wounded
- **Is the upside believable?** No as a plan, yes only as an honestly labelled tail. The author puts $100M at 3-5% unconditional and about 10% if the gates pass, and says the unconditional median is a failed pilot; that candor is credit. But the seven 'all must hold' assumptions are not independent and several are unsupported or contradicted. (a) Bill at or above $55 with wage at or below $27 is the unproven 93% markup, against a 35-45% norm and an incumbent that appears to bill less for experienced techs. (b) Cost per graduate at or below $6,500 relies partly on WIOA OJT, likely unavailable to a hire-out staffing model (Iowa read this session; other states per snippets; Texas and Georgia unchecked). (c) 25% attrition and 35% conversion at $7,500 is inconsistent with free conversion after about 1,000 hours; with rational conversion, graduates needed roughly double and EBITDA falls to about zero or below (A). (d) 25 enterprise master agreements at 24 FTE (57% of FTEs, about $59M) have no named prospect, and the refined admits likely buyers insource. (e) FTE growth to 1,000 in 7 years is faster than any precedent in the file, with recruiting at about 10% of national trade-school output. (f) $15-30M capital is plausible once vans are dropped, but yields only about $7M EBITDA at maturity on median economics. I would put the real probability of $100M at or below 1-2%. The credible upside is a $10-25M regional benching and training business, which matches the original critics' downgrade target and the dossier's best realistic outcome, not the $100M thesis. Wounded rather than dead because the key assumption (the shop pays $50 or more) is untested rather than disproven, temp-to-hire technician staffing demonstrably exists for experienced techs, and the $500 two-week test is cheap. Not 'survives' because two load-bearing mechanics (tenure and subsidy) are broken and the demand case is unsupported. - **Remaining holes:** TENURE AND CONVERSION CONTRADICT EACH OTHER. The pricing makes conversion free after about 1,000 hours (C06_refined section 3). Staffing norm is similar: one vendor summary says Robert Half's fee falls from 25% of salary to 0% after 640 hours (U, vendor blog). The unit economics assume 25-30% attrition plus 30-35% conversion a year, about 1.7 years of average tenure. With free conversion a shop saves about $14 x 2,000 = $28k a year per head by converting, so a rational shop (and the worker, who gets a raise once the 1.9x markup disappears) converts at 6-12 months. At about 6 months of tenure turnover is about 2 a year, and training cost per FTE-year rises from about $4.8k to about $16k. My arithmetic (A) puts median EBITDA at roughly -5% to 0% instead of +7.3%, and graduates needed for 1,000 FTE roughly double (about 2,000 a year, not 830). The refined pre-mortem names retention but never reconciles this. The fee is also stated three ways ($7,000 falling to zero at 1,000 hours; $7,000 at 520 hours in the test; $7,500 in the upside). | THE CUSTOMER PAYS MORE THAN THE TRAINING COST IT SUPPOSEDLY AVOIDS. The pitch says the $7,000 conversion fee is below ATRI's $8,211. At the test's own 520-hour trigger the customer has also paid about $14 an hour of premium over a direct hire: 520 x $14 + $7,000 = about $14.3k against $8.2k (A). ATRI's $8,211 is 357 hours of trainee wages, not a cash fee. Shops can hire a green tech directly at $24-28 and train them, and dealers already do (ATD 2024 entry-level turnover 44%; dealers hire and train directly, per ttnews summary). The substitute critique is restated as the riskiest assumption, not answered. | DEMAND EVIDENCE IS MISAPPLIED. The 54% (Fullbay) and 65.5% (ATRI) understaffing figures measure a shortage of qualified technicians, not open Level-1 slots. The TMC 2018 survey (search snippet via https://www.ttnews.com/articles/combat-technician-shortage) found 19.2% of shops short of applicants but 80.8% short of qualified applicants. ATRI also reports more than 30% of formal training-program graduates were unqualified in 20 core skills, so a 6-week academy graduate may be judged unqualified. The 15-35% 'Level-1-fillable' share that sizes the market has no data behind it. The only temp-technician precedent found (LAUNCH TransTechs) sells experienced techs, not trainees. | PRICE ANCHOR AND THE EXISTING TEMP-TECH COMPETITOR CONTRADICT $52. The refined concedes a 93% markup against a 35-45% norm. An undated LAUNCH temp-to-hire posting (Suffolk VA, search snippet, https://launchtws.com/jobs/13171/A-+Level+Diesel+Mechanic-Suffolk-Virginia) lists maximum pay of $26 for an A tech and $24 for a B tech with conversion at the same rate or higher. At a typical 1.4-1.5x that implies roughly $34-39 bill for experienced techs (A), against $52 for a 6-week trainee paid $27. LAUNCH bought the unit from TrueBlue (reason unknown). The refined lists LAUNCH as a competitor but never confronts its price and wage level. The two-week test also discriminates poorly: non-binding order forms with no deposit, pass at 5 of 40, and a wide 'reprice and rerun' band. | WIOA ON-THE-JOB-TRAINING SUBSIDY IS LIKELY INELIGIBLE FOR THIS STRUCTURE. The upside cost per graduate of $6,500 or less leans on WIOA OJT reimbursement. Iowa's policy (read this session, https://epolicy.iwd.iowa.gov/Policy/Index?id=146) says OJT may not be written with staffing agencies for positions that will be 'hired out'. Nebraska, Oakland and one Ohio board say the same or similar (search snippets). The one OJT source the refined cites (Wisconsin, read this session) is a superseded 2024 policy page that says nothing either way. Some boards allow agencies that provide regular ongoing work, but a 2-week-minimum temporary placement is what they exclude. Texas and Georgia board policies were not found. The direct beneficiary of OJT is the shop that hires directly, which strengthens the substitute.

## Riskiest assumption and the 2-week test
- **Riskiest assumption:** Independent shops, dealers and private-fleet shops with open Level-1 work will pay a staffing-style bill rate of at least $50 an hour (about 1.85x the Level-1 wage; a 93% markup against a 35-45% skilled-trades staffing norm) for pre-trained Level-1s with a conversion option, instead of hiring direct at $24-28. Breakeven is about $47.50 an hour. I searched and found no evidence that shops or fleets buy temporary technicians at scale.
- **2-week test:** Cost under $500, one person, one metro. (1) Write a one-page offer: pre-trained Level-1 at $52 an hour, two-week minimum, 30-day replacement, $7,000 conversion after 520 hours, start in 4-6 weeks, with a non-binding order form that says plainly cohorts are not yet running. (2) Contact 40 targets: 15 independent heavy-duty shops, 10 dealer service managers, 10 private-fleet maintenance managers, 5 tire dealers with commercial service. Ask for open Level-1 requisitions and age, work turned away last month, current all-in cost of a direct hire, and a signed order form. (3) In parallel run two paid job ads for a six-week program ($19 an hour in training, $26-27 after) with a $300 budget to measure supply. Pass: at least 5 of 40 sign at $50 or more, at least 3 report a requisition older than 60 days, and at least 40 qualified applicants at under $15 each. Fail: fewer than 2 signed or stated willingness below $44. In between, reprice or retarget (for example dealer groups only) and rerun. Then 90-day gates: three paying pilots at $50+, 80% 90-day retention, at least 24% gross margin on billed hours, workers' comp plus general-liability quote at or below 12% of payroll.
