# C11: Outreach Partner Labs (rebuilt): open-book operating company for health systems that keep their outreach lab

**One-liner:** A neutral operating company that runs the commercial half of a health system's outreach lab (billing, payer status, couriers, client services, data) at open-book cost plus 10-12%, inside a 100% hospital-owned lab entity, and only later adds a hub and one sponsor-funded performing-lab JV.

**Revenue cases (5-7 years):** median $12M/yr; upside $110M/yr.

**Full refined pitch:** round2/deep/C11_refined.md · **Research dossier:** round2/deep/C11_dossier.md

## What the adversarial critics said (severity, then top objection)
- **Competition / incumbents** (fatal): The strongest incumbents already sell this exact product, with the hospital keeping its labs, and they can outbid or out-structure any startup once it shows traction. Quest's own outreach-partnership page lists three structures: buy the outreach assets, manage the hospital's labs under a multi-year deal while the system keeps ownership (Memorial Hermann, 21 labs), or form a joint venture (Corewell 51/49). Labcorp manages Crouse's inpatient labs (Q1 2026 release), Inspira's labs (Jan 2025) and Legacy's. Kaufman Hall (Aug 2025) says national buyers hold 'significant reserves', terms for partnerships 'of all types' are competitive, and it names no independent or PE operator among the buyers. Labcorp's May 2026 Epic Aura integration offers its full menu to 'any hospital using Epic' with a 'light-lift' interface, which weakens the connectivity wedge. The precedent is PAML, the one independent operator of this model. Labcorp bought it in 2017, ended most of its JVs, and in the Kootenai case imposed a 5-year noncompete. The independent operator's exit is to be acquired by the competitor. The researcher listed Quest and Labcorp as competitors but did not weigh that they already offer the 'control' pitch and can raise the cash bid in response.
- **Customer / pricing / go-to-market** (fatal): NO PRICE WORKS FOR BOTH SIDES. The only observed price for 'an operator runs the commercial half' is the PAML/Kootenai deal: pre-analytics and billing for a 50/50 revenue split. Kootenai ended it (cited lost or relabeled specimens, reimbursement below reagent cost, no control over service) and insourced with Mayo Outreach Insights. EBIDA rose about $10M FY22-FY24. The idea's 6-10% fee cannot fund couriers, patient service centers, billing and sales (lab billing alone quotes 5-8% of collections). Taken as a fee on top of the hospital's costs, it takes about 36% of a typical program's contribution (A: $12.8M median x 25% = $3.2M; 9% = $1.15M). The hospital breaks even only if outreach revenue grows about 29% (A: assumes a 40% marginal margin). Once an operator is the outside party between the hospital and its clients, the one documented customer fired it.
- **Execution / regulation / capital / timing** (fatal): 1. THE STRUCTURE THAT MAKES IT VENTURE-SCALE IS BARRED BY MEDICARE'S OWN BILLING TEXT (verified). The sharpened pick has a 50-51% operator-controlled outreach LLC that bills while the hospital core lab does 80-85% of the testing. I extracted the CMS Claims Processing Manual ch. 16 text (pdftotext). Under Social Security Act 1833(h)(5)(A), a referring lab may bill for tests another lab performs only if (a) it is in or part of a rural hospital, (b) it is 'wholly owned by' the performing entity, 'wholly owns' it, or both are wholly owned by a third entity, or (c) it refers no more than 30% of the tests it receives. A 51/49 LLC is not wholly owned by anything, and 15-20% of the volume is the most it could refer, never 80-85%. If a hospital's lab performs most of the tests, the LLC cannot bill them. The only escapes both destroy the pitch. Either the LLC becomes the performing lab (own CLIA certificate, hospital contributes the testing and staff, plus capital for hubs and analyzers). That is the Corewell/Quest and ProMedica/Sonic template, and it removes the 'hospital keeps its lab' differentiator. Or it shrinks to a fee-only management contract of about $1-3M a system, the boutique outcome. The dossier raised this conflict; the manual text now confirms the 'wholly' wording. Orion/FMOL (January 2026) already offers the 'operations, technology and logistics' pitch, and Orion is an existing independent lab with its own testing capacity, so it likely avoids the problem. Its JV terms are undisclosed (U).

## Second-round critic: wounded
- **Is the upside believable?** Mostly no. The rewrite is candid that the upside is about 5% (range 2-8%), and the independent product of its own five probabilities is about 2%. But the $110M case is built on four weaknesses.\n\n- It needs 15 managed systems. Its own funnel yields about 10-11, which gives about $83M.\n- The hub ($20M) and the performing-lab JV ($30M) are the operator-owned-lab model that destroys the neutrality moat.\n- The managed-ops layer assumes an unbenchmarked 15-18% commercial-function cost and a 20% or larger operator cost advantage. Neither has support.\n- The 25% chance of beating Orion, TriCore, Mayo/ARUP/Chi and the nationals is a guess with no precedent: no non-national operator has been shown to renew past year 3.\n\nAt that point the revenue is mostly pass-through at about 8% EBITDA, with sponsor-owned JV economics, so enterprise value of $80-130M overstates what founders and seed investors would own. It clears a revenue threshold on paper without being a credible venture-scale outcome. I would put the real probability nearer 1-3%. The $12M median is also overstated (about $7-8M is consistent with the rewrite's own 45% failure rate).\n\nThe business is testable for under $2k, and it is better than the original, which was dead on legal grounds. That is why this is wounded rather than dead. The cheap test should be run before anything else is built, and it should be held to signed LOIs and a counsel opinion rather than expressions of interest.
- **Remaining holes:** The price model implies a cost advantage the rewrite never states or supports. Price must be at most 92% of the hospital's baseline commercial-function cost (15-18% of revenue, about $4.1M on a $25M program). The price is operator cost x 1.10-1.12 plus a $250-400k flat fee. Solving gives operator cost about 17-24% below the hospital's own cost, from a startup with 1-3 clients and no route density. The only quantified hospital benefit is the 8% guarantee, about $0.33M a year (about 1.3% of outreach revenue), against a 5-year lock-in and a billing and payer migration. The 15-18% baseline is admitted to be unbenchmarked. Billing alone is 5-8% of collections from a vendor blog, and RCM vendors sell that piece unbundled. | The funnel arithmetic contradicts the upside case. Section 5 gives 160 x 70% stay x 25-30% open x 33% win = about 10-11 systems, but the text says about 15. The upside only gets to 15 by using 45 receptive stayers (40% open). With 10 systems the stack is about $40M managed + $13M hub + $30M JV = about $83M, under $100M. Section 3 also says only 10-20% of stayers value neutrality, which is the sole edge claimed. That is 11-22 of 112 stayers, so signing 15 means winning most of the neutrality-minded segment against Orion, TriCore, Mayo/ARUP/Chi and national management offers. That filter never enters the funnel. | The unconditional median is overstated. The rewrite says 45% fail to sign a second client, yet the median is 4 managed systems (about $12M). That needs about 91% of the companies that reach 2 clients to reach 4. The $20M figure only appears after conditioning on success. A consistent unconditional median is roughly $7-8M, which is under the $20M gate the dossier cites. | Stand-up demand and customer revenue loss are not quantified. The LLC re-enrolls the outreach book at freestanding-lab payer status. The dossier's own HCCI datum is that hospital-outpatient commercial rates are about 3x independent-lab rates, and Chi found nationals' net revenue per test about 20% below typical outreach. The rewrite never estimates the CFO's revenue loss from the LLC. Its UHC pain is also softer than the pitch implies. The Preferred Lab Network is a tier inside the network, and hospital labs are not out of network. Elevance's treatment of on-campus lab tests is unknown. The only precedent for the stand-up is Frisbie's response to a real lockout (NH, Anthem, since 2010). There is no verified mandate that turns an audit into a $250-500k project. | 'Nationals cannot offer neutrality' is asserted, not evidenced. Quest already sells 'lab management lite' (hospital keeps staff and ownership) and management service agreements that lock per-test cost. A national that profits from send-out volume can price courier, PSC and billing logistics at or below cost plus 10-12%. No hospital has said it values the no-steering and no-noncompete terms.

## Riskiest assumption and the 2-week test
- **Riskiest assumption:** A non-selling health system will sign a 5-year open-book operating contract with a startup, at a price at least 8% below its own cost, instead of doing it in-house, buying advice from Mayo, ARUP or Chi, or taking a national's management offer. This bundles demand and the operator's cost advantage. The cost advantage rests on an unbenchmarked estimate that commercial functions cost 15-18% of outreach revenue.
- **2-week test:** Under $2k, 10-14 days. (1) Offer a free one-page Network and Margin Audit to 20 outreach directors, lab administrators or CFOs at systems with $15-50M outreach in two metros, reached through CLMA, ADLM, CAP and Dark Report networks. Ask for a 30-minute call, their courier, PSC, billing and client-service cost lines, and their UHC, Anthem and Elevance status. (2) Read Elevance's and UHC's own provider notices to see whether on-campus outreach tests are affected. (3) Spend one counsel hour (about $750-1,500) on the structure: 100% hospital-owned LLC, vendor agreement at cost plus flat fees, hospital-employed salaried reps, opt-in hub. Pass: at least 4 of 20 take the call, at least 3 share cost data, and in at least 2 the commercial functions cost 14% or more of outreach revenue and the buyer would outsource billing and logistics at 8%+ below cost with SLAs. At least 1 must also agree to a paid $25k audit or a non-binding LOI, and counsel must flag no blocker. Kill: fewer than 2 share data, or none would outsource at that price, or counsel's first read is that the structure fails section 40.1 or EKRA.
