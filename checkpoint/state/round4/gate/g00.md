# G00 gate: Contract Atlas (independent home-health payer-rate network)

Date: 2026-10-26. Verdict: **WEAK**. Hard gate before any build: verify what MHC actually is.

## Verdict in one paragraph
The wedge is real and the whitespace is real: no home-health-specific Medicare Advantage (MA) contract-versus-remittance auditor or payer-rate benchmark turned up. But the part Asher can legally and cheaply do (Stage 0, the audit) is worth roughly $4-7k per agency per year. The part that makes it big (Stage 1 pooled rate benchmark, Stage 2 messenger network, 2-4% admin fee) runs into three separate legal walls (payer rate-confidentiality clauses, antitrust, anti-kickback) that a teen cannot clear on a $10k budget. The insider premise (MHC is a Florida home-health company) is still unverified, and there is one conflicting signal. First *paid* recovery in 60-90 days is not realistic; 4-6 months is.

## Legal walls (this is where the "big" version dies or gets expensive)
1. **Rate confidentiality.** Payer agreements treat fee schedules as confidential. Anthem provider notices say so and extend the duty to any third party the provider shares with; Humana has called its rates commercially sensitive in an FTC filing. A "what Humana really pays agencies like you" benchmark asks agencies to risk breach and termination. (Secondary sources from search results; the Anthem page itself would not load for a primary read.)
2. **Antitrust.** DOJ withdrew the health-care information-exchange safety zone in Feb 2023 and FTC followed in July 2023; DOJ says case-by-case, no replacement. Competing sellers pooling what they are paid, through a third-party intermediary, is the pattern DOJ's statement of interest in the MultiPlan litigation attacks. Messenger-model networks work only with real firewalls and counsel. Budget $25-75k for counsel before Stage 1/2 (my estimate).
3. **Anti-kickback.** MA is a federal program. OIG Advisory Opinion 98-4 flagged percentage-of-revenue pay to a management company that arranges managed-care contracts as a potential technical violation. The 20-25% contingency is closer to market practice (Aspirion et al.); the 2-4% network admin fee is the riskier piece. Medicare reassignment/billing-agent rules bite on FFS money, not MA, but get counsel to confirm.
4. **No appeal rights.** Contracted providers have no Medicare payment-dispute rights against MA plans; disputes are contract-only. Recovery depends on contract deadlines and an owner's willingness to antagonize a payer he depends on.
5. HIPAA is manageable (BAA, minimum necessary). Age is not a legal barrier (he is an adult). No license needed for audit software.

## Reality checks on the pitch's numbers
- **Agency count:** 12,234 Medicare-certified HHAs in 2024 (MedPAC data book via secondary report), up 1.5%; ex-California supply fell about 1% since 2019. FFS spend $16.0B in 2024 (MedPAC) means about $1.3M average FFS revenue per agency. An independent's MA book is probably $0.8-1.5M (one large operator quoted 60% MA, another 40%).
- **Stage 0 yield:** 1-3% leakage (hospital-vendor range; I found no HH-specific figure) on $1M = $10-30k recovered, x 20-25% = about $2-7k per agency per year. 1,500 agencies = roughly $10M, matching the pitch's own bottom-up. Not $100M.
- **The "30-40% less" claim is soft.** MedPAC (April/June 2026) found no statistically significant link between MA penetration and HHA margins; a 10-point MA rise was tied to about a 2.7% drop in all-payer revenue and costs, mostly offsetting. Agencies *say* MA pays less (reported as a meeting point, not a MedPAC figure), and smaller agencies were hit harder. That supports the pain for independents but not a $1.4B "uplift" pool.
- **Why-now partly holds:** MA is 55.4% of beneficiaries; Trella says MA home-health volume has passed FFS; the nationwide HHA enrollment moratorium started 2026-05-13 and runs six months (extendable in six-month steps; I found no extension announced as of 2026-10-26).
- **Opposing trend:** PE roll-ups and a moratorium that makes certificates valuable push owners to sell; PE platforms have in-house contracting.

## Sharpened version (does not shrink it; reorders it around what survived)
**Contract Atlas = the payment-accuracy and payer-performance system for independent post-acute agencies.**
1. **Gate (this week):** confirm MHC's entity type, CCN, MA payer mix and annual MA revenue via Sunbiz, AHCA Health Finder and a direct question to grandfather/mom. If not Medicare-certified HH with at least about $500k MA revenue, kill.
2. **Stage 0 as is, with the fee restructured:** flat diagnostic plus a *fixed-fee per recovered claim tier or capped contingency*, vetted by a health-care lawyer for AKS before the first engagement letter. MHC pilot first, then 5 owners.
3. **Stage 1 reframed:** ship *per-payer, per-agency operational benchmarks* (days to pay, short-pay rate, denial and prior-auth rate, % of Medicare actually realized on each agency's *own* remits) before any cross-agency rate exchange. Cross-agency rate indices only through a counsel-designed structure: third-party aggregator, data at least 3 months old, 5+ contributors, no single contributor over 25%, no plan names where contracts forbid. Keep the old safety-zone numbers as a design floor, not a guarantee.
4. **Distribution through intermediaries, not 1,500 cold owners:** Florida outsourced home-health billing firms and consultants (each serves dozens of small agencies), home-health M&A brokers (payer-contract diligence in sale processes is a single-party product with no antitrust issue), then HCAF in summer 2027.
5. **Network/MSO as a Stage 3 option** only after 150+ agencies are on the data layer, counsel is retained, and a pilot with one delegated manager (tango/CareCentrix/myNEXUS) shows they will accept aggregated feedback.

## Deciding reason
The big path depends on pooling competitors' payer rates, which collides with payer confidentiality clauses, antitrust and kickback risk, none of which the free-diagnostic pitch prices in, while the part he can start now is small and the key insider fact is unverified.
