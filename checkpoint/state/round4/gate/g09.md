# G09: Auth Clock (G:mhc_revenue:4): gate report, 26 Oct 2026

**Verdict: WEAK.** The founder can reach the first customers and nothing legal blocks him. But four funded startups already sell MA home-health authorization and denial work to the same buyer, and at realistic overturn rates the whole national fee pool is about $35-105M, so $10M a year needs roughly 10-30% of it. The only unclaimed piece (home-health-level clock and denial measurement) is real, but it is a data-network bet, not the product as written.

## What the search found

### 1. Someone already does it (direct, funded, same buyer)
- **Claim Health** (YC Spring 2025, New York). $4.4M seed in Jan 2026 (Maverick, Peak XV, YC, DHVP). Sells "revenue infrastructure for post-acute": referral intake, eligibility, authorization management, payment reconciliation, and a prior-auth specialist who follows up with payers. Named customers: Ascend Health, Interim HealthCare, Home Care RN. Claims 30x revenue growth in under a year (self-reported). This is the idea's own list entry and it is the closest direct hit.
- **Silna Health.** $27M total (Accel, Bain Capital Ventures). Its home health page says it "manages the entire home health authorization lifecycle", with recertification and remaining-visit monitoring across the census, plus Predictive Document Intelligence (Jan 2026) that checks documentation before submission. No pricing published; no named home health customers found.
- **Enzo Health.** $26M total, including a $20M Series A in May 2026 (N47). An "AI-native" home health EHR and intake product that checks eligibility and MA authorization requirements. Per-user pricing, not published.
- **Adaptive Innovations.** $60M total (Felicis, Bain Capital Ventures, Optum Ventures). An AI-native home health operator buying agency licenses to run intake, prior auth and billing itself. It is not a vendor, but it is a funded player that internalizes this exact problem.
- **EMR and RCM incumbents.** HCHB lists "Authorizations" as a service and a Revenue Cycle Services team that offers to run the whole authorization and reauthorization process. HCHB's own pages do not say whether its software tracks MA decision clocks or denials, and the Axxess and WellSky pages I found only show eligibility and intake. HHAeXchange has authorization tools, but it is Medicaid-focused.
- **Channels and outsourcers.** Availity (status checks for Humana and others), Outsource Strategies International and similar vendors (per-authorization or FTE pricing; remote assistants from about $1,600 a month), and the idea's own list (Prochant and others).
- **Data-asset neighbor.** Trella Health sells MA volume, referral and payer-mix data to home health and SNF users. I found no Trella denial or clock data. Experian Health and Waystar sell payer-performance and denial analytics, aimed at hospitals.

### 2. What nobody seems to do (the real gap)
- KFF (Aug 2026): MA plans denied 12% of 2025 prior-auth requests, and 67% of appealed standard denials were overturned. KFF and Home Health Care News both say the data are aggregated across all services, so **no home-health-level plan metric is public**.
- OIG (June 2026) found denial rates of 23% down to 0.4% across MA organizations for SNF admissions, 95% overturned on appeal, and that outside contractors such as naviHealth drove many denials. OIG asked CMS for request-level data by service type and contractor. CMS has not agreed; the recommendation is open, with an update due 7 Dec 2026. OIG covered SNF, IRF and LTCH, not home health.
- I found no vendor that publishes plan-by-plan decision-clock compliance for home health. Claim Health and Silna would hold the data to do it, but I found nothing saying they do. A competitor could add it in a sprint.

### 3. The why-now is real but eroding
- CMS-0057-F clocks (72-hour expedited, 7-day standard, specific denial reasons) have applied since 1 Jan 2026. Public plan metrics were due 31 Mar 2026.
- The Jan 1, 2027 prior-auth API binds payers. LeadingAge (May 2026) says it is still unclear how fully home health and other post-acute providers will be included, and that there is "little evidence" the full range of providers will benefit. So "plug into the FHIR APIs on Jan 1, 2027" is not a dependable feature date.
- Much home health authorization is delegated. BCBS Michigan moved MA home health prior auth to independent companies on 5 Jan 2026 and to a named vendor from 1 Mar 2026. A plan-level scorecard hides this; the right unit is plan times UM contractor.
- The AHIP pledge (insurers say they cut prior-auth volume 11% in year one, MA more than 15%, self-reported) and the 80% real-time-approval target for 2027 both shrink the denial pool. Independent checks are lukewarm (KFF Health News: 16% of practices saw less work).

### 4. Size (bottom-up, with the haircuts)
- **MA home-health revenue.** The idea's $14B holds up. MedPAC: FFS home health was $15.7B in 2023. MA users are 8.3% of enrollees versus 8.6% for FFS, with 18.2 versus 20.4 visits per user, and MA is about 55% of Medicare enrollment (Trella). That implies MA is about 52% of visits, so $13-16B if MA pays 80-95% of FFS per visit. The per-visit payment ratio is my assumption.
- **12,234** Medicare-certified agencies in 2024 (MedPAC), about 1,100 in Florida. Average MA revenue per agency is about $1.1M; the median is far lower.
- **Reversible share.** The idea assumes 3% of MA revenue. KFF's 67% overturn rate is among appealed denials only, which are the strongest cases. For never-appealed marginal denials I assume 30-40%. Using 12% denials (all services; home health unknown), half worth appealing and 35% overturned gives about 2%; the plausible range is 1-3%.
- **Pool.** 1-3% of $14B is $140-420M recovered; a 25% fee is **$35-105M a year nationally**, before large chains (Amedisys, Enhabit, UnitedHealth's LHC, Aveanna) that build or buy their own. Their share of MA revenue is unverified.
- **Per agency.** About 2% of $1.1M is $23K recovered, so about $5.7K of contingency fee, plus perhaps $1.5K of packet fees: **roughly $5-8K a year per average agency**, against a $42-58K loaded cost for the coordinator the agency already employs (Payscale: $18-22 an hour).
- **$10M a year** therefore needs about 1,250-2,000 paying agencies (10-16% of all certified agencies), or 10-30% of the fee pool. The lessons file calls anything over 20% heroic.
- **The idea's "$420M recovered" and "$105M pool" are the top of the range**, not the base case.

### 5. Reachability: good for the first 5-10, thin after
- A family agency is the pilot, and the idea assumes 2-4 peers via the grandfather and 4 MA-heavy Florida owners via an association. The Home Care Association of Florida (HCAF) runs the state's HomeCareCon (the 2026 edition was 21-24 July in Orlando, so the next is probably mid-2027). Florida has about 1,100 assessed agencies and is the state MedPAC calls out for decline.
- The mentor's AV-integration network has no home health overlap. It is the weakest of the three channels; use him for investors and hospital executives, not agency owners.
- **MHC Healthcare is still unverified.** Directory listings (low-quality) show "MHC Area 6 LLC" in Riverview and "MHC Area 10 LLC" in Fort Lauderdale as Medicare-certified home health agencies. If MHC is FFS-heavy or some other type, the pilot loses its data. Confirm with Care Compare and the AHCA license lookup before building anything.
- Claim Health already lists Interim HealthCare as a customer, so franchisees of that brand are contested ground.

### 6. Legal and age: clearable, with two real frictions
- **Age:** none. He is 18 and can form an LLC, sign BAAs and take payment.
- **HIPAA:** every agency needs a BAA, and the LLM vendor needs a sub-BAA. Dates of service are identifiers, so an index must compute durations locally and ship only non-identifying intervals. Budget about $1.5-3K for counsel and $1-2K a year for cyber and E&O insurance.
- **Standing (real friction):** 42 CFR 422.574 makes the enrollee, an assignee provider who waives patient payment, or an entity with an "appealable interest" a party to an organization determination. 422.578 gives the treating **physician** (not the agency) the right to request a pre-service reconsideration on the enrollee's behalf, with notice to the enrollee. Plans can dismiss requests from non-parties. The "provider-side only" design therefore means drafting packets for a physician to sign, or using the plan's peer-to-peer and provider-dispute routes. That adds a physician step and weakens the "auto-draft appeals" promise. Contracted-provider payment disputes also run through the contract, not Medicare appeals.
- **Contingency fee:** I found no guidance either way on percentage fees for MA appeals. General billing companies charge 4-10% of collections, so 20-25% is high and will draw pushback. Have counsel review; keep a flat-fee fallback.
- **Publishing a plan scorecard:** defamation and payer-contract confidentiality risk. A pre-registered, methodology-first index limits it, but needs a lawyer's read of the first release.

## Numbers
- Cost to first revenue: about $4,500 (counsel and BAA templates, insurance, HIPAA-eligible hosting; the idea says $3,000).
- Weeks to first revenue: about 10 for a token fee from the family agency, 12-16 for the first non-family payer.
- Median case at maturity: about $0.4M a year (about 40 agencies at $6K, plus a little data revenue).
- Upside: about $8M a year (about 700 agencies plus data licenses to hospital networks, ACOs and investors). That still misses $10M unless the data layer becomes a network.

## Why weak, not kill, and not strong
- **Not strong:** criteria 5 and 6 fail. It is the first idea anyone would have and four funded startups sell near-identical work, so it is crowded. The fee pool is too small for $10M without heroic share.
- **Not kill:** the buyer is reachable, there is no license or age wall, the problem is documented (OIG, KFF, LeadingAge), and the home-health-level, contractor-level measurement gap is real and acknowledged by OIG.
- **What fails:** selling "authorization tracking plus appeal drafting" against Claim Health and Silna from a standing start, with an 18-year-old at 10-15 hours a week.

## Sharpened version (does not shrink it)
**Clock Index.** Lead with measurement, not appeals. Free 30-day clock audit of the last 90 days of an agency's authorizations, computed locally into plan-by-contractor decision times, visits requested versus approved, and denial reasons. Each agency gets its own benchmark in return for contributing the non-identifying intervals. The product is the first home-health-level MA authorization index, published quarterly with a pre-registered method. Appeals become a partner or upsell (physician-signed, flat fee), not the core.

**Gate before building anything beyond the audit (6 weeks):**
1. Confirm what MHC Healthcare is and how much MA census it has.
2. Run the audit at the family agency and 4 peers. Kill if fewer than 3 of 5 can export submit and decision timestamps.
3. Kill if measured clock misses or short approvals are too rare to tell a story.
4. Take 10 calls with hospital post-acute teams, ACOs, home health investors and HCAF. Kill if none will name a price for the index.
