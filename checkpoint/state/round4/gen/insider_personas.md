## 1. Contract Truth: managed-care underpayment and authorization-leak audit for home health

**Persona and earned secret (home-health biller).** "We chase denials all day because denials are visible. Underpayments are invisible. Every Medicare Advantage plan and Medicaid managed-care plan pays off its own rate sheet, the contract is a PDF nobody re-reads, remits get posted as 'paid vs billed', not 'paid vs contract', and visits that run past the authorized units or dates get written off without anyone noticing."

- **Customer:** owner or CFO of a Medicare-certified home health agency, at least $2M revenue, with 25%+ managed-care mix. Florida has a lot of these.
- **Who pays and budget:** the agency, out of recovered dollars. Flat diagnostic of $2,500 (12 months of remits vs. contract), then 20-25% of recovered underpayments, then $500-$1,500 a month for an authorization watchdog. No existing budget line is needed because it is self-funding. The owner or CFO signs.
- **Problem:** MA plans pay below traditional Medicare, MA volume has passed fee-for-service in home health, and nobody systematically checks payment against contract or visits against authorizations.
- **Solution:** ingest 835/ERA remits (strip patient names, keep claim control numbers), contract PDFs (LLM extracts rate tables), and an authorization log export. Flag: paid not equal to contracted rate; visits outside authorized units or dates; timely-filing and appeal windows about to close; patterns by plan to escalate to the plan's provider rep. Output is a claim-level recovery file with dispute letters. Phase 2 is a real-time watchdog that tells schedulers "visit 7 is not authorized, request an extension today."
- **Why now:** MA now outweighs fee-for-service volume (Trella), federal scrutiny of MA denials is up (OIG June 2026, Senate letter July 2026), and the moratorium means agencies cannot grow by opening branches, so they have to protect margin.
- **Founder edge:** the freight-invoice audit system he already built is the same engine (rate contract vs. invoice vs. recovery, flat diagnostic plus contingency). Mom inside MHC can find the biller and CFO on day one.
- **First 10 customers:** (1) Ask Grandfather for one MHC agency, sign a BAA and NDA, run a free 90-day look-back on one MA plan. (2) Convert that to contingency once the recovery file is real. (3) Add the other MHC provider numbers (3 in total if the directory is right). (4) Two peers via Grandfather's owner circle and the state home-care association. (5) One third-party billing company that serves 20-100 agencies is a channel worth 20+ customers; offer them free look-backs and a revenue share.
- **Cost to first revenue:** about $2,000 (LLC, healthcare attorney to review a BAA template, HIPAA-eligible cloud and LLM terms).
- **Path to big (bottom-up, assumptions):** about 11,000 Medicare-certified agencies (verify with CMS); managed-care share 40-50% of revenue (assumption; Trella only says MA volume passed FFS); typical agency revenue $3-5M, so $1.2-2.5M managed-care revenue; leakage 2-5% (assumption) gives $25-100k a year recoverable per agency; fee of 20-25% gives $5-25k per agency-year. **$10M needs about 700-1,500 agencies, which is 6-13% of all agencies**, so this is a stretch, not a given. $100M needs the same engine pointed at every post-acute and ancillary provider that bills managed-care plans (outpatient therapy, DME, behavioral health, SNF Part B, home infusion). The authorization watchdog is the software layer that makes revenue recurring, and cross-agency payer behavior data is the asset.
- **Who does it today:** Claim Health (YC X25: AI billing, appeals, claims it recovered "denied or underpaid" revenue, six-figure self-reported); Aegis and Covered Health (generic appeals); outsourced billing companies (paid on % of collections, they chase denials, not contract compliance); hospital-side underpayment-recovery vendors (general knowledge; none verified for home health). I found no home-health-specific, contract-compliance-first product, but "I did not find it" is weaker than "it does not exist."
- **Legal premise to check first:** Medicare restricts billing agents paid as a percentage of collections they receive (42 CFR 424.73; Medicaid has a similar rule at 42 CFR 447.10; verify both with counsel). Stay out of traditional-Medicare claims, never touch the money (agency's own bank account only), invoice recoveries separately. HIPAA: BAA, minimum necessary, no patient names.
- **If MHC is hospice-only:** this idea shrinks, because MA hospice is carved out to fee-for-service. Switch to ideas 3 and 4.

## 2. Agency Integrity Report: claims-integrity diligence and sell-side readiness for the moratorium deal wave

**Persona and earned secret (Medicare enrollment / RCD specialist).** "When you buy an agency you do not just buy a license, you buy its past. CMS can offset the seller's overpayments against your payments, R&W insurers carve out billing, and the buyer's consultant samples thirty charts and calls it diligence. Sellers go in blind and get repriced at the eleventh hour."

- **Customer:** (a) owner-operators weighing a sale, (b) regional operators and PE-backed platforms buying agencies, (c) M&A advisors and healthcare counsel who need data work done, (d) lenders and R&W brokers as secondary buyers.
- **Who pays and budget:** sellers pay $5-10k for a pre-sale integrity audit, paid out of expected proceeds. Buyers pay $10-25k, inside a diligence budget that already exists in every deal (my estimate of price). The owner (sell-side) or the deal lead and counsel (buy-side) sign.
- **Problem:** the May 13, 2026 nationwide moratorium on new home health and hospice enrollments (extension decision due about mid-November) turned existing certified agencies into scarce assets, and deal volume is up. Buyers inherit overpayment exposure; sellers cannot prove their books are clean.
- **Solution:** a standardized, AI-assisted package delivered in five business days: random chart-to-claim sample tested against OASIS, face-to-face, orders and PDGM grouping; extrapolated error rate and repayment range with a confidence interval; Review Choice affirmation trend; ADR/TPE history; star-rating trend; payer concentration and MA contract exposure; 36-month-rule check; clone-note screen (idea 4). Engage through the seller's or buyer's healthcare counsel so findings sit inside privilege and the 60-day overpayment clock is handled by a lawyer, not by Asher.
- **Why now:** moratorium plus deal acceleration (Braff: certified home health +41%, hospice +57% pace), plus the R&W market loosening for buyers who can show thorough billing diligence.
- **Founder edge:** multi-agent research pipelines map directly onto chart-review agents; text-forensics skill from the detector; mentor (built and sold in a roll-up-heavy industry) can intro M&A advisors; the family company is either a prospective buyer or knows who is selling.
- **First 10 customers:** (1) Run the free "mirror test" on one MHC agency (useful to MHC and gives an anonymized case study). (2) Ask Grandfather who in his circle is selling or buying. (3) Bring the case study to three or four home-health M&A advisors and healthcare law firms, offering the first deal free in exchange for a reference. (4) Mentor intros to deal people. Expect 2 family-circle deals, 3 via advisors, 5 via counsel.
- **Cost to first revenue:** about $5,000 (E&O insurance quote, counsel-reviewed engagement template, compute).
- **Path to big, honestly:** home-based-care deals alone run in the low hundreds a year (Braff 134 annualized), so even at $15k each and a huge share that is a $2-5M business, a boutique. **It is only big if it generalizes** to healthcare-services M&A broadly (physician groups, DME, labs, behavioral health, SNF, dialysis, thousands of deals a year at $10-25k) and becomes a standard that R&W insurers, lenders and PE accept ("Integrity Score"). That needs a partner with credibility; enterprise buyers will not trust an 18-year-old cold. If it cannot get that partner, call it a boutique and kill it.
- **Who does it today:** accounting and consulting firms, regional healthcare compliance consultancies (SimiTree and similar), law-firm compliance reviewers, and billing and coding auditors, all human-sampled and slow. I found no standardized AI-native integrity product for agency deals.
- **Legal premise:** the report must not create a "known overpayment" the client then ignores; use counsel-directed engagement (Kovel-style) and say plainly that it is not legal advice. This is exactly round 1's "privileged number that defeats its own privilege" trap.

## 3. One Inbox: cross-agency physician orders network (text-link signing, batch signing, CPO billing help)

**Persona and earned secret (intake coordinator and biller).** "Unsigned orders are why final claims sit. We chase the same forty doctors by fax. Each of our agencies, and every hospice and DME company, has its own portal, and the doctor ignores all of them."

- **Customer:** administrators and billing managers at agencies, hospices and DME suppliers; the second side is certifying physicians and their staff.
- **Who pays and budget:** the agency pays $300-$900 a month, or $1-2 per signed order. The cash math (assumption): a $5M agency that cuts 10 days off its collection cycle frees about $137k once, and a 0.5% cut in write-offs is about $25k a year. The budget is the A/R and write-off line; the owner or billing manager signs.
- **Problem:** final claims wait for signed orders; signatures turn into days sales outstanding and, if the doctor never signs, write-offs.
- **Solution:** the doctor gets one text link that opens a single phone-friendly queue of every pending order from every participating agency. Batch sign with identity verification and a full audit trail. Agencies get a dashboard of turnaround by physician and claim-hold dollars. No deep EHR integration at the start: orders arrive as forwarded PDFs or CSV exports and AI parses and routes them. Phase 2 helps doctors capture care plan oversight (G0181) and certification billing, so they earn something for the work. Phase 3 extends to hospice, DME written orders and SNF.
- **Why now:** the moratorium makes each existing agency's cash cycle more valuable; AI fax and PDF parsing is now cheap; physicians are drowning in portals.
- **Founder edge:** speed of build; Mom can say which doctors are slowest. Starting inside MHC's several agencies gives density from day one, because the same doctors sign for all of them.
- **First 10 customers:** the MHC provider numbers as one network; then agencies in the same markets (Tampa Bay, Broward, Palm Beach) who share referral doctors. Make it doctor-led: any physician already using the inbox gets their other agencies added free.
- **Cost to first revenue:** about $5,000 (HIPAA hosting, Twilio/SMS, e-signature compliance review, counsel).
- **Path to big:** about 11,000 agencies plus about 6,500 hospices (verify), at about $6k a year. 15% penetration is about $16M. **$100M needs the network to extend to DME, labs, SNF and payer notices, to be the "order exchange" standard for post-acute.** Network effects are the moat; chicken-and-egg adoption is the risk.
- **Who does it today:** every EHR's own physician portal (Axxess, HCHB, WellSky; general knowledge), Enzo Health's order-management module, DocuSign plus fax, and staff paid to chase signatures. I found no neutral multi-agency physician network, but EHR vendors can block integrations and may copy the idea.
- **Legal premise:** Medicare electronic-signature authentication rules (verify against the Program Integrity Manual), BAAs with every agency; physician disclosures for treatment and payment are permitted.
- **If MHC is hospice, private-duty or DME:** works for hospice (G0182) and DME written orders; for private duty only the physician side applies.

## 4. Note Forensics: clone and authorship-integrity audit for clinical documentation in the AI-scribe era

**Persona and earned secret (RCD / compliance specialist).** "The day a reviewer sees the same sentence in four hundred visit notes, it is over. AI scribes will write beautiful, identical notes and we will not know until the audit letter."

- **Customer:** compliance officer, administrator or DON at home health and hospice agencies; PE-backed platforms with dozens of agencies; later AI-scribe vendors and payers' special-investigation units.
- **Who pays and budget:** $2,500 flat diagnostic (200 charts), then $750-$2,000 a month for monitoring; platform licenses of $50-150k a year for multi-agency owners (my estimate). Budget is the QA and compliance line (a QA nurse costs far more). The administrator or owner signs.
- **Problem:** Florida agencies are inside the Review Choice Demonstration with a 90% affirmation bar; note cloning is a named auditor red flag; AI scribes raise the odds of look-alike notes and unclear authorship.
- **Solution:** apply the detector approach he already proved (read the gestalt, normalize formatting, compare drafts): cross-patient and cross-clinician similarity, within-chart copy-forward, template saturation, vocabulary-drift signals that suggest machine authorship, missing visit-specific facts, impossible timestamps and visit-length vs. mileage mismatches. Output is a chart integrity score and a per-clinician coaching list, run monthly.
- **Why now:** HCHB and Enzo are shipping scribes; audit targeting is increasingly data-driven; the CY2027 proposed rule adds revocation grounds. I could not find an OIG or UPIC statement specifically about AI-written home health notes, so this is an inference.
- **Founder edge:** his AI-text detector beat commercial tools in his own blind tests, and `compare_drafts.py` already handles "human draft vs. AI rewrite." That is the rarest asset in this lens.
- **First 10 customers:** MHC pilot (frame as "coaching and readiness", not an overpayment audit); peers through Grandfather; PE-backed platforms through the mentor; billing and QA outsourcers as resellers.
- **Cost to first revenue:** about $3,000.
- **Path to big:** provider-side is a few thousand agencies and hospices at $5-30k. The real prize is a cross-vertical "authenticity layer for clinical documentation" sold to payers' fraud units, hospital systems and PE portfolios, plus behavioral health and ABA. That is venture-shaped but needs enterprise credibility he does not have yet.
- **Who does it today:** Brellium (AI documentation auditing for home health, hospice and behavioral health), Enzo's QA tool, EHR audit modules, SimiTree-style QA outsourcing. **This is the most crowded of the home-health ideas**; the differentiator is authorship and clone forensics, not completeness checking. I did not verify that nobody offers forensics.
- **Legal premise:** a retrospective audit that finds overpayments starts the 60-day clock. Offer a prospective coaching and readiness product, deliver sensitive findings through counsel, and never promise a "defense."

## 5. Heir Apparent: next-gen onboarding for business-owner families, sold through RIAs

**Persona and earned secret (RIA founder and CCO).** "We lose the kids at the funeral. The parents love us, the kids never met us. We cannot text them casually (recordkeeping), we cannot market to them (Marketing Rule), and our onboarding deck was built for sixty-two-year-olds."

- **Customer:** RIAs and multi-family offices whose clients are business-owning families, including healthcare-agency owners like his own family. The end users are adult children, 21+ by design.
- **Who pays and budget:** the advisory firm. Pilot $2,500 for three to five client families; then $3-8k setup plus $100-300 per family per year (my estimate). Budget: client-retention, marketing and next-gen programs. An advisor with $100M under management earns about $1M a year at a 1% fee, so one retained heir household justifies the spend. The firm owner signs.
- **Problem:** heir attrition numbers vary (66% in an InvestmentNews adviser survey, 70%+ quoted second-hand from Cerulli, lower recent figures for ultra-wealthy heirs under 40), but nobody disputes that advisors rarely know the kids, and 82% of heirs under 40 want in-person meetings.
- **Solution:** a parent-invited, advisor-branded "Family Wealth Room" plus a ten-session family-meeting kit: interactive education (how the family's structure works, compounding and drawdown, inheritance taxes, business-ownership scenarios), simulators built from his Strategy Lab and play-money economy mechanics, compliance-reviewed content, archived communications, and an engagement score that flags heirs at risk of leaving. Education only; the advisor gives advice.
- **Why now:** the intergenerational transfer is under way, advisors are explicitly worried, and the sample of products aimed at the heir (not the advisor's client) is thin in what I found.
- **Founder edge:** he is the third-generation heir in a business-owning family and an actual 18-year-old; Dad has 24 years inside the advisor world; the family's own succession is a live test.
- **First 10 customers:** Dad's peers who run wealth practices; the family's own advisor; Maryland RIAs through the Annapolis network; FPA and NAPFA chapter events. One free pilot for a case study.
- **Cost to first revenue:** about $3,000.
- **Path to big:** about 15,000 SEC-registered RIAs plus MFOs, trust companies and bank wealth units. 1,500 firms at $20k is $30M; $100M needs about 5,000 firms (heroic share, over 20% of RIAs, unless banks and trust companies buy). Real risk: advisors buy slowly, and "education" is hard to price.
- **Who does it today:** next-gen consultants and in-house programs at large firms; client portals from eMoney and Envestnet; Savvy Wealth competes as an advisory firm for heirs. My search did not surface a dedicated heir-engagement platform.
- **Guardrails:** adults only; no investment advice from the platform; parent consent before any heir data is used (Reg S-P).

## 6. Festival Buyers Club: group purchasing with savings-share for independent festivals, then every community event

**Persona and earned secret (festival production manager).** "I pay retail for toilets, fencing, power, wristbands, cups and insurance because I am one five-thousand-person event and the toilet-trailer guy knows I cannot walk. Five bluegrass festivals buy the same things from the same three vendors in the same months and none of us compare notes."

- **Customer:** directors of independent music festivals (start with bluegrass and roots via the IBMA world), then fairs, races, markets and college events.
- **Who pays and budget:** free to join; the club takes 20% of verified savings, then a 2-5% supplier admin fee on pooled volume once volume exists. Purchasing lines (production, rentals, sanitation, security, insurance, printing) already exist and are large. The festival director or board signs.
- **Solution:** a "invoice autopsy": members send last year's ten biggest invoices; AI normalizes line items, benchmarks against regional bids, rebids top categories with multi-event vendor agreements, and negotiates pooled insurance, payment-processing and ticketing rates. A shared vendor scorecard (no-shows, quality) and a certificate-of-insurance and permit vault ride along.
- **Why now:** informal festival alliances are being written up as a way to cut costs (Ticket Fairy, Oct 2025); no dedicated festival co-op turned up in search.
