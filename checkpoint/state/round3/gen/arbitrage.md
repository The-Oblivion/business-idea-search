# Round 3 / Lens: Arbitrage and reselling with an AI edge (ranked best first)

Date of research: 2026-10-25. About 22 web searches and 5 page fetches. Vendor pages are marketing, so every vendor figure below is marked as such. Anything I could not source is labelled "assumption" or "unverified".

## What the research said in one paragraph

The big, loud arbitrage pools of 2026 are already being worked. The IEEPA tariff-refund wave is mostly gone: CBP's CAPE portal had accepted declarations covering 25.1M entries and certified roughly $100B to Treasury by 31 July 2026, and brokers, carriers and law firms run it. Consumer class-action claim apps already have funded players. Photo-to-resale-value apps are saturated. What is still thin is **friction arbitrage in the middle**: money that is owed or inventory that is mispriced, where the claim or the listing costs more in human time than a small business will spend, and where AI makes the cost near zero. The best ideas below sit there. They are also mostly **not software for strangers**. They are an audit or catalogue that speaks for itself, with the price taken as a success fee or revenue share, so his age does not matter.

Overlap warning: sibling lens files already hold TikTok-Shop money-back, micro-brand deduction recovery, micro-importer customs desk and small-business settlement claims. I deliberately did not duplicate those.

## 1. Co-op Cash Autopilot (unclaimed manufacturer co-op, MDF and dealer-incentive money for contractors and independent dealers)

**One-liner.** A free 24-hour audit that finds the manufacturer co-op money a contractor or dealer already earned and never claimed, then files it for about 20% of what actually gets paid.

**Customer / who pays.** Owner-operators of HVAC, roofing, window and other trade contractors, plus independent dealers (RV, marine, powersports, furniture, appliance) who buy branded product and already spend on Google, Meta, Yelp, truck wraps and websites. The owner signs. Budget line: marketing spend already incurred. The money itself comes out of the manufacturer's co-op budget. Fee: 15-25% of reimbursed dollars only, optional ~$99/month monitoring after he has proved it works.

**Problem (sourced).**
- A Local Search Association white paper (reported by MediaPost, Nov 2016) estimated $36-70B of annual US manufacturer co-op, of which roughly $14-35B goes unclaimed. A Borrell Associates figure quoted by the contractor agency Socius puts unused co-op at $14B a year in North America, about 40% of available money.
- A Manta/LSA survey in the same piece: 43% of US small businesses sell national brands that may qualify, but only 42% of those used co-op.
- Stated causes: inconsistent manufacturer procedures, heavy paperwork, confusing rules, low awareness, and a process "so murky and labor intensive" that owners give up. ACCA's overview notes pre-approval is required for several media types, and unused balances are usually forfeited at year end.
- Caveat: these are 2016 numbers from trade groups. Treat as directional. The first 20 audits will replace them with a real figure.

**Solution.** Build a rulebook database of the top ~20 trade programs (start with the ten manufacturers ACCA summarises, plus Owens Corning, GAF, Pella). The owner uploads or connects read-only: ad invoices, card exports, distributor purchase statements. An AI agent maps each expense to each program's eligible categories, flags missing pre-approvals, assembles the proof-of-performance pack (invoices, screenshots, creative compliance), tracks deadlines and filing status. The owner presses submit, or grants delegated portal access. Never fabricate or "improve" proof.

**Why now.** A model can now read 30-page program PDFs, messy invoices and ad screenshots for pennies. That made a $400 claim not worth a human before. Manufacturers are slowly opening digital co-op. Contractors' margins are squeezed by 2025-26 price increases, so "free money" lands.

**Founder edge.** He has already built a diagnostic-plus-contingency audit pipeline (freight invoices). Same structure: ingest documents, check against rules, dispute, take a percentage. The offer does not require trust in his age: it is free until money arrives. Family business contacts for supply-house introductions.

**Who does it today (searched).** Socius Marketing (contractor-focused co-op agency; pricing not published), Co-op Connect by Localogy (concierge research of co-op funds), Mediagistic and Vistex (co-op/MDF software sold to manufacturers, enterprise), Enable (partner-fund management), plus general marketing agencies and manufacturers' own reps. I found **no self-serve, success-fee product for the small contractor**. Caveat: I could not see Socius or Co-op Connect pricing; an agency could already be doing exactly this at 15-20%.

**First 10 customers.** Week 1: build the rulebook for six programs and the claim-pack generator. Week 2-3: pick one metro and two trades. Phone and walk in on 80 contractors from Google Maps: "In 24 hours I will tell you exactly how much unclaimed Carrier/Trane/Owens Corning co-op you have. You pay 20% only after the manufacturer pays you." Ask for 12 months of ad invoices and the distributor purchase summary. Parallel channel: supply-house counter managers (they benefit when contractors buy more branded product), local ACCA and roofing association meetings, owner Facebook groups. Target 10 signed audits by week 4, first reimbursements 30-90 days after filing. Offer a $149 flat "claim pack" to anyone who wants to file himself, which gives cash inside 60 days.

**Cost to first revenue.** About $500 (Florida-style LLC filing is ~$125, domain, Stripe, a small ad test).

**Path to big (bottom-up, assumptions flagged).** Reachable: ~100k home-service contractors plus ~30k independent dealers = 130k (assumption). Recoverable per customer: $6k a year (assumption; replace with audit data). That is $780M a year recoverable and a ~$156M fee pool at 20%. $10M a year of revenue needs ~8k customers at ~$1.2k average fee, about 6% of the reachable base. The route to $100M: add MDF/channel incentives for IT and AV resellers (mentor), add dealer warranty chargebacks, then sell claim automation to manufacturers (they want claims paid on time and cheaply). Heroic only if he stays a contractor-only agency.

**Biggest risks / kill test.** (a) Co-op requires qualifying spend and pre-approval; small contractors may have little qualifying spend. (b) Some programs prohibit third-party filers; get written client authorisation and use delegated access. (c) An agency copies the offer. Kill test: sign three contractors in week 1 and get their real accrual balances from their reps. If the median claimable amount is under about $1,500, stop.

## 2. Contents Desk (remote-first AI cataloguing and sale of house contents: estate sale companies first, heirs second, inherited collections as the beachhead)

**One-liner.** A phone video of a house becomes an itemised, priced catalogue ready to sell, built for the 35%-commission estate sale industry and for the heirs who have to clear a parent's house.

**Customer / who pays.** Wedge: estate sale companies and auction houses (pay per sale, $99-299, or 3-5% of gross). Second step: heirs and executors paying a 25-30% commission, below the going 35-40%, because remote AI removes the labour. Beachhead for heirs: inherited collections (cards, coins, comics, vinyl, LEGO, video games), where good price databases exist and one photo of a binder is worth thousands.

**Problem (sourced).** Estate liquidators typically take 30-40% of proceeds, averaging about 37% in an EstateSales.NET survey (2021); flat-fee alternatives run $1,500-5,000; whole-house clean-outs for a 2-3 bedroom home are quoted $1,500-4,500 (vendor-published). Cataloguing and pricing a house is days of skilled labour. EstateSales.NET sells company subscriptions ($225/month for Gold), so these are real businesses with money.

**Solution.** Walk-through video or photos in; vision model segments rooms and lots; each lot gets identification, comps from eBay/auction/price databases, a description, a reserve or start price, and a route (auction, eBay, consign, donate with receipt, dispose). For estate companies the output drops straight into their listing platform. Later a heir-facing brand with local pickers and haulers as subcontractors.

**Why now.** The Great Wealth Transfer is under way (boomers at 70-80). Goodwill has shipped an AI listing tool built with Microsoft that lists about 130-140k items a month, which proves the technology works at scale, but it is captive. ShopGoodwill GMV hit about $450M last year (+22%), showing demand for online secondhand is growing fast.

**Founder edge.** Multi-agent pipelines and a vision-to-structured-data workflow are exactly what he ships. Gen Z distribution: grandchildren are often the people actually doing clear-outs, and TikTok-style "what is grandma's house really worth" content is native to him. Family network for the first few houses.

**Who does it today (searched).** Thousands of local estate sale companies; Caring Transitions (franchise network of senior-move and estate-sale services); Everything But The House (online estate auctions; I only found 2015-16 coverage, so status unverified); EstateSales.NET (listing platform, not a valuation tool). Photo-scan apps exist (Flippr, Underpriced AI, Price Snap, ThriftAid, Resell AI) but scan one item or one pile for the buyer, not a whole house for the seller. I found **no AI estate-contents appraisal startup** (not conclusive).

**First 10 customers.** Pull estate sale companies within 100 miles from EstateSales.NET. Pitch: "Send me photos of your next sale. I return a priced catalogue in 24 hours. Pay $199 if it saves you a day." Backtest first: run the engine on photos of past sales (public), compare predictions to realised prices, and show the error. Ten companies at one sale each is roughly $2k and 10 case studies.

**Cost to first revenue.** About $900.

**Path to big (my estimate, not sourced).** About 2.8M US deaths a year; assume 5% (140k) end in a paid contents liquidation, average gross $8k = $1.1B gross, ~$415M of commissions at 37%. $10M a year of revenue is roughly 2.5% of that pool. Add downsizing moves and thrift/charity supply (idea 8) for a larger pool. Honest limit: the heir-facing brand needs local labour; scale comes from a network of subcontracted pickers.

**Risks.** State auctioneer, consignment and secondhand-dealer licensing varies. An AI estimate is not a USPAP appraisal; never call it one. No firearms, no fiduciary custody until he has insurance. Market sensitively to the bereaved; sell B2B first.

## 3. Installed-Base Buyer (AI-maintained acquirer of orphaned extensions, plugins and bots: buy users for pennies)

**One-liner.** Buy abandoned browser extensions, plugins and bots that still have tens of thousands of users, fix them in a day with coding agents, and monetise the installed base he did not have to market for.

**Customer / who pays.** End users on freemium upgrades ($3-5/month). Sellers are solo developers happy to take $1-10k.

**The arbitrage.** (assumption) buy an installed base for roughly $0.02-0.20 per user versus $1-3 per install to acquire users with paid ads, a 10-50x gap.

**Sourced facts.** A researcher scraped the Chrome Web Store and found 84,504 outdated extensions with a combined 426M users, triggered by the Manifest V2 deprecation. A commercial database, Chrome Goldmine, lists 9,656 expired extensions with revenue estimates and rebuild guides. A Flippa listing sold the source, Web Store listing and branding for an ask of $2,000 against about $250 total gross sales and 18 customers: the market is thin and most assets earn little. Community advice: the 12-24 months after abandonment is the sweet spot. Risks cited: users trust the original developer, and permission-heavy extensions draw store-review scrutiny. (I could not open the original IndieHackers post, HTTP 403, so details come from search summaries.)

**Solution.** Acquisition criteria: at least 50k active users, last update more than 12 months ago or Manifest-V2-broken, no invasive permissions, clean reviews, a utility category (PDF tools, screenshots, tab managers, dark mode, productivity) rather than coupons. Offer $1-5k via escrow. Port to Manifest V3 and fix bugs in days with agents, announce the ownership change openly in the listing and privacy policy, add a Pro tier and one AI feature, cross-promote between assets. Other supply: WordPress plugins, Shopify apps, Obsidian/VS Code/Figma plugins, Discord bots (all unverified in this research).

**Why now.** Manifest V2 shut-offs created tens of thousands of broken-but-installed assets. A migration that cost developers weeks now costs an agent hours.

**Founder edge.** Shipping real deployed software fast is his core skill. Gen Z distribution helps with Discord/Minecraft/Roblox-adjacent tools.

**Who does it today.** Brokers (Flippa, Acquire.com) and data sellers (Chrome Goldmine) serve the market; generalist small-internet acquirers exist. I **did not find** a 2026 AI-native roll-up of orphaned extensions, but I could not rule out a stealth one.

**First 10 customers.** Buy one extension with ~50-200k users by day ~30. Relaunch with a fix by day ~40; paywall by day ~50. At 100k users and ~1% paid at $3/month that is about 1,000 payers and $3k MRR, so the first ten arrive within days of the paywall.

**Cost to first revenue.** About $5k (two assets, developer account, escrow fees).

**Path to big.** One asset: roughly $18-90k ARR at 100k users and 0.5-1.5% paid. $10M ARR needs about 100-500 such assets, or a handful of breakouts, or selling a bundled user base. Comps for value of extension distribution exist (e.g. shopping extensions sold for billions) but most assets will be worth little. This is a portfolio bet with fat-tail upside, not a sure thing.

**Risks.** Chrome Web Store ownership-transfer rules; a long history of extension takeovers being abused for adware, so any hint of data harvesting or injected affiliate links kills trust and the account. Do privacy-clean monetisation only; follow CCPA/GDPR on change of controller.

## 4. Pallet Truth (manifest-to-margin underwriting for pallet buyers and bin stores)

**One-liner.** Paste a liquidation manifest and get a bid ceiling, expected net and confidence range, backed by a database of what pallets really netted.

**Customer / who pays.** Pallet flippers, flea-market vendors, eBay/Whatnot sellers, and bin-store owners buying truckloads from B-Stock, Liquidation.com, Amazon and retailer return programs. $39-149/month; the pallet itself is the budget line, since a bad pallet costs $500-5,000.

**Sourced facts.** Liquidity Services (Liquidation.com, GovDeals) reported FY2026 Q1-Q3 GMV of $398M, $389.9M and a record $453.0M (about $1.24B over nine months, my sum) and revenue of about $371M over the same nine months; consignment is 83% of GMV. Existing tools: B-Stock Buddy (free Chrome extension, ~93 users), an Auction Arbitrage Overlay extension for B-Stock Costco/Target lots, Closo (a marketplace/analytics vendor whose content is marketing). Advice in the sources: add at least 15% defect allowance and freight; manifests can be wrong; most units are C/D grade. I found **no tool that does end-to-end AI manifest-to-profit with learned outcomes**.

**Solution.** Resolve UPCs, pull sold comps and sell-through velocity per channel, apply category-specific defect and return-grade priors, subtract freight, fees and labour, output max bid and downside. The moat is a feedback loop: users report what each pallet actually netted, building a proprietary recovery-rate database by source, grade and category.

**Why now.** Record liquidation volume, AI manifest parsing now cheap, a boom in bin stores and live-selling resellers hungry for inventory.

**Founder edge.** Backtesting/strategy-lab instincts (calibrated forecasts with error bars) and fast shipping. He buys two pallets himself ($600-1,500) to create ground truth and content.

**Who does it today.** The tools above; Liquidity Services and B-Stock marketplaces themselves; general reseller software.

**First 10 customers.** Reddit and Facebook pallet-flipper groups, pallet-unboxing YouTubers (run their next pallet free, they publish the result), and bin-store owners. A single creator video can deliver ten subscribers in a month.

**Cost to first revenue.** About $1,200.

**Path to big.** A tool alone tops out around $10-30M ARR (e.g. 40k payers at $40 a month), not $100M. The route is the transaction layer: the pricing standard becomes a marketplace take-rate or a buy-side aggregator. Liquidity Services shows the scale of that layer (~$490M revenue run-rate), but also that it is a hard, incumbent-held business.

**Risks.** Scraping terms: build as a user-side extension. Free substitutes (spreadsheets). Data advantage takes months.

## 5. Statement Cop (distributor-statement auditor for independent auto shops, then other parts-heavy trades)

**One-liner.** Upload last month's parts-distributor statements and returns log; get a list of core charges, warranty credits and price errors the shop is owed, and a pre-written dispute for each.

**Customer / who pays.** Independent auto repair shop owners (and later HVAC, plumbing, marine, powersports, ag-equipment parts counters). 25% of recovered credits plus about $99/month monitoring.

**Sourced facts (vendor).** WickedFile estimates a shop that has never audited core charges may lose $5-20k a year and cites a $14k missing-vendor-credit recovery at a transmission franchise; core charges range from $25 for a caliper to over $1,000 for electronics; if the old unit never goes back the supplier keeps the deposit. These are vendor marketing numbers. The leak is partly physical (cores binned) and partly clerical (credits never posted).

**Solution.** AI reads PDF/portal statements from NAPA, O'Reilly, Worldpac, Parts Authority and others, reconciles against shop invoices and a simple core scan log, ages open cores against each distributor's return window, flags credits issued versus owed and price discrepancies, then drafts disputes. Same engine as his freight-audit pipeline.

**Who does it today.** WickedFile (core-charge and vendor-credit recovery software for shops); shop-management systems with parts ordering integrations (not recovery specialists). I did not find a pure statement-reconciliation competitor, so that is the wedge.

**First 10 customers.** Owner Facebook groups, independent-shop associations, cold calls to 60 shops: free 60-minute statement audit. Customers pay only from recovered credits.

**Cost to first revenue.** About $300.

**Path to big.** Roughly a quarter-million US repair shops (unverified). If 50k are addressable at $3k realistic recoverable (assumption, below the vendor's $5-20k) and 25% fee, that is a ~$37M pool. Reaching $100M needs adjacent trades and selling reconciliation to distributors who want fewer disputes. This is the weakest on "big" among the top 5; a solid cash engine rather than a venture bet. Call it dead if audit data shows under $1,000 recoverable per shop.

## 6. Refresh Desk (mentor-network AV and IT surplus: buy decommissioned room systems and servers, resell, and build trade-in quoting for integrators)

**One-liner.** Become the fair-price buyer for the conference-room and server hardware that integrators and managed-service providers rip out, and the seller to churches, schools and small venues.

**Customer / who pays.** Supply side: AV integrators, MSPs, IT departments (they are glad to avoid scrap). Demand side: churches, schools, small venues and offices that want pro gear at 40-70% off. Revenue is the spread plus later a trade-in quote tool sold to integrators' sales teams.

**Sourced facts.** OEM trade-in programs (Poly through AVI-SPL, AVer, Cisco Refresh) exist because OEMs pay to displace competitor gear, but they pay discounts on new product rather than cash for old. Existing used-AV channels: Gearsupply (cites broker commissions up to 15%), GAVX (Poly refurbished/trade-in reseller), eBay. Cisco does not endorse third-party refurbishment and requires software relicensing. Memory supercycle: Barclays (Oct 2026, via search summary) says DDR4 contract prices rose 24% over three months; forecasts of shortage into 2028; one benchmark has server resale values near 2.5x their seven-year average. ITAD firms (Vibrant, BuySellRAM) already publicise this, so the memory value is priced in at the high end.

**Founder edge.** The mentor founded a roughly $1B AV integrator and teaches entrepreneurship. One introduction to an operations or procurement lead converts a cold seller search into a warm one. A seller who is giving away stock does not need to take a teenager seriously.

**First 10 customers.** Ask the mentor for three integrator or MSP contacts with decommissioned stock; buy five to ten lots; sell to church tech directors (production forums), school IT staff and small venues. Ten sales in 30-45 days.

**Cost to first revenue.** About $6k of working capital.

**Path to big.** Honest answer: trading is capital-limited, so it only becomes big as a platform: trade-in-as-a-service for integrators and MSPs with graded pricing, pickup, data wipe and resale. Who does it today at that layer is thin, but Poly/AVI-SPL-style OEM programs may already satisfy integrators. Treat this as a mentor-leveraged cash engine with a platform option, not the headline bet.

**Risks.** Cisco secondary-market policy and licences, counterfeit and warranty exposure, data-wipe liability, state e-waste rules (resell working gear only).

## 7. Auction Terminal (cross-venue deal ranking and proxy bidding for ShopGoodwill, GovDeals, estate-sale and regional auction listings)

**One-liner.** A Sunday-night shortlist of the 50 most mispriced ending lots across the national auction sites, ranked by expected net after shipping, with a bid agent and consolidated shipping behind it.

**Customer / who pays.** Full-time and part-time flippers, thrift-hauls sellers, small-business buyers. $19-49/month, then a bid-agent fee.

**Sourced facts.** ShopGoodwill: about $450M GMV last year (+22%), over $3B cumulative, 135+ local Goodwill organisations, 90% of the final price goes to the local Goodwill. GovDeals GMV was $226.9M in a single quarter (FY26 Q1) and set a record in Q3. Competing tools: Arbitrage AI (eBay), BidPilot (UK, from $25), Auction Arbitrage Overlay (B-Stock), BidLens (Chrome extension with AI value estimate), a ShopGoodwill scraper on Apify, and photo apps (Flippr, Underpriced AI, Price Snap). None ranks a whole venue or handles cross-seller shipping consolidation. **Unverified hypothesis:** ShopGoodwill ships per seller with no cross-seller combining, which creates a domestic "Buyee" opening.

**Founder edge.** Scrapers, ranking models, fast shipping of tools.

**First 10 customers.** r/Flipping, resale YouTubers, a free weekly list that grows an email base.

**Cost to first revenue.** About $400.

**Path to big.** Weak as pure software ($5-15M ARR ceiling). Only big if the transaction layer (proxy bidding, escrow, consolidated shipping, financing) works. Highest ToS and scraping risk on this list. The category of "AI price scanner for resellers" is the most crowded here. Include only as a lower-ranked option.

## 8. Thrift Desk (online sales desk for independent thrift, charity and ReStore shops)

**One-liner.** An AI listing and fulfilment desk that sells the top 5% by value of a charity shop's donations online for a 30% share.

**Customer / who pays.** Independent thrift, hospice, church, Salvation-Army-type and Habitat-ReStore-type shops. They already have the inventory; in-store a $60 branded jacket sells for $8. Revenue share of 30% of online sales.

**Sourced facts.** ShopGoodwill proves the model and the demand (+22% growth, $450M GMV), and Goodwill's own AI listing tool handles 130-140k items a month, but it serves only the 135 or so participating Goodwills. Independent shops lack that tooling.

**Who does it today.** Goodwill (captive), consignment-POS vendors, local resellers who pick shops over. I did not verify any independent-thrift AI listing service.

**First 10 customers.** Pilot with 2-3 local shops; staff set aside high-value items; he lists and ships; weekly payout. First sales within a month.

**Cost to first revenue.** About $500.

**Path to big.** (unverified) if there are ~25k US thrift stores and an average $15k a year of incremental revenue share on the best-donations stream, 5,000 shops gives about $75M. Operations are heavy: photography, storage, shipping, so scale needs workflow software and a shipping hub. Low on "software moat"; I rate it last.

## Examined and killed (so later rounds do not redo them)

- **Tariff refunds for small importers.** The wave is largely served: CAPE accepted declarations covering 25.1M entries and about $100B was certified by 31 July 2026; only the importer of record or its licensed broker can file; brokers and carriers handle it; Phase 3 for finally-liquidated entries started 6 October. Why-now is mostly used up.
- **Tariff pass-through claims for downstream buyers.** Real but it is a litigation market (Covington, Foley, Sullivan & Cromwell, Arnold & Porter commentary); recovery depends on contract language and explicit surcharge lines; non-lawyer fee-splitting is a wall.
- **Class-action claim apps for consumers.** Funded players exist (Chariot Claims, $3.6M seed, July 2025; Oyster, Settlemate, Claimly, ClaimWise); administrators take claims free.
- **Restaurant delivery-platform dispute recovery.** Checkmate (8% of recovered), Voosh, Deliverect Reclaim, GetDeliveri (~17% per one merchant).
- **CPG deduction recovery.** Glimpse ($10M raised, April 2025), STAT Recovery, HighRadius, Doss. Also in a sibling lens.
- **Unemployment benefit-charge audits.** USC ChargeShield and Equifax-type incumbents; small employers can self-check.
- **PMI early-removal service.** Ownwell (25% of first-year savings) and Abode already do it.
- **Japan to US resale scanners.** A crowd of Apify scrapers and the J-Flip Pro extension.
- **Campus move-out resale.** Many student startups (Re-Fridge, Green Garage, Campus Reclaimed, Badger Reclaim, DormCycle), grants (Oregon DEQ gave the University of Portland ~$98k); the calendar (May/August) fails the 60-90-day first-revenue test.
- **Indirect-purchaser antitrust claims for restaurants.** Real classes exist (pork and chicken) but payouts are lumpy and slow, so there is no 60-90-day revenue.
- **Memory/server harvesting as a standalone play.** ITAD firms already publicise the value, so the edge is gone for a newcomer.
- **Mass-market photo-to-price scanner apps.** Saturated.

## Ranking logic

1. **Co-op Cash Autopilot** is first: biggest sourced pool, a payer with an obvious budget logic (money already earned), no licence wall, no inventory or custody, and direct reuse of the freight-audit playbook.
2. **Contents Desk** has the largest long-run upside among the inventory ideas, a non-obvious wedge (sell to estate companies first) and no AI-appraisal competitor found.
3. **Installed-Base Buyer** is the most non-obvious and best matched to his shipping speed, with real fat-tail upside but a high variance and a thin evidence base on monetisation.
4-5. **Pallet Truth** and **Statement Cop** are likely to reach first revenue fastest but have lower ceilings.
6. **Refresh Desk** uses the mentor but is capital-limited.
7-8. **Auction Terminal** and **Thrift Desk** are the weakest on moat and scale.
