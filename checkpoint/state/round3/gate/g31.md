# g31 - S:I0419 Repair-Rights Compliance Portal in a Box

**Verdict: KILL as pitched.** Tiny ceiling (bottom-up $0.6M median, ~$5M upside), no forcing function for the target buyer, cheap substitutes already exist. First sale is feasible; the business is not big.

## What the searches found (about 17 searches/fetches, 2026-10-25)

**1. The premise "hard compliance deadlines drive purchases" is mostly wrong in the US.**
- No state law found requires a public repair portal or website. Statutes require documentation, parts and tools on "fair and reasonable terms" (Faegre Drinker, Jul 2026; repair.org guide; Promise Legal).
- Kansas and Texas let a maker comply by refunding the buyer or giving an equivalent replacement, so no portal needed.
- Enforcement is attorney-general-only in most states (CT, KS, MN, TX, WA, OR, NY, CA). Texas (eff. 9/1/2026) adds a mandatory 30-day cure notice. Oregon penalties only begin 7/1/2027. Kansas starts 7/1/2027.
- I found zero state AG enforcement actions under the electronics laws. The only enforcement story is the FTC/IL/MN case against Deere (farm equipment, not this buyer set).
- Penalties are real on paper (MN up to $25k per violation, OR $1k/day, CA $1k-5k/day) but nobody is collecting from small brands. Colorado has private damages with no AG penalty, the one real risk driver.
- No small-company exemption was found, but coverage is price-based and product-based. NY exempts e-bikes and appliances with embedded digital parts. Many states exempt solar, medical, cars, consoles.

**2. The EU piece is real but already served cheaply.**
- Directive 2024/1799 applies from 31 Jul 2026. It covers Annex II goods (washers, dishwashers, fridges, displays, vacuums, phones, tablets, e-bike/e-scooter batteries). Manufacturers must give indicative repair prices on a free-access website. Non-EU makers need an EU authorised representative or the importer carries the duty.
- Only a handful of member states transposed on time (Euronews, 10 Sep 2026), so direct enforcement is patchy.
- Shopify apps already sell this: RepairProof EU (launched 21 Aug 2026; free, $39, $99, $199/mo; repair-request forms, audit CSV export), UnboxBridge (QR manuals, free tier), Repair Score Manager (from $5/mo), Dotcase EU Compliance. At $5-199/mo, the $149-499/mo price point has little room.
- I could not fetch the directive text itself (Fieldfisher returned 403), so the website-duty wording rests on law-firm summaries.

**3. Competitors and substitutes (assume the gap is not a gap).**
- iFixit (enterprise repair guides, parts distribution, "Right to Repair compliance" consulting; customers include Lenovo, Logitech, Google, Motorola, Framework, Valve, Patagonia). No public pricing.
- Documoto (parts catalogs and portals for equipment OEMs), Anipart/Animech (repair docs from CAD/PLM, public-access mode), Claimlane (warranty and repair workflow with a self-service portal, custom pricing).
- Free: repair.org "Manufacturer's Guide", state AG pages, law-firm alerts. Counsel for a small brand: Promise Legal.
- No venture-funded startup found that does exactly "R2R portal in a box". That is more likely a sign that the market is too small than a sign of an open lane (searches turned up well-funded product-compliance startups, e.g. Complir $11M, but none in repair).

**4. Buyer health and size.**
- The headline buyer set (small e-bike brands) is financially fragile: Rad Power Bikes filed Chapter 11 (late 2025, about $32M assets vs $73M liabilities), Electric Bike Company closed, VanMoof/Cake/others failed. NY exempts e-bikes. Founders fighting for survival do not buy discretionary compliance SaaS.
- Bottom-up (my estimate, not sourced): about 3,000-6,000 reachable founder-run consumer-hardware brands where a covered state or the EU applies. Realistic price about $3k a year. Realistic penetration of a voluntary product with free and $39 substitutes is 5-15%. That gives $0.5-2.7M a year. Even 20% of 6,000 at $4k is about $5M.
- Reaching $10M+ needs parts commerce, logistics or warranty data, which is a different business with different competitors (iFixit parts, Claimlane).

**5. Founder and legal check.**
- Age: no wall. An 18-year-old can form an LLC, contract and take payment.
- A per-state "requirements checklist" is close to legal advice. Fine as counsel-reviewed informational software with disclaimers, but it should never be sold as a compliance guarantee. The $1-2k counsel review is the right mitigation and it works for credibility.
- Reachability: scrapeable and emailable (Shopify and Amazon-brand founders). A first $1.5-3k setup sale in about 8-10 weeks is plausible but is a sale to a founder who does not strictly need it.

## Numbers
- Cost to first revenue: about $1,800 (counsel review plus domain, hosting, outreach tools)
- Weeks to first revenue: about 9
- Median 3-year-out annual revenue: about $0.6M. Upside: about $5M. Neither reaches the $10M bar.

## Deciding reason
The buyer is not forced to buy (US laws do not mandate a portal, enforcement is AG-only with cure periods and no actions seen, refunds satisfy some statutes), the EU duty is covered by $5-199/mo Shopify apps and enterprise vendors, and the reachable pool is a few thousand financially fragile brands. Small, substitutable and unforced.

## Sharpened version (a different idea; needs its own gate before building)
Stop selling compliance. Sell the repair revenue layer. The brand pays out of revenue share, not a compliance budget: a hosted storefront and booking page that sells genuine parts and repairs for the brand, with a certified independent-shop network, and the state/EU compliance publishing as a free by-product. The payer is the brand's P&L (parts margin, retention, fewer returns) instead of a legal line. The wedge is orphaned and distressed hardware, where owners of stranded products (Rad, Electric Bike Company, VanMoof) need parts and repair. Unvalidated hypothesis: it needs parts sourcing and a shop network, and iFixit and Claimlane sit near it, so it must be gated separately.

## Caveats
- State-law details come mainly from one July 2026 law-firm summary plus the repair.org and Promise Legal guides; I did not read statute text.
- The size model is my own bottom-up; no brand-count data was found.
- Colorado e-bike coverage rests on PIRG's reading, unconfirmed.
