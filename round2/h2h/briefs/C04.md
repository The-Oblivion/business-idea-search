# C04: Time-to-Power Desk

**One-liner:** A deposit-funded, matched-book seller of date-certain, OEM-warranted, allied-origin 40-200 MVA substation transformers, sourced from cancelled orders and pooled-deposit slot reservations and sold to utilities and EPC/IPP buyers; the $0.7B multi-ratio lease fleet is dropped.

**Revenue cases (5-7 years):** median $30M/yr; upside $120M/yr.

**Full refined pitch:** round2/deep/C04_refined.md · **Research dossier:** round2/deep/C04_dossier.md

## What the adversarial critics said (severity, then top objection)
- **Competition / incumbents** (fatal): The 'nobody holds stock' gap is false, and the dossier understated it in the exact 30-100 MVA window. Maddox's own product page lists Powerclass substations up to 160 MVA at 69-230 kV and says common sizes are 'in stock and ready to ship'. The dossier called Maddox the same product at distribution scale, based on its cited installs of 1-2 MVA. Maddox is also adding storage yards (400 padmounts in Washington, 1,200 in Ohio). JCL Energy markets 'engineered substations' to 345 kV, same-week shipment, blanket POs, and lists a data-center campus order of eight units delivered in stages (from a search summary of its projects page). Giga Energy keeps stock in Long Beach and Houston, and is also a data-center developer, so it is customer and competitor at once. Sunbelt Solomon has a Quick Ship inventory including three-phase substation units (reconditioned), 42 locations, mobile-substation rental to 230 kV, and a page titled 'Don't Wait on the Utility - Power Your Data Center Now'. Delta Wye advertises ready-to-ship units up to 138 kV. Surplus Record already carries live 30/40/50 MVA listings from JCL and Belyea, plus a Prolec 30/40/50 MVA 34.5 kV listing: 'Three new units available in 2027'. That is slot flipping done by someone else. The fleet is a late entrant into a working secondary market, not a new category. It also holds fewer SKUs (about 280 units across a few specs) than these dealers' pooled stock across all specs.
- **Customer / pricing / go-to-market** (fatal): UNIT ECONOMICS: every route to $100M of revenue is a pass-through dealer spread or a thin-margin balance-sheet bet, and no customer is shown paying enough to fix that. The sale route is about 50 units at $2M, with gross profit of roughly $11-14M on the dossier's own 12-15% markup. The lease route is a $0.6-0.85B book earning roughly its cost of capital, with break-even near 78% utilization by the dossier's own illustration. The only lease yield cited is Willis Lease's aircraft-engine factor of about 12.5% a year (https://allmind.ai/earnings/event/bt_WLFC_2025_Q3). Engines have a deep global lessee pool, and I found no transformer lease or rental-rate comp. Searches for a 30 MVA rental rate or utilization figure returned nothing; Sunbelt Solomon quotes per project. The dossier's own numbers also show the leverage problem. On a $700M fleet with 70% debt ($210M equity), a 20% mark-down is $140M, or about 67% of the equity. The fleet would be bought at peak scarcity pricing. Wood Mackenzie (2024) said supplier margins on power units had risen about 4x and made up about 20% of unit cost (https://www.woodmac.com/es/news/opinion/4-years-into-a-difficult-transformers-market-in-the-us-is-there-a-potential-end-in-sight/). Citi, via secondary summaries, projects global HV transformer capacity up 53% from 2025 to 2028. That makes the fleet a leveraged long position on the scarcity rent, held by the party with no slot priority.
- **Execution / regulation / capital / timing** (fatal): CAPITAL AND UNIT ECONOMICS. The only version that reaches $100M of revenue needs about $0.7B of balance sheet and earns roughly its cost of capital. The dossier's own table shows break-even at about 78% utilization and about a 9% equity return even at 100%. That table leaves out staff, test crews, transport and install, bad debt, and warranty costs on stored units. The equity layer is about $210M, versus the $25-26M that Ayr and FluxCo have raised. Equity is also first-loss on price: a 10% mark-down on a $700M fleet is $70M, one-third of the equity. Wood Mackenzie data say power-transformer prices are up about 77% since 2019, so partial mean reversion is plausible. Rystad does not forecast a glut and says price direction is 'hard to predict', but it does say prices and lead times have stagnated. A fleet bought now is bought at or near the peak. The sale route is a 12-15% dealer spread, about $11-14M gross profit on $100M of sales. JCL, Maddox, Giga and Sunbelt Solomon already work that spread. Sunbelt Solomon has 42 locations and a private-credit-financed PE sale in progress. Of the 7 Powers, the only reachable one is cost of capital, and that belongs to infrastructure funds and PE roll-ups, not a startup. The per-utility spec approvals are separate per SKU and give little switching cost. Pre-mortem for 2028: slots bought in 2027 at peak pricing, delivery in 2028-29 into new OEM capacity, and a neocloud lessee in distress.

## Second-round critic: wounded
- **Is the upside believable?** No. The rewrite puts the upside at 2-3% unconditional (5-8% if the test passes), and the individual assumptions do not hold together.\n\n1. The $14M of 'option, financing-origination and logistics fees' contradicts the rewrite's own pricing. Origination is about 1.75%, which is roughly $2M on $106M of sales. The 2-3% reservation fee is 'credited at order', so it is a deposit and not extra revenue. About $14M of fees implies about 13% of sales, which the pricing section does not support. The $120M headline is overstated by roughly $10M or more.\n\n2. The 11-13% gross margin does not match the tier mix. Most units (roughly 26 of 38, with only 10+ coming from cancellations) are priced at the reserved tier, +4-6% plus a credited fee. That is about 5-8% gross, well below 11-13%. Only the floor and reassigned tiers (+8-20%) and discounted cancelled units reach the high margins, and they depend on the unproven supply of cancelled units. A realistic blended margin is nearer 7-10%, which would put EBITDA below the stated $8-12M, before liquidated-damages and warranty reserves.\n\n3. Share of market. $120M is about 36% of the rewrite's own unsourced reachable-payer count ($335M a year) and 25-80% of Wood Mackenzie's delivery-critical lower bound. The rewrite labels this heroic. It has no moat, no frame agreements and no incumbent advantage, and it is a new entrant against dealers, Ayr, FluxCo and Giga, who are already scaling.\n\n4. Supply assumptions are the least supported: 10+ cancelled units a year at 12% under replacement cost with no confirmed assignability, and 25+ units a year across two allied OEM plants by month 24 from a newcomer with about $30M of equity, at a time when OEMs favor framework and volume buyers.\n\n5. Internal timing contradiction: the upside needs the shortage to persist through 2029, yet leverage over OEMs comes from large 2028-29 commitments when new capacity needs backlog, and the cancellations that supply the inventory signal weaker demand.\n\nThe median case of $30M revenue at 11-13% gross margin is about $3.5M of gross profit before team, LC, legal and LD costs. That is break-even to modest profit, not a venture outcome. The two-week disclosed RFQ test is cheap and honest, so a small, testable business remains. The upside case is not credible as stated."
- **Remaining holes:** Supply side of tiers 1 and 2 is unevidenced and is not covered by the two-week test. No source shows cancelled or in-production OEM units are assignable to a third party. Reported deposits are non-refundable, and an OEM with a multi-year queue has every reason to re-sell a freed slot itself at current prices. My search found a manufacturer cancellation absorbed by new orders within a week (Indo Tech) and found no reporting of a slot-resale market. Bernstein-style cancellations may also be double-ordering, not built units. The test checks only demand-side willingness to pay and dealer quotes. It never asks whether any OEM will accept a newcomer's pooled-deposit reservation or consent to unit transfer. | Spec fit cuts against the cancellation-sourcing idea. A unit built to developer A's impedance, BIL, vector group and bushing layout rarely matches utility B's approved-plant and spec list (Dominion-style lists). 'Multi-ratio' was dropped, but the same fragmentation now limits redeployment of the cancelled units. The 10+ units a year at 12% under replacement cost has no source. The 'cross-fit database' is asserted as process power but not shown to raise the hit rate. | Tier 3 (reserved, 30-60 weeks, +4-6%) has no speed edge over incumbents. JCL's engineered-substation page shows about 30-34 weeks of production and roughly 35-40 weeks end to end (from search results, not fetched). The dossier has Giga at 26 weeks made-to-order and Ayr at 40-72 weeks. FluxCo already runs vetted-manufacturer procurement for buyers who lack frame agreements. The competitors section answers only the dealers' stock, not Ayr, FluxCo or Giga on the reservation tier. It also never answers the OEM channel-conflict point: why an OEM would give a newcomer slots when it can take the buyer's deposit directly. 'Earned by bringing pooled-deposit backlog' is circular, because the backlog is the buyer's money that the OEM could take itself. | Timing contradiction. The cornered resource is to come from large OEM commitments timed to 2028-29, when new capacity needs backlog. That is the same moment the plan expects to be easing, so slots are no longer scarce and the speed premium compresses. The upside assumes the shortage persists through 2029 in 60 MVA and up. Cancellations are the supply source, yet accelerating cancellations signal weaker demand and a falling premium. The old correlated-risk problem has returned in a different place. | Wedge buyers are the ones dealers already serve. A JCL case study shows a co-op that needed 138 kV units, was quoted 44-52 weeks by OEMs, and was served by three 30 MVA units matched from stock. Mid-size utilities and G&Ts, many of them public or cooperative, often have competitive-bid rules. The rewrite does not say how a sole-source startup wins those bids. The claim that the large load funds the premium through CIAC or direct assignment is an untested regulatory assumption, and the test does not probe it.

## Riskiest assumption and the 2-week test
- **Riskiest assumption:** Buyers with a signed project and a binding energization date will pay at least 8-10% over the OEM benchmark, or a 2-3% non-refundable reservation fee, for a date-certain 40-200 MVA unit, and existing dealers cannot already deliver new, warranted units of the right spec in 20 weeks or less. This is the gap two critics say is false.
- **2-week test:** About $0, 14 days, disclosed. Days 1-3: write three example specs (e.g. 40/53/67 MVA 138-34.5 kV with LTC; 84 MVA 230-34.5 kV; 60 MVA 69-13.8 kV GSU). Days 2-8: request budgetary quotes from 8 sellers (Maddox, JCL, Giga, Sunbelt Solomon, Delta Wye, MARS Transformers, Belyea, Power Asset Recovery or Transformer Exchange), saying openly that I am a new sourcing firm. Record new vs remanufactured, plant of origin, weeks, price per MVA, factory testing, warranty, storage and origin attestations. Days 3-14: 20 calls (8 utility supply-chain/engineering, 6 EPC/IPP procurement, 6 data-center developers) asking for specific transformer-critical delays in the last 12 months, what was paid, and whether they would sign a 2-3% reservation; collect 2+ written indications. Kill if 3+ of 6 dealers quote 20 weeks or less at 8% or less over benchmark on 2 of 3 specs, or fewer than 5 of 20 buyers name a documented 6-month+ transformer-critical delay, or no buyer gives a written indication at 8%+ or a reservation fee. Pass needs 3+ documented cases and 2+ written indications.
