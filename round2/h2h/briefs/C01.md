# C01: Capped Outage: scope- and date-guaranteed gas-turbine overhauls for owners with no LTSA

**One-liner:** A neutral prime that sells owners with no OEM long-term service agreement a fixed-price, growth-banded, date-guaranteed, surety-bonded turbine outage, priced on measured condition and performed by third-party shops, then converts the best accounts to 3-5 year programs.

**Revenue cases (5-7 years):** median $30M/yr; upside $110M/yr.

**Full refined pitch:** round2/deep/C01_refined.md · **Research dossier:** round2/deep/C01_dossier.md

## What the adversarial critics said (severity, then top objection)
- **Competition / incumbents** (fatal): The strongest responder is not the OEM, it is the independent that already owns the wedge, and condition pricing is a contract term it can copy. Hanwha Power (PSM merged with Hanwha Power Systems in March 2026, Hanwha Group behind it) is the company the DOJ named in 2015 as the other leading 7FA aftermarket provider alongside GE. It already sells modular LTA/LTMA/Frame agreements with no parts exclusivity, a remote-monitoring 'Intelligent Data' platform, and a new 9-year US LTSA (Trumbull, June 2026). Adding a measured-wear true-up and a reinsurance quote is a pricing clause, not hardware. Counter-positioning (the idea's moat) only works against a party that would cannibalize an existing book. Hanwha, Allied, MD&A and Ethos have no averaged-hours book to protect, so nothing stops them from copying. The entrant also owns no hardware: its pooled spares and serial-level condition data come from ideas 1 and 2, which the gate found are already ISP products (PSM ready-to-install exchange, MD&A seed rotors, Ethos rotor program, ProEnergy's $350M pool). So the startup is a channel-conflicted middleman sitting on its own competitors' parts, repair and outage data. The dossier's ~12% gross margin is already after parts substitution that every independent has. Capital is also consolidating around the incumbents in 2026: Capstreet bought TOPS (Feb), Allied bought Ethos's generator assets (Apr), Allied bought TRS (Aug), and Warren Equity acquired Allied.
- **Customer / pricing / go-to-market** (fatal): BUYERS DO NOT SCORE WHAT THIS COMPANY SELLS, AND A START-UP CANNOT EVEN QUALIFY. I pulled the actual Austin Energy evaluation matrix (RFP 1100 MMH3038, 7FA+e, the only 2026 public F-class independent-LTSA award found). The five scored criteria are Price 25, Technical Plan 25, Experience and Personnel 25, Business Organization 5, and two 10-point local/small-business preferences. None rewards condition-based pricing. Scores: Hanwha 61 (price 11, tech 21, experience 25, org 4); Allied 29 (price 25, tech 0, experience 4); GE 20 (price 7); MD&A 30 (price 16, tech 8). The buyer passed over the cheapest bid (Allied, full 25 price points) and paid up for the bidder with references. A no-reference entrant scores about 4 on experience. Even if it won all 25 price points it tops out near 39 (25 + about 8 tech + 4 + 2) against 61, because 50 of 100 points are technical plan and track record. The RFP also demands 100% payment and performance bonds (minimum $10M each) and five references per scope. The 10-20% condition-pricing discount is the one thing the evaluators barely weight, and the one thing a start-up cannot substitute for proof.
- **Execution / regulation / capital / timing** (fatal): THE INSURER WRAP, THE ONE THING THAT IS SUPPOSED TO BE DIFFERENT, IS PROBABLY UNINSURABLE OR DUPLICATIVE. New York's insurance regulator (opinion 04-05-01, 2004) held that an insurer may not indemnify a maintenance-agreement maker for the cost of performing scheduled maintenance, because there is 'no fortuitous occurrence under the agreement to insure against.' A 'parts-cost cap' on planned hot-gas-path and major-inspection work is exactly that. What remains insurable is unplanned resulting damage, which owners already buy under property and equipment-breakdown cover, and the 2026 power market is soft (WTW: rate cuts of about 40% for clean risks), so the wrap adds little. Wear and tear is excluded and insurers view an OEM LTSA favorably (Aon, via POWER, per the dossier). That leaves two outcomes. Either the tail stays on the start-up's own balance sheet, or it is dressed up as insurance, the exact pattern that killed the round-1 home-value-guarantee finalist. The NY opinion is old and one state, so it is a strong signal, not a ruling. But nobody has produced even one reinsurer quote, and the gate itself made that the kill test.

## Second-round critic: wounded
- **Is the upside believable?** No. (1) The ramp to 22 packages a year (about 13% of the wedge by dollars, 'average competitor among eight') for an unreferenced newcomer, on a 25% win rate across 80+ free bids a year, in a market where Austin showed references beat price. The rewrite itself calls this aggressive. (2) The probability stack (40% x 40% x 20% = about 3%) is generous for each stage given the unresolved holes (sponsor, surety, supply slots, Hanwha). (3) The upside needs realized growth of 9% or less and a 12% margin, while the pitch and the pass test depend on growth being 10-15% or more, and the margin math only works at a 0.77x cost basis. (4) It assumes a 3-year-plus copy lag while the rewrite's moat section says 18-24 months. (5) It adds a $20-30M owned spare pool and a sponsor of $40-60M by year 5, with no evidence either exists. (6) It counts $12M from Canada/Mexico/Gulf and $7M from teamed public bodies, both unsupported. The rewrite deserves credit for stating a 3% probability and a break-even median, but even the upside is only $4-7M EBITDA, and the median outcome is a small sale to an ISP. The upside is a best case that depends on the rewrite's weakest assumptions all holding at once.
- **Remaining holes:** Unit economics contradict the demand test. The pass criterion requires median as-found growth of at least 10% on 60% of outages. The business absorbs that growth at a price of 1.00-1.02x the OEM base quote, and the upside case needs realized growth of 9% or less with margin holding at 12%. The 12% only works if the prime's cost is about 0.77x OEM (the dossier's independent-parts sourcing cost). The rewrite's own number for what an ISP charges owners is 0.85-0.90x OEM, and a prime subcontracting shops pays roughly that. Then margin is about 1.01 - 0.87 x 1.12 = about 3-4% before bond premiums (1-3%), bid costs, LDs and site management. Passing the demand gate selects for the cases where the prime loses money. | Customer selection is self-defeating. The rewrite targets owners who buy OEM-transactionally because there is no price room against owners with an ISP alternative at 85-90% of OEM. Owners who stay with the OEM transactionally usually do so for parts quality, warranty, insurer preference or controls access. They are the least likely to award a $5M outage to an unreferenced newcomer at price parity. The owners who do switch already have ISP fixed-scope turnkey offers, so the cap has to beat what an ISP already quotes. | The hard return-to-service date has no hardware behind it. The binding constraint is rotor, casting and slot availability, which the hardware holders control. The rewrite concedes ISPs allocate scarce slots to their own customers. I fetched PSM/Hanwha's 2026 outage article. It sells pre-positioned exchange components and in-house repair to 'eliminate the coordination headaches of managing multiple vendors', which is the opposite of a neutral multi-shop prime. Liquidated damages capped at about 10% of price are about $500k on a $5M package. At the pitch's own $75k per overrun day that is about 7 days. Either the 'hard date' is token and the value proposition collapses, or the cap is raised and the prime's tail risk explodes. | Moral hazard has moved but not gone. Shops earn more when more is found. The prime holds growth exposure of up to about 30% of base (20% included plus half of the next 20%) with less visibility than the shop doing the teardown. If shops quote fixed prices to the prime, they charge a risk premium and could sell direct, which leaves the prime redundant. If shops work at T&M or unit rates, the prime pays for discovery. The rewrite has no inspector, audit or QA mechanism for this. | No moat, and the upside contradicts the rewrite's own moat section. The moat section says Hanwha can match a growth cap in 18-24 months. Upside assumption 6 needs 3 or more years. The dataset moat arrives at about 50 packages. On the rewrite's ramp (2, 5, 9, 13, 16, 19, 22) cumulative packages are 29 at year 5 and 45 at year 6, so it lands in year 6-7, after the copy window.

## Riskiest assumption and the 2-week test
- **Riskiest assumption:** Owners at non-LTSA F-class units bear material, volatile as-found cost growth and schedule slip today, which a fixed-price contractor is not already absorbing. They would pay roughly half of the expected overrun to cap it, and would award to a newcomer teamed with a named, bonded shop. If incumbents already absorb scope growth in turnkey deals, or growth is small, there is no product and the business is a crowded plain independent contractor.
- **2-week test:** Run 25 calls in 14 days, at least 15 with non-LTSA, non-scale-fleet F-class owners, sourced from 7F Users Group attendee lists, APPA and NRECA generation contacts, plant managers on LinkedIn, and referrals from two LTSA consultants. For each owner's last 2-3 outages, ask: awarded price versus final cost, days late, contract form, who bore the growth, and who earned margin on it. Then show a one-page term sheet (price near the OEM base quote, 20% growth band, liquidated damages, bond, named shop) and ask whether they would take a bid, what premium they would pay for a hard cap, whether they would send their last two outage reports and an OEM quote for a written bid in 5 days, and whether they can export hours, starts and trips. In parallel, pull at least 15 public council or board change-order and amendment records for F-class maintenance and outage work to measure award-to-final variance. Pass: median growth at least 10% on at least 60% of sampled outages, at least 8 of 25 would take a bid from a teamed newcomer, and at least 5 would share records. Kill: median growth below 6%, or 70% or more say their ISP already absorbs growth, or fewer than 3 would consider a newcomer. Cost is near zero. This is stated preference, so only a signed event is strong evidence. Separately, a gate in months 3-9 asks 5 brokers and underwriters for appetite on the insured slice and a stop-loss.
