# C03: HCBS Operations Utility for Managed-LTSS Plans

**One-liner:** Fixed-price, 24/7 operations for a Medicaid plan's highest-need home-care members (missed-shift response, hours-delivered reconciliation, HCBS back office, licensed coordination capacity), paid from budget lines the plan already carries, owned by no health plan, with shared savings and risk earned only after the data supports them.

**Revenue cases (5-7 years):** median $18M/yr; upside $101M/yr.

**Full refined pitch:** round2/deep/C03_refined.md · **Research dossier:** round2/deep/C03_dossier.md

## What the adversarial critics said (severity, then top objection)
- **Competition / incumbents** (serious): The 'neutral vacuum' is contradicted by what rival plans actually do, and Elevance's incentives point toward closing it. Iowa's state EVV FAQ says the MCOs 'have selected CareBridge as the EVV solution' and that all MCOs use the same one. The dossier ties those MCOs to Elevance, Centene (Iowa Total Care) and Molina, so rivals have already standardized on CareBridge's data layer. Wyoming has run CareBridge as its free state-sponsored EVV since Feb 15 2022. Arkansas plans CareSource and Summit were set to start CareBridge EVV on 3/31/2025 (search summary only, not verified). Elevance is also shrinking its own Medicaid book: it exited DC, Louisiana ends in 2026, it expects 'additional' exits within 12-18 months, and its 2026 Medicaid margin guide is about -1.75%. That cuts CareBridge's captive volume and pushes Carelon to court rivals harder. Neutrality is also a contract clause (a firewall, audit rights, a governance seat), so Elevance can copy it in one quarter. I found no RFP or press report from any rival plan asking for a vendor free of Elevance. Boudreaux's public line was to 'continue to serve all its customers'. The pitch's claim that a neutrality guarantee is hard for an owned competitor to copy is therefore wrong: the guarantee is easy to copy and only hard to be believed. That makes it a sales-trust problem, not a 7 Powers moat.
- **Customer / pricing / go-to-market** (serious): NO EVIDENCE ANYONE WILL PAY FOR 'NEUTRAL', AND THE MARKET'S REVEALED BEHAVIOR POINTS THE OTHER WAY. The whole thesis is that rivals will not hand their sickest members to Elevance's subsidiary. But (a) rivals already bought from a competitor-backed vendor: HCI reports CareBridge served members 'in partnership with the nation's five largest managed Medicaid care plans' by 2023, and the dossier's Iowa deck shows Centene's and Molina's Iowa plans as CareBridge subcontractors, even though Anthem was an investor in the 2022 round. Neutrality was never the purchase criterion. (b) Two years after the close there is no defection, RFP or plan complaint (the dossier's negative finding; my searches also found none). (c) On the Jul 15 2026 Elevance call, management said 'CareBridge has been very successful for us. We're looking to continue to accelerate the deployment of CareBridge', claimed mid-teens medical savings, and said it is expanding to new markets. (d) Analog: Signify Health stayed 'payor-agnostic' under CVS and kept winning multi-payer MA business (+20% evaluations in Q4 2023; a new plan, Select Health SC, in 2025). Rivals tolerated a competitor-owned vendor when the product worked. (e) The big plans' answer to Elevance is to insource, not to buy neutral: Centene has 338K LTSS lives in-house, Optum owns Amedisys and LHC, Humana bought Onehome, and Bernstein read the CareBridge deal as expanding services that 'can replace existing outside vendors'. That leaves the buyers who can't build, meaning smaller regional plans and Blues, with the thinnest budgets. The '4 of 5 largest MCOs co-invested' fact the gate leaned on shows investors, not purchasers. The 2022 round was also an option on ownership: one co-investor, Anthem, became the acquirer. Any new co-investor plan will want the same option, which ends neutrality.
- **Execution / regulation / capital / timing** (serious): TIMING / BUYER HEALTH: the buyer pool and budget line are shrinking right now, which the dossier treats as a tailwind ('plan margins squeezed'). Squeezed plans cut or in-source vendors, and a savings-only contract is the first thing to go. Elevance guides a 2026 Medicaid operating margin of about -1.75%, with Medicaid membership down from 8.9M (end 2024) to 8.3M (Jun 2026). It has left DC (Aug 1 2026), Healthy Blue Louisiana ends at the end of 2026, and it says more state exits come within 12-18 months. Industry Medicaid MLR rose from 88% (2023) to 91% (2024), and Elevance says cost trend is outpacing funding for 2027 bids. Centene guides 2026 revenue down about 3%, is offering buyouts to most of its roughly 61,000 staff, reportedly plans to exit Arkansas's expansion program in 2027, had its Arizona ALTCS award cancelled and rebid (new contract date October 2027), and is in Georgia and Texas protests and Hawaii non-renewal. Molina lost Virginia. Aetna's Ohio MyCare plan ended Jan 1 2026. On the D-SNP side the sharpened pick depends on, Becker's reports HCSC, Aetna, Centene, Humana and CareSource each pulling dual plans from more than 100 net counties for 2027. The one big aligned D-SNP/Medicaid parent growing SNPs is UnitedHealthcare, which owns Optum and Amedisys and is the least likely to buy from a neutral vendor. The 6-10 reachable parents are volatile: a vendor's volume swings with each state rebid or protest.

## Second-round critic: wounded
- **Is the upside believable?** Not believable at the stated 5-8%. The upside needs 85k lives across 8-10 plan contracts in 8-10 states, including 40k labor-bearing, state-approved, delegated coordination lives. It needs a first paid pilot by month 9-18 and R3 live by month 24-30. It also needs a roughly 20% cost advantage in a labor business, 6 or more parents each giving a new vendor a fifth or more of their LTSS lives (my read), and none of the five big rival buyers to insource. CareBridge needed about four years, $180M, and four plan investors who were also customers to reach about 115k. The rewrite reaches for 85k with those anchors removed, and plans are cutting costs and shrinking, with Elevance, Centene and Aetna exiting markets. I put the $100M-revenue case nearer 1-2%. Even if it lands, it is about $14M of EBITDA, not $100M of profit. The $18M median is only plausible conditional on surviving the first test. As an unconditional median it is optimistic, because no demand evidence exists. The idea is alive as a cheap, falsifiable regional ops-services hypothesis. It is not alive as a $100M business.
- **Remaining holes:** Demand is still unevidenced. The rewrite drops 'neutral' as the reason to buy and swaps in price, capacity and performance, but offers no sign that any non-Elevance plan will pay a fixed, non-contingent fee. It also bans plan equity and board seats. That removes the investor-as-customer anchor that made CareBridge's first deals work (critic 1 flagged this) and supplies no replacement for a cold entrant. | The R3 margin is asserted, not derived. R3 is priced at 95% of the plan's loaded in-house cost, yet it targets a 10-15% margin. That implies a 15-20% structural cost advantage in a licensed, in-person, caseload-ratio-bound labor business. The rewrite itself says automation cannot cut headcount below the ratio. Pooling helps a 24/7 desk, not per-coordinator wages, and offshoring is unlikely for licensed in-person coordination. The plan's gain is about 5%, roughly $75 per member-year, in exchange for taking on delegation oversight, state sign-off and layoffs. Maximus's US Services segment runs about 11-12% margins (search summary), so the figure is not absurd, but that is a scaled incumbent, not a discount-priced entrant. A Truven/AdvancingStates deck (search summary) calls full delegation of LTSS coordination rare. | The 'why now' for R3 contradicts itself. The pitch says caseload mandates 'don't shrink' as membership falls, but a caseload ratio is a ratio, so required headcount falls with membership. The only real fixed cost is a staffing floor plus hiring lag. The pitch also sells variable-cost flex while charging 3-year terms with per-plan minimums and wind-down fees, and the vendor carries the stranded labor. | R1 pricing undercuts the scale thesis. If a desk costs about $280 per member at 3k members and about $100 at 30k, the $300 price exceeds 95% of the in-house cost for a 3k-member plan (about $266). For a 30k-member plan it is roughly 3x what building in-house would cost. The wedge definition includes plans with up to 60k HCBS members, who can build the desk themselves. The pooled-scale argument only holds for plans below about 5-10k members. | The upside arithmetic has a gap. The stated components (R1 $6M, R2 $15M, R3 $60M, R4 $10M, R5 $10M) sum to $101M. Removing R3 leaves about $41M, not the $55-60M stated. $65M only follows if the 40k R3 lives are downgraded to R2, which the file does not say. R4 and R5 ($20M) are unsupported. R4 is about $500 per life of savings share, while the rewrite itself disclaims acute savings and critic 3 cited an NBER finding that managed LTSS raised hospitalizations. The 34% blended gross margin implies roughly 65% gross margin on the non-R3 labor-bearing rungs if R3 earns 10-15% (inferred, not shown).

## Riskiest assumption and the 2-week test
- **Riskiest assumption:** A non-Elevance, non-UnitedHealth Medicaid LTSS plan will pay a fixed, non-contingent fee (about $40+ PMPM for operations, about $110+ PMPM for ratio-assured coordination) from its administrative or care-coordination budget to an unaffiliated vendor, rather than insource, buy from CareBridge, or insist on savings-only. Behind it sits a second assumption: our cost to serve R3 leaves a 10-15% margin at 95% of the plan's in-house cost, even though caseload ratios stop automation from cutting headcount below the ratio.
- **2-week test:** A priced-offer call sprint costing $0-3k. Days 1-3: write a one-page offer (R1 $300, R2 $600, R3 $1,500 per member-year; 90-day paid pilot at cost plus 10%) and list 40 contacts at 10 or more non-Elevance, non-UHC plan entities, using ex-plan operators for introductions and optionally 3-4 expert-network calls. Days 2-12: hold 12 or more calls, at least 8 with director-level or higher budget-relevant people. Ask five things: (1) fully loaded cost per HCBS member-year for coordination and for HCBS operations; (2) exception volume and time to resolve; (3) what they pay CareBridge or others; (4) who signs and which budget line pays; (5) fixed fee versus savings-only. Request a de-identified data sample for a free retrospective. In parallel, desk-check caseload and delegation language in six state contracts and the state subcontractor lists (Iowa, Tennessee, Texas, Ohio, Arizona) to see whether non-Elevance plans still use CareBridge. Pass: at least 5 of 8 give a loaded cost within $900-2,500 and call a fixed fee at or below 95% of it worth a pilot conversation; at least 2 distinct parents name a budget owner above the VP of LTSS and agree to a retrospective on 2,000+ members; no more than 3 of 8 say savings-only or nothing; measured exceptions of at least 0.5 per member-month or resolution times over 4 hours. Kill: at least 6 of 8 insist on savings-only, in-house or CareBridge, or no one will name a budget owner or share a number. Then the idea reverts to the regional boutique the critics describe, and alive becomes false.
