# P095 - Cyber and liability premiums being carved out for generative-AI risk (round 2 ideas)

Written 2026-10-18. Eight ideas, two per lens. Every number is tagged: (S) = sourced this session from a search result or fetched page, (A) = my arithmetic or assumption, (U) = unverified claim, insider hypothesis, or secondary-source figure I could not confirm. About 20 searches and one page fetch were run (log at the end).

## What the search changed (read before the ideas)

The pool file calls this "contested". It is more contested than that at the start-up end and almost empty at two other ends.

- **The obvious ideas are taken and heavily funded.**
  - Corgi raised $108M in Jan 2026, launched an AI-liability product 2026-05-05, closed a $160M Series B at $1.3B, and is reported at $4B after a July extension (S, secondary press). Its cover is primarily written by "Technology Risk Retention Group, Inc.", an RRG (S, Corgi's own booking page), and an admitted carrier launched in Aug 2026 (S).
  - Armilla is a Lloyd's coverholder with $25M limits and a Feb 2026 "Vanguard AI" product with Chaucer (S).
  - AIUC has a $15M seed plus a $40M Series A and sells the AIUC-1 certification with insurance behind it (S).
  - Testudo sells a Lloyd's-backed gen-AI policy up to $10M (S, broker-comparison site). Klaimee ($5.5M seed) and Mount are in the pool file (S).
  - Coalition has sold an affirmative AI endorsement since March 2024 (S); AXA XL, Beazley and QBE have AI endorsements or sublimits (S).
  - So "AI liability MGA for AI start-ups", "AI-agent certification" and "AI RRG" are dead as new ideas.
- **The affirmative-AI premium pool is small; the carved-out base is large.** Deloitte puts global AI insurance premium at about $4.7-4.8B a year by 2032 (S, via trade press). The base being carved is cyber ($16.3B in 2025, about $29B forecast for 2027, per the pool file) and US general liability (about $131B, derived in the pool file). Ideas that live only on AI premium need a big share of a small pool; ideas that use the exclusion wave as a wedge into the whole base have a better path. I say which is which.
- **Two gaps nobody is filling.**
  - Nothing in the market restores the bodily-injury, property-damage and advertising-injury cover that ISO forms CG 40 47 / CG 40 48 / CG 35 08 removed from general liability. A review of state filings found no admitted standalone product to replace it, and I found no buy-back endorsement for CG 40 47 (S, Insurance Business and my search).
  - The top of the tower has no capacity. OpenAI reportedly holds about $300M of emerging-AI-risk cover through Aon, "far short" of multibillion-dollar claims, and Aon's cyber head says the sector lacks capacity for model providers (S, FT via secondary summaries). Dedicated AI products top out at $25-50M per insured (U, one analysis).
- **Silent-AI regulation is not a market yet.** I found no Lloyd's or PRA bulletin on silent AI (Insurance Day says the regulatory push that drove silent-cyber exclusions in 2017-2020 is largely absent for AI). APRA (Australia) wrote to insurers on 2026-04-30 and a trade-body report (2026-06-17) warns of silent AI (S, secondary). Under the digest's "compliance-deadline whiplash" filter, nothing below depends on a mandate.
- **The contract-insurance-verification market is tiny.** myCOI is about $13.7M of revenue (U, Datanyze estimate) and TrustLayer has raised about $22M (U). Any idea that is only "verify the certificates" fails the $100M test, so I killed it.

## Pool facts used (checked this session unless marked)

- (S) ISO/Verisk forms effective Jan 2026: **CG 40 47 01 26** excludes bodily injury, property damage and personal and advertising injury "arising out of" generative AI (Coverages A and B; optional; occurrence and claims-made); **CG 40 48** excludes advertising injury only (Coverage B); **CG 35 08** excludes BI/PD in products-completed operations. Berkley, Chubb, Travelers, Berkshire Hathaway and AIG had each filed ISO forms or equivalents by April. An analysis counts 2,369 gen-AI exclusion records across 49 states by mid-2026 (the pool file calls this unverified).
- (S) Reach of the wording: commentators say AI need not be the proximate cause, only part of the causal chain, and Haynes Boone (Apr 2026) quotes an exclusion reading "arising out of, or in any way involving generative AI". W.R. Berkley has an absolute AI exclusion across D&O, E&O and fiduciary. Beazley and QBE use AI sublimits of about 10% of cyber limits.
- (S) NAIC, data year 2024: US cyber premium about $9.14B including alien surplus lines ($7.08B for US-domiciled insurers, down 7%); policies in force 4,368,614; nearly 50,000 claims reported, up almost 40%; endorsement policies are 55.1% of active policies under NAIC's new split. That is about $1,620 of premium per policy (A: 7.08B / 4.37M).
- (S) Underwriters writing affirmative AI cover are converging on one evidence set: documented human-override capability, a human-in-the-loop inventory of consequential AI decisions, data-provenance documentation and a named accountable executive (Cloud Security Alliance note, 2026-08-30). Aon's 2026 AI risk report says carriers want documented governance. Design-industry carriers have negotiated carve-backs for supervised AI use where firms document tool approval, verification, client disclosure and licensed sign-off (S, Insurance Business).
- (S) Precedent for rescission: Travelers v. International Control Services (C.D. Ill., filed 2022-07-06) sought to rescind a $1M cyber policy because the application said MFA was required for privileged access and it was only on the firewall; the application was signed by the CEO.
- (U) Firm counts: about 20,000 US firms have 500+ employees (SUSB 2002 showed 16,845; I could not retrieve the 2022 figure) and about 90-110k have 100-499. These drive several (A) calculations below.

## 1. [insider] Seam Map: the reconciliation nobody does

**One-liner.** A fee-only service and data product that reads three documents no one reads together (every policy's actual wording, the company's real AI deployments, and the insurance and indemnity clauses in its customer contracts), then turns the gaps into carve-back asks backed by wordings that carriers already granted peers.

**Persona.** Policyholder-side coverage counsel, 15+ years, who has tendered claims across towers and watched carriers deny by pointing at the other policy.

**Customer.** US operating companies with $100M-$5B of revenue and a full tower (general liability and umbrella, cyber, technology or professional E&O, D&O). Beachhead: tech-enabled services, healthcare services, financial services and engineering firms that have signed AI addenda with uncapped indemnities.

**Payer and signer.** VP Risk or Treasurer; the General Counsel where there is no risk manager; the CFO signs above $50k. Budget: risk-management advisory and legal operations. The comparable spend is coverage-counsel review of a tower at hourly rates (the anchor of $30-100k per program is (A)).

**Problem.** The AI exclusions arrive differently on every line, and the summary letters brokers send miss it. Fenwick's June 2026 analysis (as reported by Insurance Business) says narrowing mostly comes through revised base forms, tighter definitions and restrictive carve-backs, not a headline exclusion. The same AI incident can be excluded on the GL, the E&O and the cyber tower at once. Meanwhile the customer contracts still require cover that no longer exists, and "insured contract" cover for assumed indemnities (U, coverage-counsel knowledge) is exactly what a broad "arising out of" AI exclusion removes. Buyers cannot see what peers negotiated; the carve-backs sit in the brokers' files.

**Solution.**
1. Parse every policy, endorsement and sublimit into a clause graph: definition of generative AI, which coverages, "arising out of" versus "in any way involving", whether vendor-used AI is caught, what carve-backs exist.
2. Ingest the customer MSAs' insurance schedules and indemnity clauses, and an AI-use inventory (SSO and OAuth grants, procurement records, AI addenda signed).
3. Output a seam map: for each AI use and each loss type (BI/PD, financial loss, IP, privacy, regulatory, D&O), which policy responds, which excludes, which contract demands cover.
4. Carve-back kit for renewal: peer wordings that carriers granted (supervised-use carve-backs, vendor-AI carve-backs, sublimit instead of absolute), plus the governance evidence pack underwriters now ask for (human override, human-in-the-loop inventory, data provenance, accountable executive).
5. Fee-only, no carrier compensation. Every renewal outcome feeds a benchmark. Phase 2: place the residual gap into Mirror Policy (idea 6) or a carrier's affirmative endorsement, with disclosed placement fees.

**Why now.** Exclusions attach at renewal through the whole 2026-27 cycle; there is no admitted replacement product; carve-backs exist but only where governance is documented (design-industry example above); Aon says documented governance improves terms.

**Wedge / first 10.** Ten companies in an active renewal with an uncapped AI indemnity outstanding, sourced through three or four policyholder coverage-counsel referral partners. Price $35-60k for the diagnostic plus renewal support (A).

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).**
- Reachable payers: about 28,000 US firms with a real tower (A: about 20,000 with 500+ employees (U) plus about 8,000 firms of $100M+ revenue below 500 employees).
- Realistic annual fee $40k per customer for the seam map plus renewal cycle (A).
- $100M = 2,500 customers = about 9% of reachable firms, year 5-6. Share is not heroic; the sales cycle against a free broker service is the risk.
- The data asset then supports a second revenue line (benchmarks sold to brokers and carriers, placement fees) that is not counted here.

**Who does it today.** Brokers' internal analytics (Marsh, Aon, WTW, Gallagher, Lockton), bundled into commission and not shared across clients. Coverage counsel at Fenwick, Reed Smith, Haynes Boone, Shumaker and others publish client alerts and bill by the hour. Haven Safety AI co-wrote a workplace-safety AI coverage paper (niche, sells an investigations tool). Coverwatch is a flat-fee AI broker that raised $4.5M pre-seed in July 2026 and serves HOAs, venture-backed tech and contractors (S). The AI carriers (Armilla, AIUC, Corgi) assess risk only to sell their own policy. GRC vendors (Vanta, Drata, OneTrust) build AI inventories but hold no insurance corpus (U, from memory).

**Why they lose.** A broker earns commission on premium and contingent income from the very carriers whose forms it would have to criticise, and a free service cannot be seen to push premium down. Law firms bill hourly and bring no cross-client data. Coverwatch is aimed at small buyers and starts from placement, not reconciliation.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Cornered resource: a corpus of wordings, contract clauses and negotiated outcomes that the big brokers hold privately. Counter-positioning: fee-only, so incumbents cannot copy without cutting their own income. Switching costs: it becomes the system of record for program documents.

**Earned secret.** The exclusion is rarely what hurts you; the seam is. Carriers deny by pointing at the other policy, and the carve-backs are available to anyone who arrives with governance evidence and the wording a peer got. The mid-market never sees peer wordings.

**First 90 days.** Sign three coverage-counsel partners. Collect 40 real tower sets (policies plus top-20 customer contracts) from friendly companies under NDA. Build the clause parser against CG 40 47, CG 40 48, CG 35 08, Berkley's absolute exclusion and the Beazley/QBE sublimit wording, and test it by having counsel mark the same 40 sets by hand. Sell five paid diagnostics before building the benchmark.

**Outside-the-box score.** 5

## 2. [insider] Warranty File: the application that cannot be used against you

**One-liner.** Turn the signed AI and cyber application into an evidence-backed, time-stamped file, and get carriers to agree by endorsement not to rescind on verified answers, a contractual incontestability clause for commercial cyber, tech E&O and affirmative AI cover.

**Persona.** Cyber and tech E&O underwriter at a lead carrier (Beazley, Chubb, AXA XL), 15+ years, who has sat in the rescission review meetings.

**Customer.** Mid-market and enterprise buyers of cyber, tech E&O and AI cover (premium of $10k and up), their brokers, and the carriers and MGAs underwriting them.

**Payer and signer.** Two payers. The carrier or MGA pays per verified file (head of cyber or head of underwriting; budget: underwriting expense and claims leakage). The insured pays an annual subscription (VP Risk or CISO; CFO signs), priced against the premium it protects.

**Problem.** Claims die in two ways: exclusions, and rescission or misrepresentation defences. Travelers v. ICS is the public example: a CEO-signed application said MFA covered privileged access, a ransomware loss showed it did not, and the carrier sued to rescind (S). AI answers are now added to every application (human override, human-in-the-loop inventory, data provenance, accountable executive), given by IT, signed by the CEO who never saw the configuration, and different on every carrier's supplement. Underwriters spend time asking more questions because they have no way to trust the answers.

**Solution.**
1. Connectors (identity provider, agent permission scopes, gateway logs, policy documents, org chart) collect the evidence behind each answer, hash it and timestamp it at signing.
2. One standard "File" is filled once and accepted by many carriers, replacing the bespoke supplements.
3. Carriers that accept the File add an endorsement: verified answers are conclusive absent fraud (the life-insurance incontestability idea, adapted).
4. Continuous attestation between renewals; a claim-time packet that proves what was true on the signing date.

Legal premise to verify before building: rescission law differs by state (some require intent), the endorsement must be filed where required, and a carrier's agreement not to rescind on verified items must not conflict with anti-waiver rules. I did not verify any of these.

**Why now.** Affirmative AI underwriters converge on a common evidence set (CSA, Aug 2026). Sublimits and exclusions are being offered with carve-backs conditional on governance. 4.4M US cyber policies are in force, and 55% are endorsement-type policies where application depth is thin (S, NAIC). Carriers want faster quoting and fewer rescission suits.

**Wedge / first 10.** Two mid-size cyber MGAs that want to quote AI-supplemented risks without a call, plus 50 insureds they already write. Start with the five AI answers underwriters ask most.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).**
- Carrier side (A): about 400k policies a year with $10k+ premium (about 10% of 4.4M, my assumption), 35% verified, $250 a file = $35M.
- Insured side (A): 25,000 insureds at $3k a year = $75M. That is roughly 6% of the mid-market-and-up insureds.
- Total about $110M by year 6-7. The heroic parts are the 35% verified share and carriers actually signing the incontestability endorsement.

**Who does it today.** Outside-in scanners (Coalition, At-Bay, Cowbell, Bitsight, SecurityScorecard) see the perimeter, not agent permissions. GRC automation (Vanta, Drata, Secureframe) serves auditors and has insurer discount partnerships (U). AIUC-1 certifies and insures but is tied to its own capacity. Armilla runs assessments for its own policies. Trust-centre vendors (SafeBase, Whistic) package evidence for customers, not underwriters (U). I searched for rescission protection and found none.

**Why they lose.** Scanners cannot see inside the firm. GRC tools are built for auditors and carry no carrier commitment. A single carrier cannot waive rescission for answers it cannot verify, and a certifier owned by one insurer will not be accepted by its competitors. A neutral file is how competing carriers can all agree to the same clause.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Network effects (carrier acceptance and insured adoption feed each other); switching costs (the evidence history); a cornered legal asset (the filed incontestability endorsement); data on which answers fail verification.

**Earned secret.** Rescission and misrepresentation, not exclusions, are how a clean-looking claim dies. The carrier does not want to rescind; it simply has no way to trust the answers except to ask more questions. Whoever makes the answers trustworthy removes both the questions and the fight.

**First 90 days.** Interview ten underwriters and five claims counsel for the last rescission files they saw. Map the AI supplements of six carriers into one schema and measure overlap. Build the connectors for the top three identity providers and one gateway. Get a letter of intent from one MGA to run a 25-file pilot, with counsel drafting the endorsement.

**Outside-the-box score.** 6

## 3. [pattern] The Mutual: a buyer-owned excess market for frontier AI (the 1985-86 playbook)

**Pattern transferred.** The digest's "a sovereign or infrastructure-capital backstop makes a capital-heavy, uninvestable industry bankable", plus the oldest insurance version of it: when commercial markets withdrew liability capacity, the buyers capitalised their own. ACE (1985) and XL (1986) were formed in Bermuda by corporate buyers after the mid-1980s liability crisis, Oil Insurance Ltd. (1972), AEGIS (1975) and Energy Insurance Mutual (1986) are member-owned excess markets for energy and utilities, and American Nuclear Insurers (1957) pools the nuclear layer under Price-Anderson (all dates from memory, (U)). Why it hasn't happened here yet: the labs have investor cash, so each one is quietly self-insuring and no one has pooled the layers.

**One-liner.** A Bermuda-domiciled, member-owned excess liability mutual for independent frontier AI developers and the largest AI deployers, writing the $50M-$1B layers commercial markets will not, with training-data copyright carved out as uninsurable legal-regime risk.

**Customer.** About 10-15 independent frontier and near-frontier developers (the big tech groups self-insure), plus 200-400 large deployers (banks, insurers, health systems, retailers) that need excess above the $25-50M dedicated AI products.

**Payer and signer.** The General Counsel and CFO or Treasurer of each member, with board approval of the capital contribution. Budget: insurance and legal reserves. Today OpenAI reportedly holds about $300M of cover via Aon and is weighing a captive and self-insurance from roughly $60B of investor money (S, FT via secondary summaries; Reuters could not verify).

**Problem.** Insurers fear correlated, aggregated losses from a single upstream model. Aon's cyber head says the sector broadly lacks capacity for model providers (S). More than 60 P&C insurers filed AI exclusions for 2026, and AI-specific products top out at $25-50M (U). Anthropic's $1.5B authors' settlement was reported as funded from its own resources, not insurance (S, secondary). Self-insurance from the cap table is capital-inefficient and tells investors the risk is unmanaged.

**Solution.**
1. A mutual (Bermuda Class 3A/3B or a sponsored cell on an existing platform) owned by members, each contributing capital plus annual premium.
2. Layered limits (for example $50M xs $50M up to $500M), with reinsurance and insurance-linked securities above the mutual's retention.
3. Scope is idiosyncratic incident risk: wrongful death and bodily injury, defamation, privacy, agent-caused financial loss, regulatory defence. Training-data copyright is excluded because it is one correlated legal regime that hits every member at once and cannot be pooled; it stays with member balance sheets or a separate, openly retrospective layer.
4. Membership requires published safety practices and incident reporting, so the mutual sets the standard of care the way OIL and EIM do for their industries.
5. Retrospective assessments for the tail, and a seat at the table for any federal backstop (Stetler's 2025 three-tier proposal and a May 2026 arXiv paper both describe a pool plus government reinsurance; no bill exists (S)).

**Why now.** The first reported capacity shortfall at the top of the tower (OpenAI's $300M against multibillion-dollar claims); a reported self-insurance and captive discussion; carrier exclusions; the investors in the labs (hyperscalers, chip makers) benefit when customer indemnities become credible.

**Wedge / first 10.** Three or four labs plus 20 large deployers. Initial capital of $150-250M (A) from members, strategic investors and a reinsurer anchor, on a sponsored-cell platform so licensing does not gate launch.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).**
- Premium (A): 12 developers x $30M (anchored on roughly 8-10% rate-on-line for a $300M tower) = $360M, plus 250 deployers x $1.2M = $300M. About $660M in total.
- Sponsor and manager revenue (A): 10-15% in management and underwriting fees plus a profit commission = $80-110M. The manager does not own the premium; if it owns the paper, gross premium is the revenue line.
- **Share check.** $660M is about 14% of Deloitte's roughly $4.8B of 2032 global AI premium (S). That is heroic if Deloitte is right, but one company's reported $300M tower implies the forecast is low. I treat the path as plausible, not safe.

**Who does it today.** Aon-placed towers (OpenAI), Marsh, Lloyd's syndicates, Munich Re (aiSure for AI developers since about 2018 (S)), Armilla ($25M limits), AIUC, Corgi (Technology RRG, $4B reported valuation), Testudo ($10M); captives discussed by OpenAI (S); Anthropic funds settlements from its balance sheet (S). No lab-owned mutual found (searched).

**Why they lose.** Commercial carriers price for diversified books and cannot hold multibillion single-name limits with no loss history. The start-up carriers are capped at $10-50M by their capacity partners. A captive per lab cannot diversify or buy reinsurance as cheaply. Members who own the mutual share incident data and set safety conditions, which no seller of cover can require.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Scale economies (capital depth is the product at the top of the tower); cornered resource (member incident data, reinsurance relationships and founding-member capital); switching costs (member equity and retrospective liabilities); counter-positioning against stock carriers.

**Earned secret.** Capacity is not short because AI risk is unpriceable. It is short because the losses are correlated through one model and one legal regime. Split them: incident risk is poolable, legal-regime risk is not and should not be insured. The mutuals that endured wrote the layer their members could share and left the rest on balance sheets.

**First 90 days.** Interview GCs at five labs and ten large deployers on tower structure and renewal dates. Retain a Bermuda insurance counsel and a reinsurance broker to price a $50M xs $50M layer. Draft the incident taxonomy and the membership safety standard. Line up one anchor reinsurer and one strategic investor.

**Outside-the-box score.** 7

## 4. [pattern] Gate Meter: the AI liability premium on the metered bill

**Pattern transferred.** Two digest patterns, combined. "Control the repayment channel": the party that sits on the payment path gets paid first, with near-zero collection cost and no lapse. And usage-based auto insurance (Progressive's Snapshot, Root): price from observed behaviour at the place where behaviour is controlled. Why it hasn't happened here yet: AI cover is still sold by questionnaire through brokers, and the gateway vendors are not insurers.

**One-liner.** Usage-based AI liability cover sold as a toggle on the model gateway or cloud marketplace: the premium is a few basis points of the metered AI bill and is priced from runtime telemetry the gateway already sees (tool scopes, spend caps, guardrail hits, human-override rates, model versions), never from content.

**Customer.** Enterprises running production agents through a gateway or model platform (Portkey, LiteLLM, Cloudflare AI Gateway, Kong, and the cloud model platforms), and those vendors as distribution partners who share the premium.

**Payer and signer.** Head of AI platform or CISO turns it on; the CFO or VP Risk approves; the charge appears on the AI cost line. The gateway or cloud marketplace takes a share (U, mirrors cloud-marketplace resale terms).

**Problem.** Affirmative AI cover is priced from questionnaires and point-in-time tests, and testing costs more than a mid-market premium can carry (the pool file makes the same point). Exclusions force purchase but not at a price that reflects actual agent behaviour. There is no loss dataset linking behaviour to claims, so no one can price from experience.

**Solution.**
1. An MGA on Lloyd's or admitted paper with a gateway SDK or proxy that records minimal telemetry: tool permission scopes, spend caps, approval flows, guardrail triggers, model and version, never prompts or outputs.
2. Premium = metered usage x a risk multiplier with credits for controls (spend caps, scoped tools, human approval on money movement), like a driving score.
3. Billed on the same invoice through the cloud marketplace, so the premium is collected first and cannot lapse.
4. Evidence pre-captured for claims, and an exclusive incident-level loss dataset that grows with every insured deployment.

**Why now.** Enterprise agent deployments now route through gateways; cloud marketplaces make resale billing standard; sublimits and exclusions create the demand; the NAIC shows 55% of US cyber policies are endorsement-type with little underwriting data (S).

**Wedge / first 10.** One gateway vendor and one MGA partner; 10 enterprises that already pay for the gateway and have a customer contract demanding AI cover. Price a $1M-$5M limit as a percentage of monthly AI spend.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** Assumption-heavy.
- Enterprise generative-AI spend runs to tens of billions of dollars a year (U; Menlo's 2025 enterprise survey put it near $37B, from memory). Suppose $60-100B by 2029 with a quarter of it metered through gateways = $15-25B insured base (A).
- Premium of 1.5-3% of insured spend = $225M-$750M.
- MGA take of 25-30% = $55-225M. $100M needs roughly $350M of premium, which is the upper half of that range. Not heroic only if cover attaches to a large fraction of gateway traffic.

**Who does it today.** I found no telemetry-priced AI cover (searched; low-confidence negative). Adjacent: Armilla, AIUC, Corgi and Testudo sell questionnaire-and-test cover; Coalition's AI endorsement (Mar 2024) clarifies cyber triggers but is not usage-priced (S). The gateways (Portkey, LiteLLM, Cloudflare, Kong) and the cloud model platforms sell guardrails but no insurance. The hyperscalers' copyright indemnities are narrow and limited (U).

**Why they lose.** The gateways and clouds are not licensed and will not carry third-party liability on a balance sheet. Broker-sold MGAs cannot see runtime behaviour. Question-based underwriters cannot price at the speed or granularity of usage. Race-the-bundle risk is real: a hyperscaler could embed an insurer partner, so the MGA must own the loss dataset and a multi-gateway position.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Cornered resource (telemetry-to-loss dataset no one else has); switching costs (embedded in the billing path); process power in continuous pricing; scale economies in reinsurance terms.

**Earned secret.** Progressive and Root learned that observed behaviour at the control surface beats the application. For AI, the control surface is the gateway or the identity provider, not the insured's questionnaire. And a premium collected on the usage invoice never lapses.

**First 90 days.** Sign a design-partner letter with one gateway vendor. Define the minimal telemetry schema and have privacy counsel confirm it excludes content. Build a pricing prototype from public incident cases (AI Incident Database, court filings) and have two underwriters challenge it. Get an indicative quote from one reinsurer for a usage-priced quota share. Check file-and-use rules for usage-based commercial rating in five states.

**Outside-the-box score.** 8 (but see "unverified" at the end: the whitespace is a negative search result)
