Average asking lot rent about $752 a month in Q2 2025, +7.0% y/y (Northmarq) [S]; one broker says $780 [S, low authority]. That is about $9.0-9.4k a year per site [D].

At an assumed $95k average [A] that is about $5B a year, versus the pool file's $9-11B (FHFA-based). I use $5-11B.
- Price of the problem [S, FHFA NPRM as reported by HousingWire/NMN/ICBA]: chattel denial rate 65.6% vs 8.8% for site-built; average chattel rate 9.24% vs 6.63% for mortgages. Neither GSE has bought chattel loans for Duty to Serve credit so far.

Senate S.4804 (Manufactured Housing Lending Act, introduced 17 Jun 2026) would order GSE chattel pilots within 18 months of enactment; pending. The ROAD to Housing Act became law in July 2026 and removes the 1974 permanent-chassis requirement from the HUD-code definition; HUD has a 7-step implementation plan and a pending multistory proposal (12 Jun 2026); local zoning still decides where homes go [S, Multifamily Dive/HousingWire/Niskanen via search summaries].

## 1. Lot-Lender (lens: insider, a community manager with 20 years of park files)

- **One-liner:** A chattel lender that lends only inside land-lease communities that sign a three-party agreement, so the park's lot-rent ledger becomes the credit file and the collection channel.
- **Customer:** Households buying or replacing a home in a land-lease community, and the operators who need 1970s-80s homes replaced and vacant pads filled.
- **Payer and signer:** The borrower pays interest (about 9-10% gross [D from the FHFA 9.24% average]). The operator signs the master agreement (COO or VP Home Sales of a regional owner with 20-300 communities; its budget line is occupancy and home sales, not IT). Funding side: a warehouse lender, then ABS investors (the Forum/Saluda deals show they exist).
- **Problem:** A home in a leased-lot community is the only asset the lender holds, but the park controls the land under it. The lender cannot see the lot lease, rent increases or the park's approval of the buyer; the park cannot see whether the loan is current. A repossessed home becomes a liability to both: unpaid lot rent, a vacant pad, and a resale only the park can approve. Lenders price that blind spot into 9%+ rates, and 65.6% of applicants are denied [S].
- **Solution:** A master lender-park agreement: (a) lot-lease approval is part of underwriting; (b) lot rent and loan payment go out in one monthly draft with a waterfall; (c) the park shares the rent ledger (12-60 months of payment history is the best thin-file data a borrower has); (d) the park gives the lender 30 days' notice and a cure right before eviction; (e) pre-agreed voluntary surrender and in-place resale, so a default turns into a new sale on the same pad instead of a repossession. The operator gets filled pads and old homes replaced.
- **Why now:** Capital has just started to flow into chattel (Zippy Series C 14 Oct 2026, Forum's $150M commitment, Apollo). REIT occupancy is already 94-98%, so the remaining growth lever for operators is replacing aged homes. The FHFA proposal puts chattel on the policy agenda.
- **Wedge:** Purchase and replacement loans in 10-20 communities of one regional operator, in two or three states chosen after counsel reviews repossession and park-tenancy law (for example, Minnesota caps a repossessing lender's park-rent liability at three months under some conditions [S]). First 10 customers: ten regional owners with 20-100 communities each; one REIT pilot later.
- **Path to $100M:** About $1.0B of average receivables x about 10% gross yield (9.24% market average plus fees [D]) = about $100M. At a seven-year effective life and a 0.6 balance factor [A], that needs about $250M of originations a year at maturity, roughly 3,000 loans a year = about 6% of the 52,000 HMDA chattel loans a year, or 2.5-5% of $5-11B [D]. A ramp to $350M a year gets to $1B outstanding in about five years. Capital: about $150M of equity at an 85% advance [A]. Sanity check: Zippy's announced money ($20M equity plus a $150M purchase commitment spread over five years) is about one-sixth of what this path needs, so funding is the binding constraint. Gross revenue is not profit: with funding near 6-7% [A], opex near 2% [A] and losses 2-4% a year, profit only exists if park integration really cuts losses.
- **Who does it today:** Clayton's 21st Mortgage and Vanderbilt ($29.5B book, Berkshire-funded; no park contracts that I found), Triad (Champion JV), Cavco's CountryPlace [M], Cascade, Zippy (27 states), credit unions such as PremierOne (launched a chattel program Aug 2025). Operators sell homes themselves (UMH's home sales are about $50M a year [D from $12.8M in Q3]) and use third-party lenders. Free substitutes: seller financing, contract for deed, personal loans.
- **Why they lose:** No incumbent has a contract with the park; their channel is dealers. The honest caveat: they do not lose on price, because Clayton's funding is cheaper than anyone's. The bet is on lower losses and higher approval rates, which are untested.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Cornered resource (exclusive operator agreements plus the rent-ledger data) and switching costs (ledger integration). Weak until a loss-rate edge is proven.
- **Earned secret [hypothesis]:** A park manager knows on day 3 of a late payment whether a household is going to default; the lender learns 60-90 days later. Both want the same outcome (the household stays and pays) and no contract aligns them.
- **First 90 days:** Letters of intent from three operators. Get 24 months of anonymised lot-rent ledgers plus loan performance on 5,000+ homes from one operator and test whether 60-day rent history predicts default better than FICO. Counsel drafts the master agreement in two states. One warehouse term sheet.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 5.
- **Risks to kill it:** Rent history adds no predictive power; operators already have exclusive deals with 21st or Vanderbilt; state park-tenancy law blocks lender cure rights; cost of funds.

## 2. Open Lot (lens: insider, a dealership general manager)

- **One-liner:** A multi-brand retail chain built by buying independent dealers, with every lender competing for each buyer, in-house set crews, and the finance and insurance economics kept inside the chain.
- **Customer:** Home buyers (about 104,000 new homes a year) and the plants that currently depend on brand-locked retail.
- **Payer and signer:** The buyer pays the home price and finance charges. The signers are the dealer owners who sell (about 1,200 companies [S, undated]) and the plant sales VPs who give volume terms. Capital: acquirer equity plus floor-plan lines.
- **Problem:** The three integrated players (Clayton, Cavco, Champion) sell their own brands through their own lenders. Independents, who make up most of the roughly 1,200 dealer companies, borrow floor plan from, and send customer files to, a lender that belongs to their biggest competitor: 21st Mortgage floors 22,000+ units and calls itself the largest floor-plan lender [S]. Champion went from 18 factory-direct stores to 73 in two years [S], which shows manufacturers are buying distribution because independents are weak.
- **Solution:** Buy 15-25 independents in three or four states over three years. Sell several brands. Route every application to four or five lenders at once so approvals and price compete. Keep the F&I (finance and insurance) reserve in a captive structure where state law allows. Add in-house set crews and warranty. Attach the Park Shield insurance program (idea 3).
- **Why now:** Shipments are -7.3% YTD, so weaker independents are for sale and plants want volume. Chattel capital is entering (Zippy, Forum, Saluda), which creates more lender choice. Champion's retail push proves the distribution fight is on.
- **Wedge:** Three to five dealers in one state to prove same-store approval rate and margin. First 10 customers are the first 10 dealer sellers.
- **Path to $100M:** Average dealer revenue is about $9M ($11B / 1,200 [D], undated source). $100M = about 11 average dealers = about 760 homes a year at $131k [D] = 0.7% of shipments. Capital: if dealers trade at a low multiple [A], $30-70M including inventory. This is the easiest path to $100M of revenue in the pool, and the thinnest moat.
- **Who does it today:** Clayton Homes retail with 21st Mortgage; Cavco and its Palm Harbor stores [M]; Champion Homes (73 factory-direct locations in 2024, bought Iseman, agreed to buy 11 Homes Direct stores); independent regional chains [M]. Free substitute: the independent dealer as it exists.
- **Why they lose:** A brand-locked retailer cannot be neutral: Clayton cannot send its buyer to a rival's lender or sell a rival's home first. The independent's flaw is scale.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Scale economies in wholesale terms if plants are underused [A], and little else. Honest verdict: this is a distribution roll-up; it is worth building only if the lender and insurance attach economics are retained.
- **Earned secret [hypothesis]:** At a dealership the sale is decided by which lender approves, not by the home. The independent dealer feeds its buyers' credit files and prices to a competitor, who rations approvals.
- **First 90 days:** Interview 20 dealer owners about lender approval rates and floor-plan terms; get approval-rate data from five; test whether a four-lender waterfall raises approvals. Sign an LOI with two dealers in one state. Ask two plants for volume pricing.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 3.
- **Risks to kill it:** Floor plan from 21st can be pulled; dealer reserve rules (state law and the fair-lending rules that hit indirect auto) limit the F&I economics; margin of the retail layer is thin.

## 3. Park Shield (lens: pattern, "control the repayment channel")

- **One-liner:** A manufactured-home insurance program underwritten community by community, enrolled through the lease and paid in the monthly rent draft, so the insurer is paid first and the park enforces the mitigation that makes the risk insurable.
- **Customer:** Households in land-lease communities (about 3.5-3.9M homes [D: 4.3M sites, 80-90% owner-occupied, assumed]) and the operators who bear fire and wind loss from uninsured homes.
- **Payer and signer:** The homeowner pays $1,000-1,300 a year [S, low authority] through the rent draft. The operator signs the program (VP Operations or CFO of a regional owner) via a lease addendum. Lenders already require cover.
- **Problem:** Standard policies are priced per home from age, location and construction. They do not credit what the park controls: tie-down certification, skirting, lot spacing, hydrant access, 1970s wiring and gas lines. Premiums rise (a Massachusetts report in Jan 2026 describes residents paying hundreds more) and lapses are common [S, anecdotal].
- **Solution:** An MGA on a fronting carrier with reinsurance, later a carrier, writing one program per community with rating credit for mitigation the park enforces and verifies. Default enrollment with opt-out (and a proof-of-other-coverage path). Premium collected on the rent invoice. Claims data feeds the next year's community rating.
- **Why now:** Insurers are repricing; Foremost, American Modern and CoverTree are individual-policy businesses. Straight-through MGA tooling now exists (CoverTree says 92%). Operators are already in a rent-increase fight, and a bundled lower premium helps them.
- **Wedge:** One operator with 30-60 communities in one state and its claim history. First 10 customers: the first 10 communities of that operator.
- **Path to $100M:** The premium pool for homes in communities is about 3.5M x $1,100 = about $3.9B [D/A]. $100M of gross written premium = about 91,000 policies = about 2.6% of that base. If revenue means MGA commission (15-20% [A]), $100M needs $500-650M of premium = 13-17% share, which is heroic. So the idea only meets the bar as a risk-bearing program carrier. Carrier capital: surplus of $50M+ [A] with reinsurance.
- **Who does it today:** Foremost (Farmers), American Modern (Munich Re [M]), Assurant lender-placed [M], State Farm and Progressive for some homes [M], CoverTree (MGA; $10M seed 2022, $13M Series A 2024; Markel paper per one profile). Free substitute: no insurance, which is why parks care.
- **Why they lose:** Agent- and lender-sold carriers cannot see the park, cannot collect through rent and cannot enforce mitigation. A park program needs a lease addendum, which only the operator can sign.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Cornered resource (exclusive park programs and community claims data) plus switching costs (the premium rides the rent bill).
- **Earned secret [hypothesis]:** The park knows which homes are tied down, skirted and rewired, and the lease can force it. The insurer's pricing treats those homes as the average, so the best homes subsidise the worst.
- **First 90 days:** Collect three years of claims and mitigation data from one operator. Counsel memo on licensing, referral-fee limits and opt-out enrollment in the first two states (a park must not act as an unlicensed agent). Term sheets from a fronting carrier and a reinsurer. Pilot in three communities.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 6.
- **Risks to kill it:** Tying and unlicensed-agent law; adverse selection from opt-out; hurricane concentration drives reinsurance cost; state rate filings; operators decline the liability.

## 4. Park Utility Co (lens: pattern, "funded programs pass through to scarce trades", plus utility roll-up)

- **One-liner:** A water and sewer utility for manufactured-home communities: buys or operates the failing private systems under the parks, rebuilds them with state and federal money plus rate-based financing, and bills a utility charge instead of a lot-rent spike.
- **Customer:** Owners of 1960s-80s communities with private wells, septic and sewer lines; resident-owned co-ops; state agencies that run park-infrastructure grant programs.
- **Payer and signer:** The park owner or co-op pays a monthly utility charge (about $60-110 a site [A]; typical US water+sewer bills run near this level [M]). Signers: owner, co-op board president, or the state housing / environment agency that awards the grant (for example Minnesota Housing [M]).
- **Problem:** Aging water, sewer, electrical and roads are common in manufactured-home communities and owners cannot fund fixes without large rent increases. State grant demand far exceeds supply: Minnesota awarded $43.9M of $84.8M requested since 2020 and 21% of 2024 requests [S]. Costs run about $50k and $67k per home in two small Maine co-ops (n=2) [S/D]. In Delaware 98 of 170+ communities use on-site wastewater, many beyond a 30-year life [S].
- **Solution:** Standard assessment, design and construction packages, grant and revolving-fund applications written by the same team, a long-term O&M contract, and an option to transfer to the town once the system is up to standard. The utility is a separate asset from the land, so it can be financed on its own cash flow.
- **Why now:** ARPA and infrastructure-law money is being spent now and is one-time (Vermont put $26M+ of ARPA into parks) [S]; owners are under political fire for rent increases; private-equity buyers price deferred maintenance into rent.
- **Wedge:** Design-build and grant packaging for 10-20 mid-size parks in two states with active programs (Minnesota, Vermont, New Hampshire, Maine, Delaware). First customers: resident-owned co-ops that already have technical-assistance partners.
- **Path to $100M:** Two routes. (1) EPC revenue: a 100-home park at about $25k a site = $2.5M, so 40 parks a year = $100M [D/A]. The one state with data requested about $17M a year of grants, so this needs a dozen states' grants plus private financing, and the one-time money fades. (2) Utility revenue: $100M / ($90 x 12) = about 92,600 sites = 2.2% of the 4.3M sites; if the Delaware ratio held nationally (58% on-site systems [D, one state]), about 2.5M sites are on such systems, so the need is about 3.7% of them. Rate base for 92,600 sites at $25k = about $2.3B [D/A], so route 2 needs an infrastructure-fund sponsor, not a seed round. No national backlog estimate exists; the first job is to size it from a sample of 50 parks.
- **Who does it today:** Park owners themselves (RUBS billing firms such as Conservice [M]); Nexus Water Group (ex Corix) [M] and Essential Utilities [M] for small systems; municipal annexation; nonprofits (ROC USA and its technical-assistance partners) for co-ops; state grant programs.
- **Why they lose:** Small-system utilities want towns, not 90-home parks with a 70-year-old pipe network and a landlord dispute; nonprofits are capital-constrained; owners will not sell the pipes unless the deal lowers their rent-increase risk.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Process power (standard designs and packaging) and, for route 2, scale economies in operations. Honest: moat is thin unless the utility asset is regulated or contracted for decades.
- **Earned secret [hypothesis]:** Owners defer what residents cannot see; the pipe is a separate business from the land, and splitting it out is the only way to finance it without a rent hike.
- **First 90 days:** Sample 50 parks across three states for system type, age and condition; map the funded programs and their annual size; get a PUC memo on utility jurisdiction in two states; one design-build partner.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 6.
- **Risks to kill it:** One-time grant money ends; public-utility regulation and rate cases; residents see a utility charge as another rent increase; capital intensity.
