## 1. Lot-Lender (lens: insider, a community manager with 20 years of park files)

- **One-liner:** A chattel lender that lends only inside land-lease communities that sign a three-party agreement, so the park's lot-rent ledger becomes the credit file and the collection channel.
- **Customer:** Households buying or replacing a home in a land-lease community, and the operators who need 1970s-80s homes replaced and vacant pads filled.
- **Payer and signer:** The borrower pays interest (about 9-10% gross [D from the FHFA 9.24% average]). The operator signs the master agreement (COO or VP Home Sales of a regional owner with 20-300 communities; its budget line is occupancy and home sales, not IT). Funding side: a warehouse lender, then ABS investors (the Forum/Saluda deals show they exist).
- **Problem:** A home in a leased-lot community is the only asset the lender holds, but the park controls the land under it. The lender cannot see the lot lease, rent increases or the park's approval of the buyer; the park cannot see whether the loan is current. A repossessed home becomes a liability to both: unpaid lot rent, a vacant pad, and a resale only the park can approve. Lenders price that blind spot into 9%+ rates, and 65.6% of applicants are denied [S].
- **Solution:** A master lender-park agreement: (a) lot-lease approval is part of underwriting; (b) lot rent and loan payment go out in one monthly draft with a waterfall; (c) the park shares the rent ledger (12-60 months of payment history is the best thin-file data a borrower has); (d) the park gives the lender 30 days' notice and a cure right before eviction; (e) pre-agreed voluntary surrender and in-place resale, so a default turns into a new sale on the same pad instead of a repossession. The operator gets filled pads and old homes replaced.
- **Why now:** Capital has just started to flow into chattel (Zippy Series C 14 Oct 2026, Forum's $150M commitment, Apollo). REIT occupancy is already 94-98%, so the remaining growth lever for operators is replacing aged homes. The FHFA proposal puts chattel on the policy agenda.
- **Wedge:** Purchase and replacement loans in 10-20 communities of one regional operator, in two or three states chosen after counsel reviews repossession and park-tenancy law (for example, Minnesota caps a repossessing lender's park-rent liability at three months under some conditions [S]). First 10 customers: ten regional owners with 20-100 communities each; one REIT pilot later.
- **Path to $100M:** About $1.0B of average receivables x about 10% gross yield (9.24% market average plus fees [D]) = about $100M. At a seven-year effective life and a 0.6 balance factor [A], that needs about $250M of originations a year at maturity, roughly 3,000 loans a year = about 6% of the 52,000 HMDA chattel loans a year, or 2.5-5% of $5-11B [D]. A ramp to $350M a year gets to $1B outstanding in about five years. Capital: about $150M of equity at an 85% advance [A]. Sanity check: Zippy's announced money ($20M equity plus a $150M purchase commitment spread over five years) is about one-sixth of what this path needs, so funding is the binding constraint. Gross revenue is not profit: with funding near 6-7% [A], opex near 2% [A] and losses 2-4% a year, profit only exists if park integration really cuts losses.
- **Who does it today:** Clayton's 21st Mortgage and Vanderbilt ($29.5B book, Berkshire-funded; no park contracts that I found), Triad (Champion JV), Cavco's CountryPlace [M], Cascade, Zippy (27 states), credit unions such as PremierOne (launched a chattel program Aug 2025). Operators sell homes themselves (UMH's home sales are about $50M a year [D from $12.8M in Q3]) and use third-party lenders. Free substitutes: seller financing, contract for deed, personal loans.
- **Why they lose:** No incumbent has a contract with the park; their channel is dealers. The honest caveat: they do not lose on price, because Clayton's funding is cheaper than anyone's. The bet is on lower losses and higher approval rates, which are untested.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Cornered resource (exclusive operator agreements plus the rent-ledger data) and switching costs (ledger integration). Weak until a loss-rate edge is proven.
- **Earned secret [hypothesis]:** A park manager knows on day 3 of a late payment whether a household is going to default; the lender learns 60-90 days later. Both want the same outcome (the household stays and pays) and no contract aligns them.
- **First 90 days:** Letters of intent from three operators. Get 24 months of anonymised lot-rent ledgers plus loan performance on 5,000+ homes from one operator and test whether 60-day rent history predicts default better than FICO. Counsel drafts the master agreement in two states. One warehouse term sheet.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 5.
- **Risks to kill it:** Rent history adds no predictive power; operators already have exclusive deals with 21st or Vanderbilt; state park-tenancy law blocks lender cure rights; cost of funds.

## 2. Open Lot (lens: insider, a dealership general manager)

- **One-liner:** A multi-brand retail chain built by buying independent dealers, with every lender competing for each buyer, in-house set crews, and the finance and insurance economics kept inside the chain.
- **Customer:** Home buyers (about 104,000 new homes a year) and the plants that currently depend on brand-locked retail.
- **Payer and signer:** The buyer pays the home price and finance charges. The signers are the dealer owners who sell (about 1,200 companies [S, undated]) and the plant sales VPs who give volume terms. Capital: acquirer equity plus floor-plan lines.
- **Problem:** The three integrated players (Clayton, Cavco, Champion) sell their own brands through their own lenders. Independents, who make up most of the roughly 1,200 dealer companies, borrow floor plan from, and send customer files to, a lender that belongs to their biggest competitor: 21st Mortgage floors 22,000+ units and calls itself the largest floor-plan lender [S]. Champion went from 18 factory-direct stores to 73 in two years [S], which shows manufacturers are buying distribution because independents are weak.
- **Solution:** Buy 15-25 independents in three or four states over three years. Sell several brands. Route every application to four or five lenders at once so approvals and price compete. Keep the F&I (finance and insurance) reserve in a captive structure where state law allows. Add in-house set crews and warranty. Attach the Park Shield insurance program (idea 3).
- **Why now:** Shipments are -7.3% YTD, so weaker independents are for sale and plants want volume. Chattel capital is entering (Zippy, Forum, Saluda), which creates more lender choice. Champion's retail push proves the distribution fight is on.
- **Wedge:** Three to five dealers in one state to prove same-store approval rate and margin. First 10 customers are the first 10 dealer sellers.
- **Path to $100M:** Average dealer revenue is about $9M ($11B / 1,200 [D], undated source). $100M = about 11 average dealers = about 760 homes a year at $131k [D] = 0.7% of shipments. Capital: if dealers trade at a low multiple [A], $30-70M including inventory. This is the easiest path to $100M of revenue in the pool, and the thinnest moat.
- **Who does it today:** Clayton Homes retail with 21st Mortgage; Cavco and its Palm Harbor stores [M]; Champion Homes (73 factory-direct locations in 2024, bought Iseman, agreed to buy 11 Homes Direct stores); independent regional chains [M]. Free substitute: the independent dealer as it exists.
- **Why they lose:** A brand-locked retailer cannot be neutral: Clayton cannot send its buyer to a rival's lender or sell a rival's home first. The independent's flaw is scale.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Scale economies in wholesale terms if plants are underused [A], and little else. Honest verdict: this is a distribution roll-up; it is worth building only if the lender and insurance attach economics are retained.
- **Earned secret [hypothesis]:** At a dealership the sale is decided by which lender approves, not by the home. The independent dealer feeds its buyers' credit files and prices to a competitor, who rations approvals.
- **First 90 days:** Interview 20 dealer owners about lender approval rates and floor-plan terms; get approval-rate data from five; test whether a four-lender waterfall raises approvals. Sign an LOI with two dealers in one state. Ask two plants for volume pricing.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 3.
- **Risks to kill it:** Floor plan from 21st can be pulled; dealer reserve rules (state law and the fair-lending rules that hit indirect auto) limit the F&I economics; margin of the retail layer is thin.

## 3. Park Shield (lens: pattern, "control the repayment channel")

- **One-liner:** A manufactured-home insurance program underwritten community by community, enrolled through the lease and paid in the monthly rent draft, so the insurer is paid first and the park enforces the mitigation that makes the risk insurable.
- **Customer:** Households in land-lease communities (about 3.5-3.9M homes [D: 4.3M sites, 80-90% owner-occupied, assumed]) and the operators who bear fire and wind loss from uninsured homes.
- **Payer and signer:** The homeowner pays $1,000-1,300 a year [S, low authority] through the rent draft. The operator signs the program (VP Operations or CFO of a regional owner) via a lease addendum. Lenders already require cover.
- **Problem:** Standard policies are priced per home from age, location and construction. They do not credit what the park controls: tie-down certification, skirting, lot spacing, hydrant access, 1970s wiring and gas lines. Premiums rise (a Massachusetts report in Jan 2026 describes residents paying hundreds more) and lapses are common [S, anecdotal].
- **Solution:** An MGA on a fronting carrier with reinsurance, later a carrier, writing one program per community with rating credit for mitigation the park enforces and verifies. Default enrollment with opt-out (and a proof-of-other-coverage path). Premium collected on the rent invoice. Claims data feeds the next year's community rating.
- **Why now:** Insurers are repricing; Foremost, American Modern and CoverTree are individual-policy businesses. Straight-through MGA tooling now exists (CoverTree says 92%). Operators are already in a rent-increase fight, and a bundled lower premium helps them.
- **Wedge:** One operator with 30-60 communities in one state and its claim history. First 10 customers: the first 10 communities of that operator.
- **Path to $100M:** The premium pool for homes in communities is about 3.5M x $1,100 = about $3.9B [D/A]. $100M of gross written premium = about 91,000 policies = about 2.6% of that base. If revenue means MGA commission (15-20% [A]), $100M needs $500-650M of premium = 13-17% share, which is heroic. So the idea only meets the bar as a risk-bearing program carrier. Carrier capital: surplus of $50M+ [A] with reinsurance.
- **Who does it today:** Foremost (Farmers), American Modern (Munich Re [M]), Assurant lender-placed [M], State Farm and Progressive for some homes [M], CoverTree (MGA; $10M seed 2022, $13M Series A 2024; Markel paper per one profile). Free substitute: no insurance, which is why parks care.
- **Why they lose:** Agent- and lender-sold carriers cannot see the park, cannot collect through rent and cannot enforce mitigation. A park program needs a lease addendum, which only the operator can sign.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Cornered resource (exclusive park programs and community claims data) plus switching costs (the premium rides the rent bill).
- **Earned secret [hypothesis]:** The park knows which homes are tied down, skirted and rewired, and the lease can force it. The insurer's pricing treats those homes as the average, so the best homes subsidise the worst.
- **First 90 days:** Collect three years of claims and mitigation data from one operator. Counsel memo on licensing, referral-fee limits and opt-out enrollment in the first two states (a park must not act as an unlicensed agent). Term sheets from a fronting carrier and a reinsurer. Pilot in three communities.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 6.
- **Risks to kill it:** Tying and unlicensed-agent law; adverse selection from opt-out; hurricane concentration drives reinsurance cost; state rate filings; operators decline the liability.

## 4. Park Utility Co (lens: pattern, "funded programs pass through to scarce trades", plus utility roll-up)

- **One-liner:** A water and sewer utility for manufactured-home communities: buys or operates the failing private systems under the parks, rebuilds them with state and federal money plus rate-based financing, and bills a utility charge instead of a lot-rent spike.
- **Customer:** Owners of 1960s-80s communities with private wells, septic and sewer lines; resident-owned co-ops; state agencies that run park-infrastructure grant programs.
- **Payer and signer:** The park owner or co-op pays a monthly utility charge (about $60-110 a site [A]; typical US water+sewer bills run near this level [M]). Signers: owner, co-op board president, or the state housing / environment agency that awards the grant (for example Minnesota Housing [M]).
- **Problem:** Aging water, sewer, electrical and roads are common in manufactured-home communities and owners cannot fund fixes without large rent increases. State grant demand far exceeds supply: Minnesota awarded $43.9M of $84.8M requested since 2020 and 21% of 2024 requests [S]. Costs run about $50k and $67k per home in two small Maine co-ops (n=2) [S/D]. In Delaware 98 of 170+ communities use on-site wastewater, many beyond a 30-year life [S].
- **Solution:** Standard assessment, design and construction packages, grant and revolving-fund applications written by the same team, a long-term O&M contract, and an option to transfer to the town once the system is up to standard. The utility is a separate asset from the land, so it can be financed on its own cash flow.
- **Why now:** ARPA and infrastructure-law money is being spent now and is one-time (Vermont put $26M+ of ARPA into parks) [S]; owners are under political fire for rent increases; private-equity buyers price deferred maintenance into rent.
- **Wedge:** Design-build and grant packaging for 10-20 mid-size parks in two states with active programs (Minnesota, Vermont, New Hampshire, Maine, Delaware). First customers: resident-owned co-ops that already have technical-assistance partners.
- **Path to $100M:** Two routes. (1) EPC revenue: a 100-home park at about $25k a site = $2.5M, so 40 parks a year = $100M [D/A]. The one state with data requested about $17M a year of grants, so this needs a dozen states' grants plus private financing, and the one-time money fades. (2) Utility revenue: $100M / ($90 x 12) = about 92,600 sites = 2.2% of the 4.3M sites; if the Delaware ratio held nationally (58% on-site systems [D, one state]), about 2.5M sites are on such systems, so the need is about 3.7% of them. Rate base for 92,600 sites at $25k = about $2.3B [D/A], so route 2 needs an infrastructure-fund sponsor, not a seed round. No national backlog estimate exists; the first job is to size it from a sample of 50 parks.
- **Who does it today:** Park owners themselves (RUBS billing firms such as Conservice [M]); Nexus Water Group (ex Corix) [M] and Essential Utilities [M] for small systems; municipal annexation; nonprofits (ROC USA and its technical-assistance partners) for co-ops; state grant programs.
- **Why they lose:** Small-system utilities want towns, not 90-home parks with a 70-year-old pipe network and a landlord dispute; nonprofits are capital-constrained; owners will not sell the pipes unless the deal lowers their rent-increase risk.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Process power (standard designs and packaging) and, for route 2, scale economies in operations. Honest: moat is thin unless the utility asset is regulated or contracted for decades.
- **Earned secret [hypothesis]:** Owners defer what residents cannot see; the pipe is a separate business from the land, and splitting it out is the only way to finance it without a rent hike.
- **First 90 days:** Sample 50 parks across three states for system type, age and condition; map the funded programs and their annual size; get a PUC memo on utility jurisdiction in two states; one design-build partner.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 6.
- **Risks to kill it:** One-time grant money ends; public-utility regulation and rate cases; residents see a utility charge as another rent increase; capital intensity.

## 5. Covenant Parks (lens: dilemma, what REIT and private-equity owners structurally cannot promise)

- **One-liner:** An owner-operator that buys manufactured-home communities under a recorded covenant (capped rent growth, a pre-granted resident right to buy, a resident equity glide path), financed with agency tenant-protection loans.
- **Customer:** Retiring owners of small and mid-size communities (most of the 43,000 are small), residents, and impact and pension capital that wants yield without a rent-gouging label.
- **Payer and signer:** Residents pay lot rent (about $9.0-9.4k a site a year [D]). Signers: the selling owner (or the seller's broker in a right-of-first-refusal state) and the investment committee of a pension, insurer or impact fund. Debt: a Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac MHC lender.
- **Problem:** Residents own the home but rent the land, so the owner's rent increases (ELS core MH rent +5.6% in Q2 2026; UMH same-store rental +8.7% [S]) compound against fixed incomes (about 70% of ELS's MH communities are senior-oriented [S]). States are responding: NY (2023), Maine (2025-26) and others have right-of-first-refusal laws, PA and MI bills are in committee, and a federal bill was introduced in Apr 2026 [S]. A court has held a resident association's right ended when it missed a statutory deadline [S], so sellers also want a clean process.
- **Solution:** Buy at market, record a covenant enforceable by a residents' association: rent growth capped at CPI or a fixed percentage for 10 years, a pre-agreed formula price and 90-day path for residents to buy, and an equity credit funded from NOI above a threshold that converts toward a resident-owned purchase in years 7-10. Fannie's own tenant-protection terms (one-year renewable lease, 30 days' notice of increases, 5-day late grace [S]) are the floor; the covenant goes beyond them.
- **Why now:** Political pressure on private-equity owners is rising; agencies already price tenant-protected communities (Fannie financed 122 properties and 19,808 pads in 2024 [S]); owners in right-of-first-refusal states need a buyer who will not get litigated.
- **Wedge:** Two to four communities of 50-150 sites in a right-of-first-refusal state (NY or Maine) and two in a state without one, bought from owners aged 65+. First 10 customers: ten sellers, found through brokers and the state manufactured housing association.
- **Path to $100M:** $100M / $9,000-9,400 per site = about 10,700-11,100 sites = about 110 communities of 100 sites = about 0.26% of the 4.3M homesites [D]. Assets: Fannie's 2024 deliveries average about $47.6k of UPB per pad [D, mission-adjusted figure], so about $70k of value per pad at 65-70% LTV [A] = about $0.75B of assets and about $250-300M of equity. Time: six to eight years at 15 communities a year. Share is trivial; capital and the sellers' price expectations are the constraints.
- **Who does it today:** ELS, Sun, UMH (public REITs); Havenpark, Yes! Communities and Apollo-backed funds [M]; ROC USA (nonprofit, about 346 resident-owned communities, $450M+ financed since 2008, a $47M acquisition pool). Free substitute: the owner sells to whoever pays most.
- **Why they lose:** REIT guidance and exit values depend on 5-6% annual rent growth, so they cannot promise caps or equity to residents. ROC USA cannot deploy more than its grant-and-CDFI capital. A for-profit covenant owner is neither.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Counter-positioning (the REITs cannot copy it without cutting their own rent growth); cornered resource in ROFR states (sellers' trust).
- **Earned secret [hypothesis]:** In a leased-land community, the rent schedule is the largest driver of what a resident's home is worth on resale, so a recorded cap should raise home values; that is something the owner can sell.
- **First 90 days:** Draft the covenant and a residents' association charter with a landlord-tenant lawyer in NY and one other state. Ask a Fannie/Freddie MHC lender whether a covenant earns tenant-protection pricing. Underwrite five parks. Line up $50M of impact capital.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 5.
- **Risks to kill it:** Sellers will not accept a covenant discount outside ROFR states; capped rent makes returns too low for institutional equity; a covenant may complicate agency underwriting.

## 6. Deposit Bridge (lens: dilemma, deposit-funded lenders cannot run chattel and chattel specialists cannot use deposits)

- **One-liner:** A credit-union service organization (CUSO) that gives member credit unions a turnkey chattel program (origination, underwriting standard, servicing, repossession and title in every state) and lets them sell and buy participations, so deposit funding finally reaches manufactured-home loans.
- **Customer:** Credit unions, especially rural and community ones whose members live in manufactured homes.
- **Payer and signer:** The credit union pays an origination fee and a servicing fee out of the loan yield. Signers: the CU's chief lending officer and CEO, with ALCO and board approval; each founding CU commits capital to the CUSO.
- **Problem:** Banks have been leaving chattel; the three largest lenders (about 76%, undated [S]) are non-depository and one is funded by Berkshire, not by deposits. Credit unions already originate these loans and lobby for them (America's Credit Unions, 24 Jul 2026; PremierOne launched a chattel program Aug 2025 [S]), but each one originates a handful of loans and cannot staff repossession, title perfection and servicing across states.
- **Solution:** A CUSO owned by credit unions: a dealer and direct origination channel; one underwriting standard; sub-servicing and repossession and resale (the Lot-Lender function) in all states; and a participation market where the originating CU keeps 80-90% and sells the rest to others. Later, a securitization shelf for members.
- **Why now:** Chattel is on FHFA's agenda; credit unions say they want secondary-market infrastructure; capital from outside the industry has just arrived (Zippy, Forum, Apollo), which shows the loans are bankable at about 9%; deposit costs are lower than warehouse and ABS costs [A, unquantified here].
- **Wedge:** Servicing, repossession and title as a shared service for 10-20 credit unions that already make some chattel loans. First 10 customers: those credit unions in Texas, the Southeast, Michigan, Washington and similar states.
- **Path to $100M:** A managed book of about $6B (about 10% of a $45-65B stock [A]). Servicing 0.55% x $6B = about $33M; origination 1.25% x $1.2B a year = about $15M; a retained 10% slice of $600M at 9.5% = about $57M of gross interest; total about $105M [D/A]. That is 11-24% of annual originations ($5-11B) and is heroic: it needs credit unions to go from a small share to a large one within about eight years.
- **Who does it today:** Triad markets chattel to credit unions (I could not read the program terms) [S]; individual credit unions (PremierOne and others); Cascade and Zippy sell to investors, not to credit unions. I did not find a CUSO for manufactured housing.
- **Why they lose:** Triad, Clayton and the others earn on the spread and would not hand a credit union's deposit-funded scale a reason to compete; they also cannot use deposits. Credit unions alone cannot cover 50 states of repossession law.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Scale economies in servicing and repossession; the owners are the customers (cornered resource).
- **Earned secret [hypothesis]:** A credit union's compliance officer is less afraid of chattel credit than of running a repossession in a state where they have 12 loans; the missing piece is operations, not capital.
- **First 90 days:** Interview 25 credit unions on volume, losses and appetite. Get five founding CUs at $2-5M each. Counsel on the CUSO structure under NCUA rules [M: 12 CFR Part 712] and participation rules. Sign one sub-servicer and one repossession and remarketing vendor.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 5.
- **Risks to kill it:** Credit union appetite for 10%-loss-type collateral (Kazdin's modelled lifetime loss; Clayton's realised losses are much lower) may be low; regulators may not allow the structure; Triad may already do the job.

## 7. Title I Reborn (lens: shock, FHA Title I plus Ginnie Mae as the live government wrapper for chattel)

- **One-liner:** A Ginnie Mae manufactured-home MBS issuer that aggregates FHA Title I chattel loans from credit unions, banks and non-Berkshire lenders, so the largest unfunded part of the market gets a government-guaranteed funding path now, and a GSE exit later if the pilots happen.
- **Customer:** Originators outside Berkshire (credit unions, community banks, Triad-type finance companies, new lenders) and, through them, chattel borrowers.
- **Payer and signer:** Originators pay a gain-on-sale discount and a servicing strip; borrowers pay the FHA premium inside the loan. Signers: the originator's CFO or treasurer (funding is their budget line) and Ginnie Mae / HUD approvals.
- **Problem:** Private chattel funding is expensive: a Fitch-rated Cascade deal (likely 2024) carried 36.6% credit enhancement on collateral with a weighted FICO of 637 [S]. Lenders price for that, hence 9%+ rates and 65.6% denials. FHA Title I can insure up to 90% of the loss on each loan and Ginnie can wrap the pool, but the program has been dormant ("no loans have been made in recent years", Pew 2024 [S]).
- **Solution:** Make Title I the standard product of a new issuer: closed-loan purchase from correspondents, a Title I underwriting and claims desk, and pooling into Ginnie Mae manufactured-home MBS. In return the borrower rate could fall below the 9.24% market average [A: depends on FHA premium rates I did not find].
- **Why now:** HUD indexed the limits to $105,532 / $193,719 in Mar 2024 with annual review; Ginnie cut issuer requirements in Feb 2024 (the old rule was net worth of $10M plus 10% of outstanding MH MBS); the FHFA proposal and S.4804 push chattel onto the policy agenda; a House modernization bill was circulated in May 2026 [S].
- **Wedge:** Buy Title I loans from 10 credit unions and small lenders in two states. First 10 customers: the credit unions of idea 6 (the two ideas can share an origination base).
- **Path to $100M:** Gain on sale at 2-3% [A] x $2.0B a year = $50M; a retained servicing book of about $6B x 0.6% = $36M; warehouse carry and fees about $14M [A]. $2.0B a year is 20-40% of annual chattel originations ($5-11B) and is heroic. It only works if Title I becomes the default box for the 25-35% of the market not funded by Berkshire.
- **Who does it today:** Effectively nobody at scale: Pew reports few participating lenders and no recent loans [S]. Private-label chattel ABS (Cascade, Clayton and Cavco deals) is the substitute.
- **Why they lose:** Berkshire's lenders do not need Ginnie (cheapest funding in the industry) and would cannibalise their own spread. Others had a barrier (net worth, low limits) that fell in 2024.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Cornered resource (HUD and Ginnie approvals plus a working Title I claims process) and process power (the only team that has made the claims process work).
- **Earned secret [hypothesis]:** The binding constraint on chattel pricing is funding liquidity, not borrower credit. A guarantee converts an asset that funds at a 36.6% enhancement into one that funds at a Treasury-linked spread.
- **First 90 days:** Find out why the program is dormant: ask HUD's Title I desk for claim approval rates and processing times, interview two former Title I lenders, read Ginnie's MH MBS guide for the current net worth and G-fee. Model the all-in borrower rate including the FHA premium. If the all-in rate is not below ~8.5% [A], stop.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 5.
- **Risks to kill it:** The program is dormant for reasons the 2024 changes did not fix (premium cost, slow claims, loan caps); the portfolio-reserve cap may still bind older rules; investors may not buy thin MH MBS.

## 8. Keyring (lens: shock, a home-only landlord for the buyers the lenders turn away)

- **One-liner:** A fund-backed landlord that buys new HUD-code homes at fleet prices, places them on vacant pads in other people's communities under a three-party agreement, and offers a rent-to-own path to residents who were denied credit.
- **Customer:** Households denied chattel credit (about 66% of applicants [S]) who can afford about $1,000 a month for the home, and small and mid-size park owners who have pads but no capital to replace old homes.
- **Payer and signer:** The resident pays home rent plus lot rent in one monthly bill (the park keeps lot rent and remits the home rent). Signers: the resident (lease), the park owner or manager (placement agreement), and the credit committee of an institutional fund (capital).
- **Problem:** Lenders reject about 65.6% of chattel applicants [S]. That is roughly 100,000 rejected applications a year [D: 52,000 funded / 34.4% approval x 65.6% denial; upper bound, one household can apply more than once]. The pads are there (REIT occupancy 94-98% [S]), but old homes in small parks are not replaced because the owner has no capital and the lenders do not lend.
- **Solution:** Buy single-section and double-section homes from plants running below capacity (shipments -7.3% YTD [S]), set them on vacant pads in partner communities, rent them under a true lease with an option to buy at a fixed formula, report on-time rent to credit bureaus, and refer the resident to Lot-Lender-type lenders after 24-36 months. The lease and the lot lease are the lock.
- **Why now:** Institutional capital has started to flow to the pool (Apollo $850M first close; Zippy and Forum); plants have empty capacity; the denial rate is documented in the FHFA proposal; REITs prove the model works (UMH has about 11,400 rental homes at 95% occupancy [S]). The ROAD Act's chassis rule change and HUD's pending rules may lower home cost over time [S, not final].
- **Wedge:** 100-200 homes in 10-20 partner communities of one regional owner in one state. First 10 customers: ten park owners with 10+ vacant or obsolete pads.
- **Path to $100M:** At $1,000 a month for the home [A], one home yields $12,000 a year; $100M needs about 8,300 homes = about $0.8B of assets at about $95k all-in (home plus set; Census prices are $88-91k single-section and $161-164k double-section retail excluding setup [S, from the pool file]; fleet pricing is my assumption). That is 8% of one year's rejected applicants, or about 2% a year over four years, or about 2% of annual shipments a year [D]. Capital: about $0.8B, with 70-75% debt [A].
- **Who does it today:** UMH (about 11,400 rental homes in its own parks), Sun and ELS in their own parks [M for Sun/ELS rental counts], Apollo-backed funds [S/M], local rent-to-own operators. No one I found places homes in third-party parks at scale.
- **Why they lose:** REITs place homes only in parks they own and do not buy small ones that lack scale; lenders do not lend to this customer; RTO retailers do not do homes.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Scale economies (fleet purchasing from underused plants) and switching costs (resident and park); weak on its own.
- **Earned secret [hypothesis]:** The constraint on a small park is the home, not the pad; its owner will give up part of the first year's rent for a filled pad and a new home.
- **First 90 days:** Counsel memo on rent-to-own and installment-sale law in the first two states (general rent-to-own statutes often exclude manufactured homes, New York has a 2019 manufactured-home-specific law, and a 2022 North Carolina complaint alleges a park operator's used-home installment contracts were disguised contracts for deed [S]); structure as a true lease with no equity forfeiture. Sign three park owners. Quote fleet pricing from two plants. Pre-wire one lender for the 24-month take-out.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 4.
- **Risks to kill it:** Rent-to-own and contract-for-deed law, eviction law and reputational risk; the rent that denied buyers can pay may not cover home cost plus losses; return on assets versus cost of capital; UMH or Sun could copy the third-party-park model.

## Ideas I killed on the numbers (do not revive without new evidence)

- **Second Chance Homes (chattel special servicer and remarketer).** Non-Clayton repossessions are probably 5,000-8,000 a year [A]; $100M needs 40-60% of them or nearly the whole non-captive sub-servicing pool. Kept as the in-place resale function inside Lot-Lender and Deposit Bridge.
- **Floor-plan lender for independent dealers.** 21st Mortgage floored about $1.6B in 2023 (22,000 units) [S]; the whole market's floor-plan interest pool is about $100M [D], so $100M is 100% share.
- **Chattel-to-mortgage conversion factory (detitling, foundation certification, refinance).** Only about 88,000 land-owning chattel borrowers in seven years = about 12,600 a year [S/D]; at a $4,000 fee that is about $50M. Some states even require payoff before retitling [S].
- **Right-of-first-refusal desk or bridge lender for resident buyouts.** ROC USA has financed $450M in 17 years [S]; the brokerage pool is far below $100M.
- **Title and lien e-registry; chattel data utility; "GSE day-one" conduit.** About 52,000 loans x $150-250 = $8-13M; Kazdin's model says GSE ownership could raise the all-in chattel rate to 10.6-10.9% and pilots are sized around $100M [S].
- **Used-home certification.** I found no count of used-home sales; at $650 per transaction it needs 154,000 transactions a year [D/A].

## What I would do next

Interview 10 park operators, 10 dealers and 10 credit-union lenders before building anything. If I could test only two ideas it would be Lot-Lender (does a 60-day rent history predict default better than FICO?) and Title I Reborn (why has the program been dormant, and what would the all-in borrower rate be?). If the answer to both is no, this pool has no $100M idea that a new company can win on better data alone, and the winners are whoever has the cheapest capital (Berkshire).
