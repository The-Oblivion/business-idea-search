# P032: US purchases of power and distribution transformers (round 2 ideas)

Pool: about $14B a year (MarketsandMarkets $13.8B 2025; CRS cites a $12.4B North American distribution-only market for 2024, so definitions vary by a lot). Generated 2026-10-14.

## What the search changed (read before the ideas)

Things I found that kill or reshape the obvious ideas:

- **Procurement and leasing brokers are already funded.** FluxCo raised a $26M seed (8VC, Congruent; July/Aug 2026) for transformer procurement, leasing, service and warranty with 100+ suppliers. Ayr Energy has raised about $25M, claims a $500M+ order book, has Indian contract factories build to its modular design, and quotes 40-72 week delivery (TechCrunch, Apr 2026). A generic "transformer marketplace" or "fabless OEM" is taken.
- **Co-ops already pool.** RESCO supplies 272 co-ops and public power districts with about $60M of stock and a 10-14 week lead time from ERMCO, a co-op-owned manufacturer (now building a 3-phase plant in Arizona for 2027). United Utility Supply, a co-op, holds about 10,000 units. Ohio (OMPA) and Florida (FMPA) municipal agencies run joint purchasing. So "group purchasing for co-ops" is only open where this pooling does not reach.
- **Spares pooling exists for transmission owners.** Grid Assurance (utility-owned, cost-based subscription, 5-year terms), EEI's STEP, NATF's RESTORE, APPA's voluntary exchange.
- **The sole US grain-oriented electrical steel (GOES) mill is becoming a competitor.** Cleveland-Cliffs is building a $150M distribution-transformer plant in Weirton, WV.
- **Tariffs were restructured in April 2026.** Section 232 duties now apply to full customs value; sources report a 15% transitional rate for some electrical grid equipment through 2027. I could not confirm whether transformers or cores sit in that annex. CRS reports a 50% duty on derivative products including electrical cores and laminations since Aug 15, 2025.
- **New shock, 6 weeks old: Executive Order 14420 (Aug 26, 2026; one source says 14421).** It bars acquisition, import, transfer or installation of foreign-produced bulk-power equipment (69 kV and up; transformers, breakers, inverters, batteries, relays, controls) tied to 24 countries, once DOE determines risk. DOE rules are due Dec 24, 2026. DOE may create pre-qualified vendor lists and licensing, and may order installed equipment isolated, monitored or removed. The 2020 predecessor order was suspended and never reinstated, so enforcement is not guaranteed.
- **DPA determinations (Apr 20, 2026)** call US transformer capacity "dangerously limited" but the money is small (about $323M of remaining DPA funds, per an unconfirmed advocate figure).
- **DOE's 2024 distribution-transformer efficiency rule** (compliance Apr 23, 2029) is under review; DOE issued an RFI in June 2026 and Cleveland-Cliffs wants it rolled back. Do not build on it either way.
- **Demand is partly phantom.** Wood Mackenzie (Aug 2026) puts only about 12% of 1,066 GW of requested US large load as firm and another 17% as likely. Lead times: generator step-up units over 160 weeks; distribution units over 2 years (APPA, DOE); public power has deferred or cancelled 1 in 5 projects.

Sizing anchors used below (sourced unless marked assumption): 1.4-2.4M distribution units added or replaced a year (NREL via CRS); 60-80M in service; DOE counts 34 OEMs; over 90% of utilities buy direct from manufacturers; rural utilities and co-ops buy through distributors, repair shops and contractors (CRS); AMI is about 72% of US meters (EIA, 2022); 5-20% of meter-to-transformer records are wrong (Siemens estimate).

## 1. [insider] Swap, Don't Buy: AMI-driven redeployment of the installed transformer base

**One-liner.** Use smart-meter data to find oversized, lightly loaded transformers and move them to sites where load has outgrown the unit, so a utility buys fewer new ones during a 2-year-plus wait; paid a share of the purchases avoided.

**Persona.** Utility distribution asset manager / planning engineer, 15+ years.

**Customer.** Co-ops and municipal utilities with AMI first (no return on equity to protect), then investor-owned utilities under affordability pressure.

**Payer and signer.** Capital budget line for distribution transformers and system improvements. Co-op/muni: general manager plus engineering manager. IOU: VP Distribution Engineering or Director of Distribution Planning; CFO for the success fee.

**Problem.** Public power cannot get units: 80% hold less stock than in 2018, 30% report high risk of running out within a month, and 1 in 5 projects have been deferred or cancelled (APPA 2024 brief). Meanwhile planners size generously and rarely revisit, so many units sit lightly loaded while EVs, heat pumps and storage overload others. (DOE's efficiency test points assume about 50% load for liquid-filled units, which I recall as the reference for typical loading; verify against 10 CFR 431.) Engineers will not trust a swap list because 5-20% of meter-to-transformer records are wrong.

**Solution.**
1. Repair connectivity from AMI voltage and outage correlation plus GIS and a field check.
2. Compute each transformer's load shape, hot-spot aging and 5-year growth.
3. Generate a swap plan that pairs surplus oversized units with overloaded or failing sites.
4. Run the redeploy yard: test (ratio, DGA, PCB), tag, truck, install.
5. Feed what is left over into the pooled purchase forecast (idea 3).

Pricing: data subscription plus 25-35% of the avoided new-unit cost and avoided failure cost.

**Why now.** Lead times over 2 years; PPI for distribution and large power transformers up about 40% real, 2020-24 (BLS via CRS); AMI at 72% of meters; load growth from EV, heat pumps and data centers; regulators asking utilities to exhaust non-purchase options.

**Wedge / first 10.** Single-phase pole-mount and small pad-mount units at 10 AMI-equipped co-ops and municipals, introduced through state associations and joint action agencies. Fee only on swaps that happen.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).**
- Assume 70% of the 1.5M units bought a year are at AMI utilities (about 1.05M). Redeployment displaces 10% (105k units) at an assumed $5k average for the swappable class: about $525M a year avoided.
- Success fee at 30% is about $158M if every AMI utility took part. Add subscriptions: about 150 IOUs at $300k plus about 1,200 AMI co-ops/munis at $40k is about $90M.
- $100M therefore needs roughly 35-50% of AMI-enabled utilities. **That is heroic.** It is only plausible if the corrected transformer-to-customer ledger becomes the system of record that EV hosting, DER interconnection, outage and procurement forecasting all use.

**Who does it today.** Itron (transformer load management), Landis+Gyr, Siemens EnergyIP equipment load management and connectivity analytics, Oracle Utilities (DataRaker; PECO's use is documented in T&D World), consultants (Quanta Technology, Burns & McDonnell), and utilities' own planners (free). None found taking performance risk or running physical redeployment (searched; "none found", not "none exist").

**Why they lose.** AMI vendors sell per-meter software licenses and avoid truck-roll liability. IOUs earn a return on rate base, so avoiding capex lowers earnings (structural), which is why the first customers are co-ops, munis and performance-based-regulation states.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Counter-positioning against rate-base utilities and AMI vendors; process power in the redeploy yard; a cumulative failure and aging dataset.

**Earned secret.** The shortage is partly a records problem. The "half the fleet is over 33 years old" number is nameplate age, but real loading is low and the cheapest transformer is already on a pole. Nobody swaps because they do not trust which customers hang on which unit.

**First 90 days.** Sign 3 AMI co-op/muni pilots (free analysis). Deliver a corrected loading map and swap list for one district. Execute 50-100 swaps. Measure units avoided and hours saved. Fix the success-fee schedule.

**Outside-the-box score.** 6

## 2. [insider] Cores for Coils: a closed-loop second-life factory for retired distribution transformers

**One-liner.** Buy utilities' retired and failed transformers on scrap-plus terms, test and strip them, put new coils on good grain-oriented cores, and sell warrantied rebuilt units in 6-10 weeks at about 60-70% of new price.

**Persona.** Transformer repair-shop owner, 15+ years.

**Customer.** Co-ops, munis, industrial plants, solar/storage and data-center developers, and utilities' own stock.

**Payer and signer.** Utility materials/stock budget (capex or inventory): materials manager or VP Supply Chain at an IOU; GM or operations manager at a co-op/muni; procurement or facilities director in industry.

**Problem.** Lead times over 2 years. About 95% of cores are GOES and about 95% of domestic core steel comes from one mill (CRS), yet a retired unit's core is sold as scrap. Failures are mostly insulation and coil damage; many cores and tanks survive. About half the installed base is over 33 years old, so retirements are plentiful.

**Solution.**
1. Multi-year retired-unit supply contracts with utilities (scrap price plus a share of resale).
2. PCB screening and core-loss testing.
3. A semi-automated coil-winding line that re-coils good cores to the buyer's spec.
4. 1-3 year warranty backed by an insurer.
5. Later, a large-power-transformer repair and rewind line (the only domestic non-import capacity that scales in about a year).

**Why now.** GOES single-sourced; 50% tariffs on steel and on cores and laminations (CRS); the EO covers 69 kV and up but excludes local distribution, and the lane does not depend on virgin GOES. Legal premise to verify: NRECA's comments say DOE counsel stated that existing and recycled transformers are not covered products, so rebuilt units sit outside the efficiency rule (my search could not retrieve DOE's "Rewound and Rebuilt Equipment" section, and the June 2026 RFI could change it).

**Wedge / first 10.** One regional plant (about 20k units a year) beside a PCB-licensed processor. Inputs from 5 co-ops' retired yards. Products: 25-100 kVA single-phase and 75-500 kVA pad-mount. First 10 customers: co-ops/munis in the same state association plus solar developers.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** Retirements are perhaps 0.8-1.2M units a year (inferred from NREL's 1.4-2.4M added or replaced). Rebuilt units at roughly $2-4k (60-70% of an assumed $3-6k new price for the class) times 40-60k units a year is about $110-240M. That is 3-5% of annual unit demand and 4-6% of the retirement stream. Not heroic. Large-power-transformer rewinds are a second line: 30 jobs at $1-3M is $30-90M.

**Who does it today.** Sunbelt Solomon (17 locations; remanufactured equipment and rentals up to 50 MVA), OEM service arms (Hitachi Energy, Siemens Energy, GE Vernova/Prolec), utility in-house rebuild shops, regional rewind shops, used-equipment dealers, scrap dealers (today's buyers of retired units). A Datacentres.com piece claims used units trade at 78-82% of new (weak source). Sunbelt Solomon is the one to beat; it is rental and big-unit focused.

**Why they lose.** OEMs sell new units and face retooling for 2029. Distributors have OEM exclusives. Small shops lack capital for automation, insured warranties and utility scrap contracts. Utility shops are shrinking through retirement.

**Moat.** Cornered resource (exclusive scrap-stream contracts that secure the scarcest input, GOES cores) plus process scale.

**Earned secret.** In a core-limited shortage the scrap yard is America's largest hidden supply of GOES. The failure is in the coil, not the iron, and winding labor (not iron) is the constraint you can automate.

**First 90 days.** Legal memo on DOE, state and TSCA/PCB rules for rebuilt units and on utility spec acceptance. LOIs for scrap streams from 3-5 utilities. Test 200 pulled units to measure core reuse yield. Pre-sell 500 units to 3 co-ops. Line up the warranty insurer.

**Outside-the-box score.** 5

## 3. [pattern] Gavi for Transformers: a take-or-pay buyers' consortium that underwrites standard-spec capacity

**One-liner.** Pool the demand of non-investor-owned utilities and mid-size developers into one binding 3-year take-or-pay on about a dozen standard designs, credit-enhanced by a lender, so manufacturers can finance US capacity and small buyers finally get production slots.

**Pattern transferred.** Vaccine advance market commitments plus the hospital GPO / co-op supplier model (digest: wrapper intermediary on pass-through flows; infrastructure-capital backstop for capital-heavy industry).

**Customer.** Co-ops, munis and small IOUs outside RESCO-type programs; plus solar/storage developers needing medium-voltage pad-mounts.

**Payer and signer.** Member utility capex/materials budgets; signers are the GM/CEO and board (co-op boards, city councils), usually through a G&T or joint action agency.

**Problem.** NRECA says long-term agreements "don't solve everything, particularly for smaller utilities that don't have the scale"; hyperscalers sign direct supply deals and fund capacity; APPA reports 1 in 5 projects deferred. Pooling works where it exists (RESCO, ERMCO, United Utility Supply) but only regionally.

**Solution.**
1. A standard spec book of about 12 SKUs.
2. Binding rolling 36-month member forecasts, aggregated.
3. Take-or-pay frameworks with 2-4 manufacturers, including new capacity (Cleveland-Cliffs Weirton, ERMCO Arizona 2027, Hitachi Virginia 2028).
4. Credit enhancement from CFC or CoBank, or a DPA Title III / DOE commitment.
5. Consigned stock at distributors; financing of deposits.

Revenue: principal margin (6-9%) or manufacturer-paid fee, plus financing spread.

**Why not yet.** About 3,000 fragmented, regulated buyers under varying bidding laws; no credit-enhancing counterparty; distributors cannot commit OEM capacity; OEMs sell direct to large buyers and would have channel conflict with distributors if they dealt directly with small ones.

**Why now.** Shortage; DPA determinations (Apr 20, 2026) authorise purchase commitments and financial support; new plants need committed volume; 2029 standard review.

**Wedge / first 10.** One G&T or joint action agency with 30+ members, 6 SKUs, 100% pre-commit.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** Reachable spend: assume non-IOUs buy 25-30% of utility units, utilities are about 65% of the $12.4B distribution market, so about $2-2.6B including small power transformers. Gross revenue of $100M is about 4% of that; 25% share is about $650M of sales. **The revenue test passes easily but the profit pool is thin** (6-9% gross margin is $6-9M at $100M of sales, about $50M at 25% share). It only becomes a real business if it also earns the financing spread, a manufacturer fee for demand certainty, and cross-sells other T&D materials.

**Who does it today.** RESCO (272 co-ops, ERMCO partnership), ERMCO and Arkansas Electric Cooperatives' contract vehicle, United Utility Supply, Texas Electric Cooperatives' buying program, OMPA and FMPA joint programs, distributors (Wesco, Graybar, Border States, Irby, KBS, Alamo Transformer Supply), APPA's voluntary transformer exchange.

**Why they lose.** Regional silos; distributors are not capacity underwriters; joint action agencies are state-level; nobody provides credit support to turn forecasts into plant financing.

**Moat.** Switching costs from a binding forecast and a standard spec; scale economies; a cornered resource in allocation rights.

**Earned secret.** OEMs allocate on forecast certainty, not price. A binding 3-year forecast on a standard SKU, bundled across 200 utilities, is worth more than a 5% discount, and hospital GPOs never took capacity risk.

**First 90 days.** 5 anchor members; a pooled RFQ on 6 SKUs; 2 manufacturer term sheets with capacity reservations; credit support from CFC/CoBank or a surety; counsel on state bidding law, co-op purchasing exemptions and buyer-group antitrust.

**Outside-the-box score.** 5

## 4. [pattern] TSMC for Transformer Cores: a neutral, automated core-and-coil foundry

**One-liner.** Build a US plant that cuts and winds cores and builds core-coil assemblies for dozens of independent transformer makers, new entrants and rebuilders, buying GOES from every mill, because the only domestic mill is now also a competitor.

**Pattern transferred.** Neo-prime / Hadrian-style automated sub-tier factory, and the foundry model (digest: neo-primes and defense supply-base rebuild).

**Customer.** Independent distribution and power-transformer makers (DOE counts 34 OEMs), fabless entrants such as Ayr, and repair shops (including idea 2).

**Payer and signer.** Cost-of-goods line for purchased cores and assemblies; VP Operations or Chief Procurement Officer at the OEM; CEO at a new entrant.

**Problem.** About 95% of cores are GOES; about 95% of domestic core steel comes from one mill; half to three-quarters of the cores in domestically assembled transformers are imported, almost all from Mexico and Canada (CRS). Cores and laminations carry a 50% Section 232 duty since Aug 2025. Cleveland-Cliffs petitioned in 2020 claiming cores cut in Canada and Mexico evaded GOES tariffs and is now building its own transformer plant.

**Solution.** Step-lap cutting, wound-core machines, annealing and robotic stacking; coil-winding cells; standard core families that fabless OEMs and the idea 3 consortium design to; toll or foundry terms and capacity reservations; multi-mill GOES contracts (Korea, Japan, India, Cleveland-Cliffs).

**Why now.** Tariffs; steel-supplier conflict; new OEM plants coming (Hitachi, Siemens, ERMCO, Hyundai) that will want cores; fabless entrants dependent on Indian contract factories while the EO and tariffs push toward allied or domestic origin; 2029 retooling.

**Wedge / first 10.** Distribution cores for 10 independent OEMs and 5 rebuilders, with committed volumes from ideas 2 and 3. Core-cutting machines have long lead times, so commitments must precede the order.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** Assumptions: cores (steel plus processing) are 10-15% of transformer price, so about $1.2-1.9B of North American distribution cores and about $0.9B for power transformers. If 40% is non-captive, the independent pool is $0.8-1.1B. $100M is about 9-12% of independent core spend (4-5% of all). Equivalent to roughly 30-40 kt of GOES a year at an assumed $3-3.5k per tonne of finished core.

**Who does it today.** Captive core lines at OEMs (Prolec GE, Hitachi, ERMCO and others), Mexican and Canadian core makers, lamination houses (Cogent Power, Tempel Steel, Magnetic Metals; named from memory, not verified in searches), Cleveland-Cliffs (steel plus its new plant), Metglas (only US amorphous producer). No startup foundry found.

**Why they lose.** OEMs keep core capacity captive and proprietary. A GOES monopolist moving downstream cannot be a credible neutral supplier. Cross-border core lines carry circumvention and tariff risk.

**Moat.** Process power (automation), multi-mill supply contracts (cornered resource), and design-in switching costs.

**Earned secret.** Output is gated at the first station: the core. Machines take a year or more to arrive, GOES is rationed and a miscut core is scrap worth tens of thousands. The plant manager who has begged for steel knows nobody will build a neutral line while GOES is single-source.

**First 90 days.** 15 OEM and 5 rebuilder interviews for volume LOIs by core family; mill quotes (POSCO, JFE, Nippon, Cogent, Cliffs); confirm 232 treatment of cores and whether a foreign-trade zone helps; site near rail and cheap power; cutting-line vendor quotes; raise.

**Outside-the-box score.** 6

## 5. [dilemma] Time-to-Power Fleet: build-to-stock multi-ratio substation transformers for whoever signs the interconnection first

**One-liner.** Hold a financed fleet of pre-built, multi-ratio 30-100 MVA substation transformers (US or allied origin) and sell, lease or option them for 8-16 week delivery, because utilities cannot order speculatively and developers cannot carry the order.

**What incumbents structurally cannot do.**
- Utilities: commissions want a signed customer before an order is prudent.
- Data-center developers: will not commit until power is certain, yet lead times are 3-4 years.
- OEMs: cyclical-capex scars; build-to-order.

The workaround today is speculative private orders ("ordering transformers before we've signed the interconnection agreement", per a colo procurement director in a Datacentres.com piece) and heavy collateral: Dominion $1.5M per MW for customers failing credit tests; AEP Ohio collateral at 50% of minimum charges; Consumers Energy standby letters of credit only. Neoclouds are shut out. Wood Mackenzie says only about 12% of requested load is firm.

**Customer.** Data-center developers and neoclouds, storage and solar developers, utilities needing a unit at energy-service-agreement signing.

**Payer and signer.** Developer VP Power or CFO (pre-development and collateral budgets); utility chief engineer or VP Supply Chain (rate-base capex once a customer agreement is signed); IPP CTO.

**Solution.** A fleet of standardized multi-ratio units pre-approved on several utilities' specifications, supplied by OEMs, the idea 4 foundry and the idea 3 consortium. Lease at 12-15% of unit cost a year, lease-to-own, or sell to the utility at signing. Reservation fees and redeployment of units from dropped projects. Portfolio effect: single projects are junk options, a pool is near-certain to redeploy. Funded by infrastructure debt.

**Why now.** Shortage; collateral regimes; phantom load; EO pushing buyers to allied origin.

**Wedge / first 10.** Two voltage classes covering most data-center and storage interconnections (exact classes to be confirmed with utility engineers) and 3 standard MVA sizes; 10 mid-size developers.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** Revenue is easy; capital and risk are the hard part. 35 units sold a year at an assumed $3M is about $105M. Lease-only: about 280 units ($0.85B of assets, assumed $3M each) at 12% is $100M. Demand check: Wood Mackenzie projects US data-center capacity rising from 24 GW to 110 GW by 2030, about 14 GW a year; at an assumed 2 units per 100 MW that is about 280 units a year for data centers alone. Fleet turnover of 100-140 units a year would be about 15-25% of annual unit flow if lease-only: **heroic**; selling is far lighter.

**Who does it today.** FluxCo (procurement, leasing, service, warranty; $26M seed), Ayr Energy (modular build-to-order; Indian factories; 40-72 weeks claimed), Sunbelt Solomon (largest rental stock; 20 MVA mobile substation case study for a data center awaiting a utility feed), Grid Assurance (spares for transmission owners), OEM stock programs, hyperscalers' direct contracts.

**Why they lose.** Ayr and FluxCo sell to buyers with defined projects; neither carries stranded-project risk on a standard fleet. Sunbelt Solomon rents temporary units. Utilities cannot speculate. OEMs will not.

**Moat.** Cost of capital and scale, portfolio redeployment (a data asset on queue attrition), utility specification approvals as switching cost.

**Earned secret.** Queue attrition is the hidden asset. Most requested load never gets built, so a single order is a bad option, but about 80% of substation transformers are the same few designs with different nameplates, so a standard fleet redeploys.

**First 90 days.** Get 3 utilities' engineers to sign off on one multi-ratio design; secure slot options from 2 OEMs (allied origin); an infrastructure-credit term sheet; refundable reservation LOIs from 3 developers; counsel on hoarding optics and EO exposure.

**Outside-the-box score.** 6

## 6. [dilemma] Time-to-Restore Cover: parametric outage insurance plus spares access for owners nobody covers

**One-liner.** Sell independent power producers, data centers and industrial plants a contract that pays per day, and delivers a pre-qualified spare and crew, if a named main transformer is down more than N days, filling the gap between 12-24 month insurance indemnity periods and 3-4 year replacement.

**What incumbents structurally cannot do.**
- Insurers: cannot hold inventory or force a delivery date. FM identifies transformers as a primary business-interruption driver with replacement times exceeding common indemnity periods.
- OEMs: will not promise delivery dates. In the Waratah Super Battery transformer failure, the owner reportedly could not force a faster replacement.
- Utility-owned pools: Grid Assurance and STEP serve transmission-owning utilities on cost-based rates.

**Customer.** Non-utility owners of main step-up and substation transformers: solar, wind, storage and gas owners, data-center operators, industrial sites; their lenders and tax-equity partners (some now require developers to fund future replacement).

**Payer and signer.** Owner's CFO or VP Risk and Insurance; lenders' reserve covenants. Brokers (Marsh, Aon, WTW) as channel.

**Solution.** A fleet inventory by spec cluster (MVA, voltages, impedance, bushings) with condition scores; reserved access rights to spares held by several pools (rental fleets, OEM stock, idea 5); pre-negotiated transport and install crews; a parametric payout triggered by SCADA-verified outage after day N; reinsurance capacity; MGA fee.

**Why now.** Lead times; documented BI gap; lender demands after the EO.

**Wedge / first 10.** GSUs at two IPP or yieldco portfolios plus 5 colocation operators.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** Assume about 12,000 non-utility main transformers averaging $2M, so $24B of replacement value (assumption; EIA plant counts not pulled). A 1.0-1.5% annual price is $240-360M. $100M is 28-40% of that pool (**heroic; this is the weakest idea on size**) unless data-center buildout (24 to 110 GW by 2030) roughly triples the insured base.

**Who does it today.** Grid Assurance, EEI STEP, NATF RESTORE, APPA exchange, FM Global, Zurich and HSB (equipment breakdown plus BI), Sunbelt Solomon, OEM service contracts, FluxCo.

**Why they lose.** Each owns one piece (cash payout, spare unit, crew) and none will sign a time-bound restoration obligation.

**Moat.** Pooling economics improve with scale; data on common-mode failures by vintage and maker.

**Earned secret.** A spare shared by 20 owners costs one-twentieth, but only within a spec cluster. Failures are not independent (a bushing batch or one maker), so pooling must be by spec and vintage, and insurers price physical damage but leave the multi-year lead-time tail uninsured.

**First 90 days.** Collect 3 IPP fleet inventories; build a first-pass actuarial model from public failure statistics; talk to 3 reinsurance MGAs and 2 spare-pool operators; get lender letters of interest.

**Outside-the-box score.** 7

## 7. [shock] Achilles for the Grid: the supplier-paid, accredited trust network under Executive Order 14420

**One-liner.** Run the accredited qualification network (ownership and sub-tier bill-of-materials tracing, firmware attestation, witness inspection and a warranty/spares wrap) that DOE's pre-qualified-vendor list and lenders' replacement-reserve demands will need, paid by suppliers to be listed and by buyers per purchase.

**Pattern.** UK utilities' UVDB (run by Achilles; suppliers pay an annual subscription, buyers share the verification, audits for high-risk categories) and the CMMC third-party-assessor model, applied to hardware provenance.

**Customer.** Equipment suppliers (transformer, breaker, inverter and battery makers), asset owners and developers, lenders and tax-equity investors.

**Payer and signer.** Suppliers: head of US sales or compliance. Buyers: Chief Procurement Officer, CISO or VP Supply Chain at utilities, IPPs and data-center operators; general counsel. Lenders via covenants.

**Why now.** EO 14420: DOE rules due Dec 24, 2026; possible pre-qualified lists and licensing; possible orders to isolate, monitor or remove installed equipment; lenders already require replacement funding. Data centers had used Chinese units to shorten lead times (Wood Mackenzie). About three-quarters of one utility's bids now come from foreign suppliers. Each utility audits new foreign factories alone.

**Filter check (compliance-deadline whiplash).**
- Named enforcer: DOE, but rules unwritten.
- Revocation risk: the 2020 predecessor was suspended and never reinstated.
- Buyer loses: the ability to buy or install, so the penalty is real if enforced.
- Decision point: **Dec 24, 2026.** Treat it as a 10-week option.

**Solution.** A sub-tier risk taxonomy (bushings, tap-changer controllers, monitoring modules, relays, firmware); supplier subscriptions; per-purchase attestation; witness testing; continuous monitoring; surety partner for replacement cost; seek DOE recognition as assessor or accreditor.

**Wedge / first 10.** An "exposure audit" of installed foreign-origin gear for data-center and IPP owners, leading into purchase attestations.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** Assumptions: 15,000 covered purchases a year (transformers, breakers, relays, inverters, batteries) times $20k is $300M; plus about 1,500 suppliers times $15k is $22M. If DOE orders inspections of installed units, a weak source says over 8,000 Chinese high-power transformers were imported from 2022 to Oct 2025; at $50-150k each that is $0.4-1.2B once. $100M is 25-30% of the recurring pool or 10-25% of the installed-base pool: **heroic and enforcement-dependent**.

**Who does it today.** Fortress Information Security (A2V network, over 40,000 vendors and 300,000 assets managed; AEP joint venture; Goldman Sachs stake), Interos, Exiger, Everstream, Resilinc, OT security vendors (Dragos, Claroty, Nozomi), test and inspection majors (UL Solutions, DNV, TUV, Intertek, Bureau Veritas), customs brokers, DOE itself (free list).

**Why they lose.** Questionnaire platforms do not touch factories or hardware; inspection majors are slow, global and certify the same factories; the government lacks staff.

**Moat.** Regulatory recognition (cornered resource), two-sided network effect (list once, share results), data.

**Earned secret.** Provenance is decided at the sub-tier. The unit is Korean but the bushings, tap-changer controller or monitoring module may not be. Someone who has walked factory floors and read BOMs knows which 12 components carry the risk.

**First 90 days.** Read the EO and the DOE request for information; build the component taxonomy; sign 3 owners for exposure audits; recruit ex-DOE, NERC or utility supply-chain people; partner with an accredited inspector; file comments before Dec 24.

**Outside-the-box score.** 5

## 8. [shock] Virtual Transformers: buy capacity from the installed fleet instead of the factory

**One-liner.** Contract to deliver extra megavolt-amperes at existing substations in months (cooling upgrades, bushing, tap-changer and current-transformer swaps, dynamic ratings, load transfers), priced per MVA unlocked at a fraction of new-unit cost, for utilities and data centers that cannot get a new transformer for 3-4 years.

**Customer.** Transmission and substation planning at utilities; large-load customers funding upgrades (customer-funded, utility-owned); IPPs.

**Payer and signer.** Utility T&D capital budget (capex deferral): VP Transmission Planning or Chief Engineer. Data-center developer VP Power (preconstruction / energization budget).

**Why now.** Large substation units are past 3 years, generator step-up past 160 weeks (Q1 2026), breakers 125 weeks; Wood Mackenzie projects data centers could reach 40% of electrical equipment demand; data centers pay for speed.

**Solution.** Thermal modeling to IEEE C57.91 plus DGA and age; hardware kits (fans, pumps, radiators, ester fluid, limiting bushings, tap changers, CTs); sensors and dynamic-rating software; switching studies; a $/MVA-year price with a performance guarantee backed by an insurer.

**Wedge / first 10.** Substations with N-1 headroom at 5 transmission owners; data-center campuses on existing substations.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up).** Assumption: the US buys about $5B of power transformers a year at about $50k per MVA, i.e. about 100 GVA. Unlocking 8-10% is 8-10 GVA at an assumed $10-12k per MVA: $80-120M. Needs roughly 10% of annual capacity additions to come from uprating: **heroic-ish**.

**Who does it today.** OEM service arms (Hitachi Energy, Siemens Energy, GE Vernova/Prolec) selling uprating and life extension; Doble and Weidmann; monitoring vendors (Hitachi TXpert, Siemens Sensformer, Qualitrol, GE Kelman); consultants (Quanta Technology, Black & Veatch, Burns & McDonnell); utilities' own planners.

**Why they lose.** OEMs sell new units, so uprating cannibalizes; planners follow nameplate standards and rate-base incentives; consultants bill hours, not megawatts; nobody guarantees MVA.

**Moat.** Counter-positioning against OEM service arms and rate-base planners; a fleet-level condition and rating database.

**Earned secret.** Nameplate is a contract, not physics. Capacity is often limited by bushings, tap changers, CTs and planning rules, not by copper.

**First 90 days.** Pick 3 substations; run rating studies; propose a pilot with a performance guarantee; hire an ex-OEM thermal engineer; sound out one commission on cost treatment.

**Outside-the-box score.** 5
