Honest overall read: the pool is huge (the EO's own $120B plus about $28B a year of T&M), so a delivery firm needs well under 1% of it, but access, trust, authority-to-operate and past performance are the real gates, not market size. ## 1. Badge-to-Bill: pre-payment hours integrity (INSIDER: OIG or GAO auditor, 15+ years)

- **One-liner:** Reconcile every labor-hour invoice to independent evidence of work (badge, network login, ticket and repository activity) before the agency pays, and give the CO a decision-ready package instead of a post-hoc audit.
- **Customer:** Civilian agency CFO shops, senior procurement executives and OIGs with large T&M or cost-type services (civilians put 11% of obligations in T&M versus 1% at DoD, S). Second customer, the same ledger sold to contractors as False Claims Act defense and incentive-fee evidence. First 10: ten large T&M orders at agencies with a prior IG finding (IRS, GSA, HHS components, DHS components, VA, State, SSA; GAO flagged State and SSA T&M use as growing, S).
- **Payer and signer:** Deputy CFO or senior procurement executive, from the contract-administration and payment-integrity line; agencies are required to run recovery audits on every program above $1M a year where cost-effective, and contract payments are a payment type (31 U.S.C. 3352(i), S). Contractor side: VP of contracts or general counsel at primes (mandatory-disclosure exposure).
- **Problem:** With T&M the invoice is hours times a rate and the only control is a collateral-duty COR comparing timesheets. TIGTA's sample put 39% of invoiced IRS hours on one procurement into "unsupported" (S). FCA recoveries were a record $6.8B (S) but arrive years later and mostly in health care. DCAA cleared its backlog by sampling (GAO-25-107558, S), so it is not a full-coverage control. Cost-type consulting is $120B (S); every dollar of it is hours.
- **Solution:** Read-only connectors to the invoice stream (WAWF or IPP, or the contractor's feed), to access evidence (PIV badge logs, directory logins, ticketing and commits inside the agency boundary) and to timesheets. Flags hours billed on days with no presence evidence, labor-category mismatches against resumes, duplicate hours across contracts, and idle-time patterns. Output is an evidence package the CO or COR decides on inside the Prompt Payment return window (7 days for a defective invoice, U). The product never decides an overpayment.
- **Why now:** The EO forces a top-10 review and twice-yearly OMB reporting; GSA's $23.3B savings claim needs verification; FCA is at a record; existing T&M task orders run off over 2027-29 (A), so the exposed pool does not vanish on conversion.
- **Wedge:** A fixed-fee retrospective on 12 months of invoices for one large T&M order, offered to an OIG or CFO who already has a finding. Convert to an annual per-contract subscription.
- **Path to $100M (A):** Labor-based pool is about $28B a year of T&M plus about 70% labor share of $120B cost-type consulting, roughly $110B. Fee 0.4% of covered spend (below the 1-2% of contract value a COR function costs, A). Agency side $55M needs $13.8B covered, about 12-13% of the pool; contractor side 300 accounts x $150k = $45M. Heroic: it only works if OMB or a CFO council recommends it as a standard control.
- **Who does it today:** DCAA (sampled, after the fact), agency OIGs and TIGTA (episodic), CORs (collateral duty), DOJ and qui tam relators (years later), commercial recovery auditors that work commercial payables, Big 4 forensic teams. I found no productized continuous control for federal labor invoices.
- **Why they lose:** Each owns one piece (invoice in finance, badge log in security, the judgment in the program office) and none is paid to join them before payment.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Cornered resource (agency-granted data access plus a labeled dataset of billed versus evidenced hours by labor category and contract type) and switching costs once embedded in the payment workflow.
- **Earned secret (U):** The proof of an hour already exists inside the agency; the GSA and DIA case shows investigators use badge data. The control gap is organizational, not technical: nobody owns the join.
- **First 90 days:** Hire an ex-OIG auditor and an ex-COR; offer a no-cost retrospective to one agency through an OIG referral; build the badge and directory ingestion in the agency environment; draft CO decision memos; run one contractor-side pilot on a contract where the agency is not also a customer.
- **Legal premise to check:** (1) Under 31 U.S.C. 3352(i) a contractor may identify but not finally determine or settle an overpayment, so the CO decides (S). (2) Do not price as a contingency: the 2020 amendment removed the express paragraph on paying recovery contractors from 3562 (S), and 3562(a) only reimburses the agency's actual administration expenses. (3) Access logs sit in Privacy Act systems; the vendor operates as the agency's contractor (U). (4) Hard firewall so one contract never has both the agency and the contractor as customers.

## 2. The subcontract rail: Textura for federal services (INSIDER: owner of a small-business subcontractor, 15+ years)

- **One-liner:** The workspace where a prime and its subs exchange flow-downs, status, timesheets and accepted milestones, report to the government, and move money within days of the prime being paid.
- **Customer:** Subcontracts and contracts managers at mid-tier primes ($100M-$1B revenue, 20-100 subs) and the small-business subs they depend on. First 10: mid-tier primes that lost 8(a) subs to suspension and now have to re-verify every small-business credit.
- **Payer and signer:** The prime's VP of subcontracts or contracts, from subcontract administration (an allowable indirect cost, A); subs pay for early payment. Both sides pay per transaction.
- **Problem:** Primes reported a record $86.4B of small-business subcontract awards in FY2023 (S) and the plumbing is email and spreadsheets: flow-down clause packages that now differ by agency deviation, small-business status that can flip overnight (1,091 suspensions, S), subaward reporting (threshold now $40,000, S), timesheet collection, pay-when-paid terms, and no portable record of a sub's performance because CPARS rates primes. Services subs have no Miller Act or lien protection, the construction-industry safeguard Textura and Procore were built around.
- **Solution:** Sub onboarding with live SAM and SBA status feeds, flow-down generation, sub timesheet and milestone submission with evidence, prime acceptance, automatic payment to the sub from the prime's own account on a fixed schedule after the prime is paid, eSRS and FSRS report generation, limitations-on-subcontracting dashboards for small primes. Each accepted milestone becomes a prime-signed entry in the sub's portable performance record.
- **Why now:** Fixed-price milestone billing, the lower reporting burden threshold change, the 8(a) wave, RFO clause churn, and two shutdowns in 12 months that left unpaid subs (S).
- **Wedge:** Status monitoring plus eSRS and FSRS report generation for mid-tier primes (compliance only, no money moves), then the pay rail.
- **Path to $100M (A):** Platform fee 0.15% plus payment and early-pay take 0.30% = 0.45% of flow. $100M needs about $22B a year of flow, about 26% of the small-business sub flow, or roughly 10% of all subcontract flow if the total is near $220B (U, no governmentwide total found).
- **Who does it today:** Exostar (defense supplier credentialing and collaboration), Deltek and Unanet (GovCon ERPs with subcontract modules), Oracle Textura and Procore (construction only), Coupa and Jaggaer (corporate procurement), supplier-diversity software. For money: Blackstar Capital (non-bank financing that works with primes to fund subs, reported $1B facility), X-Caliber, and factors. No two-sided services workspace with an acceptance-linked pay rail found.
- **Why they lose:** ERPs are the prime's cost-accounting books, not a shared workspace; Exostar handles credentials, not acceptance and payment; factors fund after the invoice exists and do not control when the prime pays.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Network effects (a sub works with many primes and keeps one history) plus a cornered resource in the portable, prime-signed performance record.
- **Earned secret (U):** Subs do not lose because they cannot win work; they lose on cash timing and on having no past performance of their own. Late payment is mostly manual invoice matching and acceptance, not lack of cash at the prime.
- **First 90 days:** Sign 3 mid-tier primes on the free compliance tier; wire the SAM and SBA feeds; pilot with 30 subs; get a sponsor bank for ACH pay-outs from the prime's own account.
- **Legal premise to check:** Money movement stays on the prime's funds through a bank partner to avoid money-transmitter status (A); FAR 52.232-40 accelerated-payment duty to small subs (U); CUI in sub invoices for DoD work (needs FedRAMP Moderate, U).

## 3. The Acceptance Rail: per-event verification for outcome contracts (PATTERN: "liability-shifting mandate becomes a per-event verification rail", imported from age and identity checks)

- **One-liner:** A neutral measurement standard that checks each milestone or metric against objective evidence and gives the CO a signed evidence package, priced per verified event.
- **Customer:** COs, CORs and program managers on fixed-price, performance-based services above $10M (the EO's approval line); contractors with profit at risk. First 10: ten IT-operations contracts whose acceptance criteria are already numeric (ticket resolution time, backlog burn-down, uptime) at agencies restructuring their top-10 contracts.
- **Payer and signer:** Program office, from the contract's own surveillance and QASP line (COR labor is typically 1-2% of contract value, A); signer is the CO with a chief acquisition officer sponsor. Contractors pay for evidence that protects their performance-tied profit and CPARS rating.
- **Problem:** The EO pays for results and penalizes weak ones, which needs a metric both sides accept. CO and COR capacity is thin (GSA attrition 39%, S). CPARS is late and weak: a DoD IG sample found 83 of 238 assessments late by 73 days on average and 200 of 238 not prepared to the rules (older audit, S). IV&V is episodic and only for big IT. As AI-delivered work produces thousands of small outputs, nobody can review them by hand.
- **Solution:** A library of machine-readable metric definitions for about 30 common service outcomes, attached to the contract at award; connectors to systems of record (ServiceNow, Jira, GitHub, logs); a signed, time-stamped event ledger; CO dashboard and a CPARS-ready evidence summary. The CO accepts; the rail never does.
- **Why now:** EO of April 30, 2026; RFO batch 2 adds consumption-based fixed-price solutions that need metering; CR and workforce gaps; OneGov's move to consumption pricing normalizes metered billing.
- **Wedge:** Free for the CO for 90 days on ten IT-operations contracts, paid per event afterward.
- **Path to $100M (A):** Outcome-priced services under measurement reach $200-300B by 2028 (A). Fee 0.3% of verified contract value; with 20% of revenue from contractors, agency side needs $80M, i.e. $27B verified, about 9-13% of that spend. Heroic, but measurement standards tend to be winner-take-most once GAO and COFC decisions cite them.
- **Who does it today:** IV&V providers (Booz Allen, SAIC, Leidos, small IV&V shops, NASA's IV&V facility), CPARS and PPIRS (government), in-house QASPs, contract-writing vendors such as Unison PRISM (paper, not evidence), observability tools that do not speak FAR. I found no productized "acceptance evidence" vendor.
- **Why they lose:** IV&V firms sell hours and often also sell delivery, so they are conflicted; contract-writing tools manage documents, not data; government cannot hire to build it.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Network and standard (once COs and tribunals accept the format) plus counter-positioning against conflicted IV&V.
- **Earned secret (U):** Disputes about outcomes mostly come from nobody agreeing at award how the metric is measured, not from bad data. Freeze the definition in the contract and keep a neutral log and the argument mostly disappears. A former COR or GSA FAS official will say the same.
- **First 90 days:** Co-write five metric templates with two former COs; instrument three contracts; apply to OASIS+ or MAS; publish the metric spec openly to seed the standard; get a chief acquisition officer letter.
- **Legal premise to check:** Acceptance and certification are inherently governmental (FAR 7.5), so the rail supplies evidence only. Strict organizational conflict-of-interest firewall: it cannot deliver on any contract it measures. Data rights to performance data in the contract.

## 4. Verified-WIP lender: control the repayment channel for fixed-price work (PATTERN: Brazil's payroll-deduction loans, "repaid first from a channel you control") - weakest of the eight, flagged

- **One-liner:** Lend 60-75% against verified, not-yet-billed work in progress on fixed-price federal services, repaid by Treasury directly to the lender under the Assignment of Claims Act or by controlled-account sweep for subs.
- **Customer:** Mid-tier and small GovCons converting from monthly T&M invoices to milestone-billed fixed-price work. First 10: ten firms with milestone contracts at civilian agencies whose bank has told them unbilled work is outside the borrowing base (U).
- **Payer and signer:** Contractor CFO or owner, from financing cost, which fixed-price pricing can absorb (interest is unallowable on cost-type work, so this is a fixed-price product, A).
- **Problem:** Under T&M the invoice follows the hours every month. Under milestone billing, cost runs ahead of billing and acceptance timing is discretionary. Asset-based lenders typically advance against billed invoices only (U). Shutdowns (43 and 76 days, S) stop payment altogether. Factors and banks price all of this into a flat fee.
- **Solution:** A non-bank lender that sees progress and acceptance state (contractor ERP and ticket feeds, WAWF or IPP status) and advances against verified WIP, assigned under the Act for prime contracts and sweep-controlled for subs; shutdown repricing built in.
- **Why now:** EO and RFO; shutdown frequency; AI investment needs working capital; banks are cautious.
- **Wedge:** Verified-progress financing on task orders up to $5M at agencies with fast acceptance history; a $20M facility from a credit fund.
- **Path to $100M (A):** At about 12% yield, $100M needs about $830M average outstanding. Fixed-price milestone services reach $100B a year by 2028 (A); unbilled WIP of 1.5 months is $12.5B; mid-tier and small firms hold half, $6.2B; the lender needs about 13% of that and about $100M of equity under a warehouse line.
- **Who does it today:** Blackstar Capital ($1B facility reported; funds prime contractors and subs), X-Caliber GovCon Capital (contract-based advances without factoring), United Capital Funding, Porter Capital, Bay Street Lending, Altline Sobel, bank asset-based lending desks. Advance rates cited by one lender guide: 85-95% of approved invoices at 1-2.5% per 30 days (S). None found underwriting acceptance state.
- **Why they lose:** They underwrite the borrower or the submitted invoice; none sees milestone progress or the CO's acceptance history.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Scale economies in cost of capital plus a cornered data resource (loss history by agency and CO). Thin.
- **Earned secret (U):** The lender's real exposure is acceptance timing, not government credit, and acceptance timing is the most predictable variable from a CO's modification and payment history.
- **First 90 days:** Hire an ex-GovCon ABL underwriter; build the progress-evidence feed; sign a credit fund; close 10 facilities.
- **Legal premise to check:** The Act assigns only to a "bank or other financing institution" and needs notice to the CO and disbursing officer; a Fall 2026 ABA article says modern credit structures (participations, successor lenders) raise unsettled questions (S). State commercial-lending licenses (U).

## 5. The Evidence Desk: ex-auditors who cannot audit, for the 2028 DoD deadline (DILEMMA: auditor independence)

- **One-liner:** An all-ex-auditor firm that never takes an attest engagement and sells audit-ready evidence per Notice of Findings and Recommendations closed, for DoD components and civilian agencies chasing clean opinions.
- **Customer:** Component financial-management offices (Army, Navy, Air Force, DLA, DISA, DHA and others among the 29 stand-alone audits, S) and CFO Act civilian agencies. First 10: ten recurring NFR families at two components (property accountability and valuation, unliquidated obligations, system access controls).
- **Payer and signer:** Component deputy CFO, from the audit-remediation funding that already exists ($4.11B in FY2018-22, government plus contractor, S). Deloitte holds a Comptroller remediation contract of up to $78.3M with $12.2M obligated, work to Nov 2028 (S).
- **Problem:** Eight failed audits, 26 material weaknesses, and a clean opinion promised by 2028 (S). GAO found the rate of fully resolving NFRs fell from FY2019 to FY2022 (S) and DoD OIG found components did not manage FIAR contracts well (S). The people who know exactly what an auditor will accept are barred from remediating the entities they audit (auditor-independence rules on non-audit services, U), and the firms that remediate sell hours.
- **Solution:** For each NFR, reconstruct the auditor's test (population, sample, evidence request), use AI to tie transactions to supporting contracts, receiving reports and invoices, run mock testing with auditor logic, and close the corrective action plan. Priced per NFR accepted-as-closed, with a holdback until the auditor closes it.
- **Why now:** The December 2025 failure and the comptroller's public admission that DoD will not get there "without a significant acceleration" (S); the 2028 statutory date puts the money in FY2027-28.
- **Wedge:** Tie-out of one recurring finding for one component in 60 days, paid per item resolved.
- **Path to $100M (A):** Contractor-addressable remediation about $0.4B a year now, rising to about $0.8B on the 2028 ramp, plus $0.3-0.5B of civilian internal-control and audit-readiness work (U): about $1.1-1.3B. $100M is 8-12% of that. Heroic for a boutique; it only scales if the desk's evidence engine becomes the platform components share.
- **Who does it today:** Deloitte, Booz Allen, Accenture Federal, Guidehouse, Grant Thornton public sector, Kearney; KPMG, EY and other independent public accountants hired for component audits (barred from remediating the same entity, U); DoD's Advana data platform; component FIAR teams.
- **Why they lose:** Juniors who have never sat across from the auditor; T&M incentives that reward hours, not closed findings; independence rules that keep the best-informed firms out.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Cornered resource (alumni of audit firms and an NFR-by-NFR playbook library reusable across 29 entities) and process power.
- **Earned secret (U):** The same few dozen root causes recur across the 29 entities; a tested remediation at Army is a template for Navy. Auditors do not fail DoD for effort but because transactions cannot be tied to supporting documents.
- **First 90 days:** Recruit three former audit managers and a former DoD OIG auditor; take one NFR pilot; stand up a firewall so no affiliate is an independent public accountant for any component served.
- **Legal premise to check:** Independence under government auditing standards (U), organizational conflicts, clearances for some systems, no inherently governmental decisions.

## 6. The cost-guaranteed sub that primes buy to reprice their own contracts (DILEMMA: the T&M book)

- **One-liner:** Sell the primes a fixed unit price per outcome for the automatable 30-50% of their civilian task orders, with a guaranteed price glide path, so they can submit a credible fixed-price restructuring without gutting margin.
- **Customer:** Pricing leads and delivery VPs at primes and mid-tier GovCons whose largest civilian contracts are in the EO's top-10 restructuring queue (Booz Allen, Accenture Federal, Deloitte GPS, GDIT, Leidos, SAIC, CACI, Guidehouse, ICF and Peraton-class firms). First 10: one mid-tier prime with three contracts of $20-80M.
- **Payer and signer:** Prime's contracts director with the program manager and pricing lead, from the subcontract line inside the restructured fixed-price offer.
- **Problem:** A prime's revenue is hours times rate. The EO forces fixed price at an assumed 20-30% lower number with the same people (A). Leidos reports rising fixed-price mix and margin pressure; Booz Allen's civil revenue is falling 23% (S). Their internal AI efforts collide with utilization, bench and headcount targets that pay their delivery leaders (U), and nobody inside can sign a contractual cost-down.
- **Solution:** Per-outcome unit prices (per authorization package, per ticket class, per case file prepared, per report) under the prime's contract, with a scheduled annual price decline, service credits, and acceptance tests; operates inside the agency boundary under the prime's authorization. The prime keeps program management, the customer and past performance.
- **Why now:** EO, the July 29 restructuring deadline, GSA's outcome-based push, and civilian weakness lasting through CY27 (S).
- **Wedge:** One slice (security documentation or ticket triage) on one restructured task order, priced at about 60% of the prime's current labor cost with a performance guarantee.
- **Path to $100M (A):** $120B cost-type consulting (S) x 70% labor x 30% automatable = $25B of cost base; at 60% of that, $15B of addressable sub revenue. $100M is 0.7% of it, or 30 restructured contracts at $3.3M of sub revenue each. Share is easy; trust and cost-guarantee credibility are not.
- **Who does it today:** Primes' own AI programs (Booz Allen, Accenture Federal and Deloitte have internal platforms, U), Palantir (platform inside agencies), Ask Sage (BigBear.ai), C3 AI and other forward-deployed teams, Kaizen, staff-augmentation firms.
- **Why they lose:** Primes cannot contract a cost-down internally against their own headcount incentives; an external guaranteed price converts a hope into a number they can put in a proposal. Honest weakness: after 18 months a prime can copy the method.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Counter-positioning today; process power (a unit-cost database from every restructured order) and switching costs (inside the prime's authorization boundary) later.
- **Earned secret (U):** A prime's pricing lead is not afraid of the technical approach; they are afraid of the minus-25% line in the proposal and of not knowing if cost will follow. They pay a premium for certainty.
- **First 90 days:** Pitch three mid-tier primes with live restructuring; build the unit-price sheet for three work types; offer a first-year guaranteed price; get the sub consent path settled.
- **Legal premise to check:** Pass-through limits on cost-type contracts (FAR 52.215-23, U); consent-to-subcontract on cost-type work (U); small-business subcontracting plans; data rights.

## 7. The Outcome Catalog: unit-priced deliverables under the simplified threshold (SHOCK: fixed-price default, T&M requires agency-head approval)

- **One-liner:** A schedule vendor that sells a short menu of fixed-price, acceptance-tested deliverables (authorization packages, control-test cycles, information-collection packages, records and accessibility work) that federal offices now buy as hours, each sized to land under $350,000 and obligated with year-end money.
- **Customer:** Program offices and COs at civilian agencies. First 10: ten components at HHS, DHS, VA, Treasury and State, starting with security authorization packages and internal-control test cycles. Evidence-Act and records deliverables follow; FOIA surge is a SKU but crowded (DHS reportedly gave Deloitte an AI FOIA build in June 2026, U; FOIAXpress, Seekr, Veritone sell tools).
- **Payer and signer:** The ordering CO, with program-office O&M funds that used to fund T&M hours; orders up to $350,000 (S) need only a reasonable-price determination, and a published unit price is the CO's whole analysis (U). Orders at or below $15,000 (S) need less.
- **Problem:** Today these deliverables hide inside large task orders on a 12-18 month cycle, or in small sole-source orders. The EO makes T&M harder to justify. COs are leaving (GSA 39% attrition, S) and cannot write outcome-based requirements quickly; 18F, which used to help, is gone (S).
- **Solution:** About ten SKUs with a published unit price, an objective acceptance test and a fixed delivery date, delivered by AI-assisted teams inside the agency boundary and staffed with the largest glut of experienced ex-federal and ex-consultant talent in a decade (BAH 2,500 layoffs, S; ex-federal headcount U). Orders go through MAS or OASIS+ BPAs; the top-down route is a OneGov-style enterprise rate card with the GSA FAS commissioner.
- **Why now:** EO, RFO batch 2's consumption-based fixed price, OASIS+ open on-ramp with no cap, the higher simplified threshold, and an ordering workforce that cannot run long competitions.
- **Wedge:** One SKU (a moderate-impact system authorization package at about $90-140k, A) and ten pilot orders under $50k in the first two quarters; the big Q4 (July-September) obligation window of FY2027 is the first scale test (the FY2027 CR runs to Dec 11, S).
- **Path to $100M (A):** 400 orders x $250k = $100M by year four, which is 0.07% of the $148B a year of T&M plus cost-type consulting (S/A). The statutory-deliverable subset is smaller and unsized (U), so assume 1-3% of that subset. Capacity: about 100-150 delivery staff with AI leverage (A).
- **Who does it today:** Thousands of small MAS holders and the primes; AI-forward entrants are Kaizen ($21M), Ask Sage, Palantir forward-deployed teams, Pryzm ($12.2M, AI platform for contractors and agencies), C3 AI; compliance software (RegScale, Paramify, Telos Xacta, U). I found no one that unit-prices accepted outcomes for agencies.
- **Why they lose:** Primes cannot make money on a sub-$350k order and their rate cards are contract terms; small shops cannot afford AI or authorization inheritance; software vendors do not deliver accepted outcomes.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Process power (unit-cost database improving every order) and counter-positioning against T&M books; cornered resource in agency-accepted templates.
- **Earned secret (U) (GSA ordering official or agency budget officer):** COs buy what they can obligate in nine days, not what is best. A non-severable, fully funded, fixed-price deliverable with an objective test is the easiest thing to obligate in a September sprint, and a CO no longer has to defend T&M to the senior procurement executive.
- **First 90 days:** Subcontract under eight existing MAS holders as SKU partner while a MAS offer is evaluated (U, months); recruit two former COs and two former ISSOs; publish the unit price list; run five micro-purchase and under-$50k pilots; operate only in agency enclaves.
- **Legal premise to check:** Bona fide needs and the one-year severable-services rule for crossing fiscal years (U); inherently governmental line (the AO signs authorizations); no requirements-writing for any agency that is also a customer; size standards: set-asides vanish once receipts pass the standard, so $100M is a large-business plan.

## 8. Award Ops: outcome-priced grant operations for offices that lost their staff (SHOCK: grants-management staff collapse plus new review layers)

- **One-liner:** Per-unit pre-award packet review, progress-report review and closeout for grant-making offices, with the human grants officer still signing every award.
- **Customer:** Grant-making offices at NIH institutes, NSF, ED, HHS (ACF, HRSA, CDC), DOT, DOE, EPA and Interior. First 10: ten NIH institutes and centers with grants-management shortages (NHGRI fell from 11 specialists to 3, S).
- **Payer and signer:** Deputy director for management or the grants management officer, from program-support funds; contractors can prepare and monitor, but award decisions stay with a federal grants officer.
- **Problem:** NIH had dispersed $2.3B less than its five-year median by Q3 FY26 and renewals are running 90+ days late more often (S). NPS requires two political appointees to review every grant of $50,000 or more, leaving about 1,400 grants and $362M behind schedule (S). NSF is at about 1,300 staff and wants more (S). There are 1,386 grant programs and more than $1.2T a year (Cato, S). GAO counted more than 5,100 employees significantly involved in grants management at just four agencies in 2013 (S).
- **Solution:** Unit prices per application packet brought to "award-ready" (budget reasonableness, indirect-rate verification, terms and conditions, risk assessment, debarment and audit-status checks), per progress report reviewed, per closeout, per single-audit finding resolved; for politically reviewed agencies, the review package is also prepared by the desk.
- **Why now:** Staff losses, new review layers, a pending Uniform Guidance rewrite (blocked through Dec 11 in the Senate CR text, U), and the EO's pressure against hourly contractors.
- **Wedge:** Closeouts and progress-report review first (no award decision), then pre-award packets.
- **Path to $100M (A):** Federal-side grants administration labor about $3-6B a year (0.25-0.5% of $1.2T, A); contractor-addressable 20% = $0.6-1.2B; $100M is 8-17%. Heroic, flagged.
- **Who does it today:** GrantSolutions (HHS ACF shared service) and NIH eRA (systems), Salesforce and Oracle grants modules, Euna, SmartSimple and Fluxx (mostly state, local and foundation), integrators such as Guidehouse, ICF, Deloitte and Booz Allen (which hold which grants-operations contracts is unverified, U), and in-house staff. No outcome-priced AI grants-operations vendor found.
- **Why they lose:** Systems vendors sell software but staff are the bottleneck; integrators sell hours that the EO disfavors; hiring is frozen.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Process power and switching costs (agency-specific term libraries); weak-to-moderate.
- **Earned secret (U):** The bottleneck is not the award decision, it is about twenty mechanical checks before a grants officer can sign; automate them and three specialists do the work of eleven.
- **First 90 days:** Recruit two ex-grants-management officers; pilot closeouts for one NIH institute; build a library of 2 CFR 200 checks; keep a human signature on every action.
- **Legal premise to check:** Obligating funds and signing awards are inherently governmental; political-review requirements and shifting priorities may remove the work overnight; research-security checks.
