## Considered and killed

- Remote-supports substitution for overnight staff: the latest national count (FY2021) was 10 states, $22.4M, 3,039 people. A false dawn under the digest's gate.
- 80/20 compliance rail: rule in limbo.
- AI scheduler or voice agent to fill shifts: cliche, and supply-limited (the problem is aides, not phone calls).
- Independent assessor (NYIAP is run by Maximus): no contract value or volume found, reassessments postponed since Nov 2023. Cannot be sized.
- Payer-sponsored supply-chain finance as a stand-alone: at 7-9% yield it needs about $17B a year of volume (A), over a third of personal care. Kept only as a scale path inside idea 1.
- Offshore Medicaid back office: many state contracts restrict offshore handling of Medicaid data (U).
- Roll-up of agencies: ModivCare is the warning; what survives of it is idea 8 (partner, not buy).
- Caregiver neobank or payroll-deduction loans: cliche and under $50M by my arithmetic.

## 1. Clock-Out Capital (INSIDER: owner of a $12M Medicaid personal care agency, 17 years)

- **One-liner:** Buy each EVV-verified Medicaid visit the day it happens, pay the aide that night and the agency by morning, and price it on whether it will be clawed back, not on how old the invoice is.
- **Customer:** Medicaid personal care agencies with $3-20M of annual Medicaid billings that now live on factoring lines or personally guaranteed bank lines. First 10: agencies in NY, PA, IL, NJ and FL picked from the HHS provider file (T1019 billers paying $300k+ a month and growing), plus referrals from two home care billing firms and one state association.
- **Payer and signer:** The agency, from the interest and factoring-fee line it already pays (reported 18-36% effective annual rates, U) and the payroll-processing line. Signer is the owner/CEO or CFO.
- **Problem:** Aides are paid weekly. Medicaid or the MCO pays 30-60 days later, and an EVV mismatch or authorization gap restarts the clock. Lenders underwrite the agency's aging report. The real loss is different: a payer claws back or denies a visit months later because of an EVV, authorization, eligibility or exclusion-list problem.
- **Solution:** (1) Ingest visits from the EVV vendor (HHAX/Sandata, Netsmart/Tellus, Fiserv, state-run portals), plus authorizations and daily eligibility. (2) Run a five-point visit check: units left on the authorization, eligibility that day, EVV match, caregiver exclusion screen, valid rate and modifier. (3) Advance 85-90% against passing visits into a lockbox in the agency's name under a control agreement (the "double lockbox" structure). (4) Pay aides through a payroll rail the next morning, with the agency still the employer. (5) Release the reserve when the payer pays. Target price 12-16% annualized for clean visits. Funding: specialty credit funds first, asset-backed notes once loss curves exist; a payer-anchored version (the MCO confirms visits as approved payables, priced off its credit) is the scale path.
- **Why now:** CMS scrutiny of personal care since Feb 2026 and state moves to enforce EVV matches slow and shrink payments; the NY edit that blocks unmatched claims existed but was off. HHAX has put payroll on EVV data (CarePay, Aug 2026) but has no balance sheet. The public HHS provider file lets a new lender screen every biller. MCOs now pay most Medicaid claims, so more agencies wait on adjudication.
- **Wedge:** $1-2M advances to 10 agencies in one or two states, sold as "paid on clock-out" payroll bridging, with the loss data kept as the asset.
- **Path to $100M (A):** Gross revenue at 15% annualized needs about $667M average balance, which at a 50-day turn is about $4.9B of visits funded a year. That is about 10% of the $46.4B personal-care pool or 3.8% of all HCBS; in agencies, about 700 agencies at $7M financed each (the HHAX network alone is 30K+ agencies). Share needed: about 10% of personal care, which I call heroic. Net: after funding cost (about 9.5%), losses (1-2%) and operations, pre-tax margin is roughly 10-20% of gross revenue, so $100M of revenue is $10-20M of profit. Payroll-rail fees add little (50k aides x $8 x 12 = $4.8M).
- **Who does it today:** Alleon Healthcare Capital and other healthcare factors; eCapital (markets HCBS receivable lines); bank asset-based lines; small-business lenders advertising payroll bridges; HHAX (payroll plus earned-wage access, no agency financing found). No venture-funded home-care-specific lender found, but I only ran 3 searches on this (U).
- **Why they lose:** Factors price the agency (aging, concentration, personal guarantee) and cannot see the five checks at visit level; banks will not do $1M lines on payer-assigned paper; HHAX is paid by payers, has no balance sheet and cannot be seen to lend against its own customers' payers; earned-wage vendors advance the aide's pay out of the employer's cash, not against the invoice.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Cornered resource (visit-level loss data by payer, agency and exception code, plus lockbox relationships) and scale economies in funding cost. Switching costs once the payroll rail is in.
- **Earned secret (U):** In Medicaid personal care the credit risk is not the payer defaulting; it is recoupment and denial found later. A visit that passes five checks is far safer paper than a 30-day-old invoice from a clean-looking agency.
- **First 90 days:** Get EVV exports from 10 agencies; build the five-check engine; replay 12 months of their visits to measure how many would have been denied or recouped; line up a $5M warehouse from a specialty lender; sign a healthcare-finance law firm for the lockbox in two states; hire an ex-HHAX or MCO provider-ops lead.
- **Legal premise to check:** Anti-assignment rule 42 CFR 447.10 (lockbox must sit in the provider's name; no direct payment to the funder; state rules vary). True-sale versus loan treatment and commercial-financing disclosure laws (California, New York, others). IRC 3505 lender liability when funds are advanced for wages (U, from memory). New York criminal usury cap on loans under $2.5M (U).

## 2. The exception-free fiscal intermediary (INSIDER: fiscal-intermediary operations VP, 15 years)

- **One-liner:** Run statewide participant-directed payroll at 30% under the incumbent's price by making the EVV record the timesheet and automating the exceptions that eat the call center.
- **Customer:** State Medicaid and developmental-disability agencies that buy fiscal/employer-agent services. First targets: Kentucky's participant-directed FMS RFP (due Nov 6 2026), Ohio DODD's statewide FMS, and a waiver-only contract in a mid-size state.
- **Payer and signer:** The state, from the FMS administrative line (per participant per month; federal match applies). Signer: the state Medicaid director or developmental-disabilities director through a competitive procurement.
- **Problem:** New York showed states that one statewide FI can replace about 600, and the claimed savings were large (disputed). But the same case showed the risk states fear most: aides unpaid on day one. Fixed per-member fees reward the incumbent for staying labor-heavy. Only 4 of 130+ bidders in New York even qualified (reporting, U).
- **Solution:** An FI built on three rules. (1) Hours come from EVV-approved visits and flow to payroll automatically; the timesheet is not a separate document. (2) Onboarding (I-9, W-4, state new-hire, background and exclusion checks) is a tracked pipeline with a target of days, not weeks. (3) Every exception (missing approval, hours over the authorized budget, overtime, wrong funding source) is auto-resolved or routed with a deadline. Offer a payroll-certainty backstop: the FI fronts payroll from a credit line if the state's payment is late. Price at $75-115 per participant per month against reference points of $98-160 elsewhere (S).
- **Why now:** Budget pressure from the federal Medicaid cuts; the New York precedent; live RFPs in Kentucky and Ohio; EVV aggregators exist in 29 state programs; CMS and state attention to EVV makes "EVV equals timesheet" a feature a state will want.
- **Wedge:** Win one small FMS contract (a waiver-only or mid-size state) with a teaming partner or an acquired small FI that supplies the required past performance; displaced New York FIs hold staff, registrations and track records and are cheap to partner with (A).
- **Path to $100M (A):** At $1,270-1,920 per participant a year ($106-160 a month), $100M needs about 52,000-79,000 participants. That is roughly a fifth to a third of New York's 250,000, or two mid-size states. I did not find a national count of self-directing participants, so I cannot state a national share. The books gross much more pass-through (New York is about $9B for about 250k people, roughly $36k each (A)), but net fee per participant is what counts.
- **Who does it today:** PPL (New York sole FI), GT Independence, Acumen Fiscal Agent, Tempus Unlimited (Massachusetts); Palco and Consumer Direct Care Network also operate (U, from memory); NY payroll firms such as Viventium; HHAX runs EVV for FI/FMS programs (Michigan session, Feb 2026); Maximus was named in a New York draft.
- **Why they lose:** Fixed monthly fees mean any saving is the incumbent's margin, so they will not cut price. Their onboarding and exception handling are call-center based (U). In a rebid, a lower bid with a payroll-certainty backstop is a story a state director can defend.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Switching costs (multi-year state contract plus thousands of enrolled workers), scale economies (fixed software spread over more participants), process power in exception handling.
- **Earned secret (U):** The unit of work in an FI is the exception, not the paycheck; 30-40% of timesheets need a human touch in a legacy FI (my estimate from experience, not sourced). A state values "zero missed payroll" above a lower price, so the pricing wedge is the guarantee.
- **First 90 days:** Answer the Kentucky RFP with a partner that has past performance; sign a former state FMS program manager as bid lead; build the EVV-to-payroll pipeline against one aggregator; line up a payroll backstop line; approach two displaced New York FIs.
- **Legal premise to check:** IRS Form 2678 / Section 3504 agent status; state FEA or FMS certification; procurement scoring on prior performance; CMS requirements for FMS in 1915(c) waivers. Political risk: the New York single-vendor award is contested.

## 3. Proof-of-Visit: the verification rail for home care (PATTERN: liability-shifting mandate becomes a per-event verification rail)

- **One-liner:** Sell a signed assurance score and evidence pack for every home care visit (right person, right place, no impossible overlaps), the way identity vendors sell a check per login.
- **Customer:** In order: EVV vendors that want to white-label it; MCOs and state Medicaid agencies that want to pay only provable visits; agencies and their lenders that want a "verified" fast lane. First 10: three agencies and one EVV vendor on a historical replay, then one state program-integrity office.
- **Payer and signer:** Three budget lines. (a) States: EVV and program-integrity lines (federal systems match is available for certified modules, U). Signer: Medicaid program-integrity director. (b) MCOs: special investigations or payment-integrity budget; signer: SIU head. (c) Agencies: compliance spend. Pricing: $0.30-0.80 per visit, or a savings share with a state.
- **Problem:** EVV was built as a time clock, not proof. A caregiver can tap an app or call in without being there, and manual edits are invisible to the state. New York paid $14.5B in claims with no matching EVV record, and the blocking edit was off. The Philadelphia charges (Aug 2026) came with a federal prosecutor saying EVV "was not sufficient".
- **Solution:** Layer 1, at the visit: device attestation, geofence against the service address, and an optional consented face match for flagged visits. Layer 2, with no hardware: a graph across everything a caregiver is paid for (overlapping visits, impossible travel, reuse of payroll bank accounts, billing during hospital stays or incarceration, visits under 8 minutes). Layer 3, the rail: a signed score and evidence pack through one API, consumed by EVV vendors, claims engines (a "pay-when-proven" hard edit) and lenders. It is the underwriting feed for idea 1.
- **Why now:** The Feb 2026 CMS push, the Minnesota deferral, the Philadelphia case, Ohio's high-risk criteria (Oct 6 2026, U), the NY audit, and an OIG work-plan item on how states use EVV data. Pressure is now enforcement, not a deadline that can slip.
- **Why it has not happened here:** EVV vendors are paid per visit by states and collect to a state specification that excludes identity proof; a stricter check adds friction for their own users. Payment-integrity vendors work after payment, on claims, without payroll or identity data. No identity vendor has a home care product that I found.
- **Wedge:** Historical replay for a state or MCO: run 24 months of EVV and claims through the graph and show the flagged dollars, then pay on a savings share.
- **Path to $100M (A):** Per-visit route: HHAX alone confirms 485M visits a year (S); assume 600-800M nationally (A). $0.40 x 250M visits = $100M, which is 30-40% of national visits, heroic. Savings-share route: New York personal care is $15.67B (S); at the 12% improper rate from the OIG sample (A), that is about $1.9B a year; if the rail plus a hard edit avoids a quarter ($470M) and the vendor keeps 20%, that is about $94M from one state. So the realistic path is one NY-class state savings-share plus per-visit fees elsewhere. Share needed: about a third of national visits, or one New York-sized contract.
- **Who does it today:** EVV vendors (HHAX including Sandata, Netsmart/Tellus, Fiserv AuthentiCare, state-run portals); biometric EVV devices (state-allowed, but needs a device at each home); payment-integrity vendors (Gainwell, Conduent, Cotiviti, Performant, Optum, named from background knowledge, U); CMS and state fraud partnerships (free); identity vendors (Persona, ID.me, Clear) with no HCBS product found.
- **Why they lose:** EVV vendors cannot add friction to their users or make visits fail. Integrity vendors see claims but not who was physically where. Only a party with payroll and cross-employer data can build the cross-check.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Network economies (a caregiver identity graph across states, payers and employers), counter-positioning against EVV vendors, process power.
- **Earned secret (U):** Fraud sits in the last 10% of the workflow (manual edits and reason codes). The cheapest proof is not biometrics but cross-checking a caregiver's other obligations.
- **First 90 days:** Replay 24 months of visits for three agencies and one EVV vendor; show flagged dollars; brief two state integrity offices; get privacy counsel on face matching; add a non-biometric mode from day one.
- **Legal premise to check:** Biometric privacy statutes (Illinois BIPA, Texas, Washington); HIPAA treatment of visit data; whether a contingency or savings-share fee is allowable on federally matched integrity work (U); due-process rights for providers who are flagged.

## 4. Personal-care administrator for fee-for-service states (PATTERN: the NEMT-broker model)

- **One-liner:** Take what a state buys piece by piece (assessment, authorization, network, EVV, payroll, fraud) and sell one reconciled paid hour, the way NEMT brokers sell a managed trip.
- **Customer:** State Medicaid agencies that still run personal care fee-for-service and have a cost or fraud problem. First: one mid-size state with an RFI or waiver window.
- **Payer and signer:** The state, from the administrative services line (50% federal match; 75% for certified systems operations, U). Signer: state Medicaid director and CFO.
- **Problem:** The assessor sets hours, the FI pays what is billed, EVV records what is clocked, the payment system pays claims, and nobody owns the reconciliation. Personal-care T1019 spending rose from $9.6B to about $23.5B in six years (S, secondary).
- **Solution:** An administrative-services contract that owns assessment scheduling and utilization review, authorizations, network enrollment, EVV and claims matching, fraud triage and capacity reporting for a defined population, paid a per-member monthly fee plus shared savings with a floor. Take no delivery risk and employ no aides.
- **Why it has not happened:** The NEMT model had explicit statutory authority for brokerage (SSA 1902(a)(70), U) and a single simple unit (a trip). Personal care has free-choice-of-provider rules and clinical assessment, and ModivCare's bankruptcy taught states and investors that brokers who take risk and own delivery can fail. CareBridge went to the MCO side, where risk transfer already exists, and was bought for $2.7B.
- **Why now:** Cost trend, the NY audit, the CMS crackdown, and federal pressure on how states use EVV data.
- **Wedge:** A single-function contract (EVV-to-claims reconciliation plus utilization review for one program), then widen to the administrator role.
- **Path to $100M (A):** At a 3-5% administration fee, $100M needs $2.0-3.3B of managed spend, which is 4-7% of the $46.4B personal-care pool. State anchors: Massachusetts's PCA runs about $1.4B a year through one FI (A from the file); Missouri T1019 was $6.2B across 2018-24 (about $0.9B a year, secondary dashboard). So it needs two or three mid-size fee-for-service states. I could not source fee-for-service state spend in total. Share needed: about 5% of personal care, heroic, and cycles are 18-36 months.
- **Who does it today:** Nobody as a whole. Pieces: Maximus (New York independent assessor), Public Consulting Group and Acentra-type utilization-review vendors (U), HHAX/Sandata (EVV), PPL and peers (FI), Gainwell and Conduent (payment systems). Only 1 search on the integrated model, so low confidence.
- **Why they lose:** Each vendor is paid per transaction (an assessment, a visit, a paycheck, a claim), so none is paid for the total bill.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Switching costs (state systems integration) and process power; scale economies in shared analytics.
- **Earned secret (U):** States leak money at handoffs; the unit a state should buy is a reconciled paid hour.
- **First 90 days:** Hire an ex-state HCBS director; pick the state; build a reconciled-hour prototype from public provider-level data and sample EVV; form a bid team with an FI and an assessor.
- **Legal premise to check:** Whether an administrative-services contract suffices or a 1915(b)(4) waiver is needed; free choice of provider; state procurement and conflict-of-interest rules.

## 5. The neutral LTSS manager (DILEMMA: Elevance owns the leading HCBS manager)

- **One-liner:** The same at-risk HCBS management that CareBridge sells, owned by no health plan, for the Medicaid plans that will not hand their sickest members to a rival.
- **Customer:** Medicaid managed LTSS plans that compete with Elevance: Centene, UnitedHealthcare Community Plan, Molina, AmeriHealth Caritas, Humana, CVS/Aetna Better Health. First 10: two plans and a Blue plan with a 5,000-member shared-savings pilot in states where they run managed LTSS.
- **Payer and signer:** The plan, from medical-cost management or care-management vendor budgets, paid out of savings against its own HCBS and hospital trend. Signer: SVP/VP of LTSS or the Medicaid chief medical officer.
- **Problem:** The top few percent of HCBS members (long hours, frequent missed shifts) drive a large share of spend, and a missed aide shift turns into an ED visit. Plans cannot run EVV, payroll, claims and hospital data together, so they pay a vendor to. The category leader is now a competitor's subsidiary.
- **Solution:** An at-risk manager with a contractual data firewall: members' EVV, claims, hospital admissions and payroll signals in one operating system; same-day response to missed shifts; network steering to agencies with the best continuity; fraud triage (idea 3 is a component); 20% shared savings with a floor. It employs no aides.
- **Why now:** Elevance's $2.7B purchase (Dec 2024) removed the neutral option; Elevance says it will serve all customers and has the plan associations as partners, which is exactly why rivals should hesitate. H.R.1 Medicaid cuts put pressure on plan margins.
- **Wedge:** A 5,000-member pilot in one state with one plan, priced at savings only.
- **Path to $100M (A):** Comparable: CareBridge served over 115,000 patients in 2023 (S). At about $30k of managed spend per member (A, below the $35k in the two NY/MA files) and a 5% net fee, $100M needs about 67,000 at-risk members, about 58% of CareBridge's 2023 count. Share needed: roughly 4% of managed-LTSS HCBS members if there are about 1.5-2M (U).
- **Who does it today:** CareBridge (Carelon/Elevance), plans' own LTSS teams, HHAX's payer network tools (a software layer, no risk). Others (care-management firms) not verified.
- **Why they lose:** CareBridge sits inside a competing plan, and its data and savings go to a rival. HHAX takes no risk and is paid per seat. In-house teams lack EVV, claims and payroll integration.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Switching costs, process power, and a neutrality guarantee that is hard for an owned competitor to copy.
- **Earned secret (U):** Plans do not buy "HCBS management"; they buy a lower hospital and nursing-home trend on a small, expensive cohort.
- **First 90 days:** Recruit ex-CareBridge and ex-plan LTSS leaders; pitch the plans that did not own the category; offer a firewalled pilot at savings only; use public provider files to choose states.
- **Legal premise to check:** Delegated-entity and utilization-management rules; state licensing of risk-bearing vendors; counting shared savings as medical cost; data-use limits.
- **Hidden-incumbent warning:** If four of the five largest plans co-invested in CareBridge in 2022 and remain happy customers, neutrality has no buyer. Test with three plan calls before building.

## 6. Risk and Mobility MGA for home care agencies (DILEMMA: carriers, brokers and PEOs cannot use operating data)

- **One-liner:** Use visit, route and authorization data as the underwriting file for workers' comp, non-owned auto, liability and billing-error cover, and give agencies their loss-control savings back.
- **Customer:** Medicaid agencies with 50-500 aides. First 10: agencies on state-fund or assigned-risk workers' comp in NY, NJ, FL, TX and PA, introduced by two trade associations.
- **Payer and signer:** The agency, from the insurance premium line (often the second or third biggest cost after wages). Signer: owner or CFO.
- **Problem:** Carriers price home care by payroll class (NCCI 8835 or a state code) and see nothing of the exposure; injuries cluster in patient handling and in driving between clients (musculoskeletal 33% of claims in one state study, S). Brokers earn a commission on premium, so loss control that cuts premium cuts their pay. PEOs cannot carry Medicaid payment lag.
- **Solution:** An MGA on admitted carrier paper that ingests scheduling and EVV exposure (hours per day, consecutive days, two-person assists, travel legs, vehicle use from phone telematics) with client identity removed; funds lift equipment; manages claims for early return to work; adds recoupment and billing-error cover tied to idea 3's evidence pack; bundles payroll and benefits.
- **Why now:** Immigrant labor losses make injured-aide days costlier; claim scrutiny and recoupment risk are rising; scheduling systems now have the data.
- **Wedge:** Workers' comp plus billing-error cover for 10 agencies that agree to share de-identified scheduling data.
- **Path to $100M (A):** Assume 1.8M agency-employed aides (about 60% of the 2.9M home care workforce, U), $28k payroll each, about $50B payroll; workers' comp at about 5% is $2.5B; non-owned auto, liability and billing cover add about $1-1.5B, so a $3.5-4B premium pool. The MGA keeps 15-18% (commission plus fees), about $525-720M at 100%. $100M needs 14-19% of the pool. Share needed: heroic. I did not find a sourced premium rate for class 8835, so the pool is soft.
- **Who does it today:** PMC Insurance Group (home-health WC program, S), incumbent carriers and broker programs, state funds, PEOs. Insurtech MGAs for small business exist (Pie, U) but none in home care that I found.
- **Why they lose:** Carriers have class codes and no data; brokers are conflicted; PEOs cannot wait 45 days for payment.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Cornered resource (a loss-and-exposure data set carriers lack) and switching costs from the bundle.
- **Earned secret (U):** Premium follows payroll, but injuries follow visit mix and drive time; the two diverge enough to pick winners.
- **First 90 days:** Partner with one carrier; sign a state association; get de-identified data from 10 agencies; price a pilot on paper; file with a state department.
- **Legal premise to check:** MGA and producer licensing in each state; filed rating plans; use of client health data for underwriting (HIPAA); rating-bureau class rules.

## 7. Continuity Bench: pooled W-2 aides for the TPS and fill-rate gap (SHOCK: immigration enforcement plus unfilled hours)

- **One-liner:** A Medicaid-native pool of W-2 aides, work-authorization verified and guaranteed full-time hours, that agencies draw on when staff are lost or hours go unfilled.
- **Customer:** Medicaid agencies that lost workers to TPS and work-permit terminations, and MCOs or states measured on service-start time. First 10: five agencies in Florida, Massachusetts or New Jersey that reported TPS losses, plus one plan paying a fill bounty.
- **Payer and signer:** The agency's overtime, recruiting and agency-staffing lines; signer: administrator or COO. A plan can add a fill-rate incentive from network-adequacy budget; signer: VP provider network.
- **Problem:** Medicaid fill rate was 71% for home health aide services (S, 2024), and one LeadingAge member lost 40% of dietary aides and 15% of CNAs overnight when Haiti TPS ended. Agencies cannot pool staff because each is the employer of record. Aides split hours across employers and average fewer hours than they want (U).
- **Solution:** A pooled employer of record with E-Verify, onboarding to each agency's EVV and authorization, and 32+ guaranteed weekly hours stitched across two or three agencies' routes. Agencies pay wage plus burden plus a spread; the agency still bills Medicaid with the aide as subcontracted staff where the state allows.
- **Why now:** Supreme Court TPS ruling (Jun 25 2026), Haiti TPS end, about 53,000 TPS holders in health care in 2024 (S); the fill gap predates it.
- **Wedge:** Emergency backfill for TPS-hit agencies at wage plus $4.
- **Path to $100M (A):** About 1.5B delivered personal-care hours ($46.4B at about $30 an hour, assumed) at a 71% fill rate implies about 2.1B authorized and about 0.6B unfilled hours. A $3.5-4.5 per hour spread means $100M is 22-29M hours, 4-5% of unfilled hours, about 11,000-14,000 full-time aides. Gross billings at about $24 an hour would be about $600M. Share needed: about 4-5% of unfilled hours.
- **Who does it today:** ShiftMed/HomeCare.com (W-2, 350K+ worker network including personal care aides, sells to payers and personal care providers; the biggest risk to this idea); Clipboard Health and ShiftKey (contractor model, mostly acute care and nursing homes); agencies' own per diem pools.
- **Why they lose:** ShiftMed prices as staffing, with fixed-rate economics set for higher-wage roles, and shows no Medicaid EVV or authorization support on its page. This is a thin edge a staffing firm could close.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Mainly scale economies in route-stitching density; weak otherwise. Honest score: a staffing business.
- **Earned secret (U):** The constraint is fragmented hours, not headcount.
- **First 90 days:** Choose a state where staffing firms can supply aides to Medicaid agencies without an extra license; pilot with five agencies; test the plan fill bounty.
- **Legal premise to check:** State licensure of home care staffing (New York has a license moratorium); joint-employer rules; Medicaid rules on subcontracted aides and EVV enrollment; wage-hour treatment of pooled employment.

## 8. Partner-not-buy network for independent agencies (SHOCK: rate pressure, labor loss, ModivCare, New York)

- **One-liner:** Give independent agencies the back office, payer contract, insurance and capital of a chain without selling the company.
- **Customer:** Independent owner-operators with $8-15M of Medicaid revenue, many family or immigrant owned, who will not sell. First 10: owners in one state met through trade associations and the HHAX customer base.
- **Payer and signer:** The owner, from a management fee out of margin gained; signer: founder/CEO. MCOs pay through one network contract, with value-based upside shared.
- **Problem:** About 13,000 independent agencies (S, 2021 count) face rate cuts, the TPS loss, EVV enforcement and payer delays while chains buy only those willing to sell. ModivCare's bankruptcy killed the buy-everything story. A 2021 attempt (IHCA) at back-office sharing had a $1.26M pledge and no visible traction.
- **Solution:** A management company owning no agencies: shared billing, payroll, EVV compliance, HR and recruiting; group insurance (idea 6) and working capital (idea 1); one delegated-network contract with plans; shared analytics and audit readiness. Owners keep equity and brand. Fee: flat per-claim and per-aide charges for billing and payroll plus a percentage for management services.
- **Why now:** Many owners are near exit with few buyers; plans want network adequacy; the shocks all hit small agencies first.
- **Wedge:** Shared compliance and audit readiness plus capital for 10 agencies in one state.
- **Path to $100M (A):** A 5% total fee needs $2B of affiliated revenue, about 170-200 agencies at $10-12M, or about 4% of the $46.4B personal-care pool. The IHCA count is all independent agencies, mostly private-pay; Medicaid-heavy agencies at $10M+ may number 1,500-2,500 (U), so it needs 8-12% of those. Share needed: heroic.
- **Who does it today:** Chains that buy (Addus, BrightSpring, Aveanna, Help at Home, Elara, Maxim, named from the pool file and background, U); agency software (AxisCare, AlayaCare, WellSky, Axxess); IHCA (2021, status unknown); HHAX payer network tools.
- **Why they lose:** Chains need control, and owners refuse to sell. Software vendors sell seats and cannot hold payer risk. Plans cannot sign hundreds of small agencies one at a time.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Network economies on the payer side (one contract for many agencies) and scale economies in the back office.
- **Earned secret (U):** Independent owners will trade a fee for scale and capital but not for equity; whoever offers that first gets the cleanest agencies.
- **First 90 days:** Pilot with 10 agencies in one state; build shared billing and a compliance calendar; pre-wire a plan network contract.
- **Legal premise to check:** Medicaid billing-agent rule: 42 CFR 447.10(f) bars compensation for billing services that is a percentage of billings or depends on collection (U, from memory; confirm). So billing must be flat or cost-based, and a percentage may only apply to non-billing management services. Also state fee-splitting and change-of-ownership rules.
