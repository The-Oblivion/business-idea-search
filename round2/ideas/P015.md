# P015: US building-permit plan review and inspection: 8 ideas (round 2)

Written 2026-10-14. Pool facts come from pools_selected.json (BLS: ~147,600 inspector jobs in 2024, roughly $10-11B in inspector wages alone). About 45 searches and fetches were used to anchor sizes and check competitors. Anything marked "my estimate" or "assumption" has no source and must be verified before anyone relies on it.

Headline finding: the big money in this pool is the labor and fee pool (inspections and reviews), not the software pool. City AI plan-review contracts are tiny (Denver: about $4.6M over five years; Harris County: about $750k), so a $100M company has to own licensed inspection/review work, or the data and liability that sit around it.

Lens split: insider 1-2, pattern 3-4, dilemma 5-6, shock 7-8.

---

## 1. The Tele-Inspection Factory (insider)

**One-liner.** A licensed, insured private provider whose inspectors sign off from a desk on guided video that tradespeople capture on their own phones, priced per inspection, starting with Florida single-trade finals.

**Customer.** HVAC, water heater, roofing, electrical and solar-plus-storage contractors that pull 300+ permits a year in private-provider states. Later: building departments and outsourcers that need overflow inspection capacity.

**Payer and signer.** The contractor's owner or operations manager, paid from the permit-coordinator and inspection-wait labor line. In a city, the building official signs an on-call contract paid from the fee-funded enterprise fund.

**Problem.** A building inspector's day is mostly driving and waiting windows, so inspection stays labor-bound even after plan review is automated. Supply is shrinking: an older ICC survey found 30% of code officials planned to leave within five years. NYC DOB application-to-approval time rose from 8.3 days (2020) to 20.3 days (2025) per the NY State Comptroller (July 2026).

**Solution.** The contractor follows a trade-specific guided checklist (for a water heater: T&P discharge, venting, strapping, gas connection). The app pre-screens completeness. A licensed inspector reviews the clips (target 25-40 inspections a day) and issues the pass/fail card. Failures escalate to an in-person local partner. Price: $45-75 per inspection (my estimate).

**Why now.** Florida HB 803 (signed May 6, 2026; effective July 1, 2026): 10-business-day clock, 5 for single-trade, deemed approval on a miss, and locals may not charge plan-review or inspection fees when a qualifying private provider does the work (Becker & Poliakoff summary). The statute (s. 553.791(8), 2025 text) says private-provider inspections "may be performed in person or virtually". HB 683 (2025) lets private providers use automated plan review for single-trade work and added solar and storage. Utah SB 185 (2024) and Texas HB 14 (2023) allow third-party inspection when the city is late.

**Wedge.** Florida single-trade residential finals (water heater, HVAC changeout, reroof, panel swap, solar-plus-storage). First 10 customers are contractors pulling 500+ permits a year, with registrations in 5-8 big counties.

**Path to $100M.** $100M / $60 = about 1.7M inspections a year. There is no published national inspection count. My estimate is 60-100M permit inspections a year (derived from BLS inspector headcount and wages), so this is about 2% of the pool. Reachable contractor-paid base: AHRI 2025 shipped about 9.3M water heaters (4.25M gas + 5.03M electric), 3.64M heat pumps and 3.25M gas furnaces. Only a minority of replacements are permitted (NRDC: about 10% of residential HVAC replacements in California). So permitted trade finals are maybe 3-5M a year nationally (my estimate), and the private-provider and fallback states (FL, GA, TN, UT, plus TX and CA triggers) might hold 30% of that. That is 1-1.5M finals, so the idea needs roughly 10-15% of that base plus a few city overflow contracts.

**Who does it today.** Municipal remote-video pilots (NYC DOB; West Hartford, which found remote took 2-3x longer per inspection). Inspected.com ($2.2M Series A, contractor-side virtual inspections). Sitewire ($3M seed, lender draw inspections). Inspace ($6M, commercial real estate). Built Technologies (lender inspector network with optional remote review). PermitFlow (inspection module; $91M raised). Private providers such as Bureau Veritas/CAP Government and Milrose (435+ pros, Littlejohn-owned since June 2026) do in-person work.

**Why they lose.** Departments are civil-service, jurisdiction-bound and paid per head. Outsourcers bill hours or fee splits for in-person staff. Software firms (PermitFlow, Built) hold no license or E&O and cannot be the signing party. West Hartford's 2-3x slowdown came from live, interactive video; asynchronous guided capture is a different workflow, which is the bet.

**Moat.** Process Power (guided capture plus an evidence archive that trains the pre-screen, letting one inspector clear far more than the 10-15 in-person visits a day) and Cornered Resource (a roster of licensed pros with local registrations and E&O). Reached after roughly 100k inspections.

**Earned secret.** The inspector's signature, not their physical presence, is the scarce asset. Most single-trade finals are binary checks of 6-10 visible items. Windshield time and 4-hour windows are most of the day. This is a practitioner claim and the first pilot must test it.

**First 90 days.** Hire or contract a Florida building code administrator or PE (s. 553.791(1)(n)); bind E&O ($1M per occurrence/$2M aggregate for projects up to $5M, per (18)); register with 5 counties; sign 5 contractors; run 500 inspections side-by-side with local inspectors and publish agreement rates and building-official audit results.

**Outside-the-box score: 6.**

## 2. Inspection-as-a-Service for the Top Homebuilders (insider)

**One-liner.** One master contract that replaces the builder's patchwork of city inspection windows, third-party QA walks and lender looks with a single independent inspection crew per division, funded by the city inspection fees the builder no longer owes.

**Customer.** Division VPs of construction at the 15 largest production builders, plus mid-size builders (500-5,000 homes a year), in Florida, Georgia, Tennessee, Utah and (as fallback) Texas.

**Payer and signer.** Division president or regional VP of construction, signing a master agreement. Budget: permit and inspection fees in cost of sales plus the warranty/QA line.

**Problem.** The builder's schedule is set by inspection waits and failures, not by plan review, and each failure costs days. Builders pay the city and then pay again for QA and verification. They also carry their own defect risk: D.R. Horton has been "almost exclusively self-insured" for construction defects since June 2021, booked $240.1M of legal contingency expense in FY2025 (98% of legal reserves defect-related), and set aside about $2,348 per home in warranty costs (Lennar: about $3,602) per Hunterbrook's reading of SEC filings. Inspection data therefore has direct P&L value.

**Solution.** An embedded independent crew (private provider where statute allows) does code inspections same or next day. It captures structured trade-by-trade evidence that feeds the builder's QA and warranty system, and optionally lender draws. Price: about $900-1,500 per home, funded by the statutory fee reduction (s. 553.791(2)(b): the locality must cut the fee by its savings and may not charge inspection fees for private-provider work).

**Why now.** HB 683 and HB 803 in Florida; Georgia's 2019 private-provider framework; Tennessee PC 771; Utah SB 185. Also, 45L (the $2,500/$5,000 energy-efficient home credit) ended for homes acquired after June 30, 2026 per the IRS, so HERS rating volume (420,135 homes in 2025) is likely to fall and rater businesses are weak.

**Wedge.** A mid-size Florida builder for two communities, then a top-10 builder's Florida division, then state by state.

**Path to $100M.** $100M / about $1,250 per home = 80,000 homes a year. NAHB counted 909,280 single-family permits in 2025 (Texas 140,002). Top builders build roughly half of US single-family starts (background knowledge). 80,000 homes is about 9% of national single-family starts, or perhaps 20-25% of the starts in states with usable private-provider access (my estimate; state counts for FL/GA/TN/UT not sourced). That means 3-4 national builders across 5 states. This is heroic if only Florida is reachable.

**Who does it today.** Florida private providers (Universal Engineering Sciences is on Miami-Dade's roster; Bureau Veritas via CAP Government; Milrose), builders' in-house QA, HERS raters, 2-10 HBW/Frontdoor risk management (no field network found), and PermitFlow for permit paperwork (customers include Lennar and Toll Brothers).

**Why they lose.** Private providers are local, price project-by-project and offer no multi-state SLA or data feed. HERS raters are micro-businesses losing credit-driven demand. PermitFlow does paperwork, not inspections. Cities cannot commit to windows.

**Moat.** Switching Costs (embedded in the builder's QA and warranty data and scheduling) and Scale Economies in routing density.

**Earned secret.** The inspection fee is already in the budget; the real value is cycle time and defect data. Waiting on and failing inspections costs more than review time.

**First 90 days.** Pitch the builder's VP of construction with a two-community pilot; pre-register with the counties; run side-by-side with city inspections and report re-inspection rate, days saved and defects caught before drywall.

**Outside-the-box score: 4.** This is close to the pool's own wedge hint, aimed at concentrated buyers.

## 3. Technical Control plus Latent-Defect Warranty for Attached Housing, an NHBC for the U.S. (pattern: liability-shifting mandate becomes a per-unit verification rail)

**One-liner.** Independent staged inspections during construction that unlock an insured 10-year structural warranty, sold to condo and townhome developers who otherwise cannot price or insure defect liability.

**Customer.** Condo and townhome developers in states that tied liability relief to warranty plus third-party inspection (Colorado now; Washington and Oregon moving). Later: single-family builders who want to move self-insured defect exposure off balance sheet.

**Payer and signer.** Developer CFO or VP of development, from the construction-defect/wrap liability insurance and soft-cost lines. Colorado's law is designed to lower those premiums.

**Problem.** Condo construction is choked by defect litigation. The US lacks the UK structure because FHA dropped its 10-year warranty requirement effective March 14, 2019 (HUD-92544 completion warranty remains), and big builders self-insure and arbitrate. So nobody inspects systematically for an insurer's benefit.

**Solution.** A registered third-party "technical control" firm inspects at foundation, waterproofing/envelope, framing, MEP rough and final to a published standard, issues the certificate that satisfies the statute, and an admitted insurer or MGA writes the 1/2/6-year (later 10-year) cover priced off the inspection data. Revenue is the inspection fee plus a share of premium.

**Why now.** Colorado HB 25-1272 (signed May 12, 2025): opt-in warranty (1 year workmanship, 2 years plumbing/electrical, 6 years major structural) plus third-party inspection during construction leading to the certificate of occupancy. Washington HB 1403 passed (signing unconfirmed). Oregon HB 3746 cuts condo repose from 10 to 7 years from 2026. California AB 1903 reform died on Aug 31 per The Close (the Construction Defect Journal still showed it pending, so verify). Frontdoor paid $585M for 2-10 Home Buyers Warranty (closed Dec 2024).

**Wedge.** Front Range Colorado townhome and condo developers, first 5 projects, with a carrier or MGA partner.

**Path to $100M.** UK analog: NHBC registered 115,350 homes in 2025 and its revenue is about GBP 250M per Wikipedia (unreferenced), roughly GBP 2,170 per home. At about $2,800 per home, $100M = about 36,000 homes a year, around 4% of US single-family permits, or perhaps 12-15% of attached-for-sale units (my estimate of about 250k; unsourced). It needs 8 or more states to adopt, or builders to buy voluntarily. Heroic and legislation-dependent.

**Who does it today.** 2-10 HBW (Frontdoor; claims about 1 in 5 new US homes, no systematic inspection network found), construction-defect and wrap insurers, builders' self-insurance (D.R. Horton), local third-party inspectors, and Colorado's own program rollout. No US NHBC exists.

**Why they lose.** Warranty administrators earn premium minus claims and have no field force. Big builders self-insure. Cities cannot carry liability to buyers. Inspection firms have no balance sheet. Each fails at one leg.

**Moat.** Cornered Resource (a proprietary as-built defect and claims dataset that improves loss ratios, plus regulatory designation as program inspector) and Switching Costs.

**Earned secret.** Code compliance is not what kills condos; the liability tail is. The statute makes inspection the price of admission, and the first underwriter to see inspected-building loss data sets the price.

**First 90 days.** Read the Colorado program rules and qualify as an inspector; sign 2-3 Front Range developers; negotiate a quota-share with an admitted carrier or MGA; build the five-stage standard.

**Outside-the-box score: 7.**

## 4. The Platform Behind Independent Private Providers (pattern: pre-position permitted capacity, with an independent-broker-dealer model)

**One-liner.** A licensing, insurance, software and payments platform that lets individually licensed engineers, architects and building code administrators run private-provider practices without running a firm.

**Customer.** Supply side: solo and small-firm licensed professionals. Demand side: builders and contractors that choose private providers.

**Payer and signer.** The affiliated professional pays a 20-25% platform fee out of fee revenue. The builder or owner pays the professional.

**Problem.** The statute puts liability on the licensed individual (must hold a building code administrator, engineer or architect license, s. 553.791(1)(n)). Required coverage is $1M/$2M (projects up to $5M) or $2M/$4M above that, with a 5-year tail (s. 553.791(18), 2025 text). Solo pros cannot scale registrations, notices, scheduling, billing and insurance. Corporate firms cannot hire enough licensed signers.

**Solution.** A platform with a group E&O program, a jurisdiction registry and notice automation, dispatch, tele-inspection and automated single-trade plan-review tools, instant pay, and a brand builders and building officials trust. Pros keep about 75-80%.

**Why now.** HB 803 limits local re-review of private-provider-approved plans and cuts commercial fees up to 50%, so volume is arriving. The old market was too small for a platform.

**Wedge.** Recruit 50 Florida private providers using published city rosters (for example Miami's) with lower group E&O and overflow work from 5 anchor builders.

**Path to $100M.** $100M net at a 22% take = about $450M of gross professional billings. I could not find Florida's private-provider fee pool; my rough guess is $0.7-1.5B (assumption). That would mean roughly 30-60% of Florida, so multi-state (GA, TN, TX, CA, UT) is required. Heroic; the bottleneck is gross-to-net.

**Who does it today.** Employed-pro firms (Universal Engineering Sciences, Bureau Veritas/CAP, Milrose, regional engineers). No platform found.

**Why they lose.** Firms must employ and insure the signer; Bureau Veritas and Milrose sell mostly to municipalities; software firms avoid licensure.

**Moat.** Cornered Resource (E&O program plus local registrations), Scale Economies in insurance buying, and Network Economies between pros and builders.

**Earned secret.** Supply is individuals with personal signature liability. The scarce inputs are E&O capacity and registrations, not software. Independents earn more alone but cannot handle the admin.

**First 90 days.** Get a quota-share E&O program quoted for 50 pros; sign 5 builder anchors; onboard 20 pros in 3 counties.

**Outside-the-box score: 6.**

## 5. Fixed-Price Special Inspection and Closeout (dilemma: time-and-materials firms cannot follow)

**One-liner.** Replace time-and-materials special inspection and testing on commercial projects with a fixed price per project, remote review, digital evidence and a guaranteed closeout package the building official accepts.

**Customer.** General contractors and owners of nonresidential projects (tilt-up warehouses, data halls, schools, hospitals) in IBC jurisdictions.

**Payer and signer.** Owner or GC testing-and-inspection allowance. Signer: GC project executive or owner's rep. The building official approves the agency (IBC Chapter 17).

**Problem.** IBC Chapter 17 requires owner-engaged special inspection, and a Statement of Special Inspections is often a condition of permit approval. Firms bill hours and trips; final reports arrive late and hold the certificate of occupancy (insider claim, to verify).

**Solution.** Technicians capture structured evidence (rebar, anchors, welds, fireproofing, concrete) with photos and sensors. A PE reviews remotely. Reports auto-assemble in the building official's format, and a live dashboard shows closeout status.

**Why now.** Inspector shortage and construction boom pressure (Census data center spending hit $50.7B annualized in April 2026, up 28.1% year on year, per AGC).

**Wedge.** Tilt-up warehouse and data-center-shell GCs in Texas, the Southeast and Midwest; sign 10 GCs.

**Path to $100M.** Pool (my estimate): Census total construction $2.2T minus $882B private residential leaves about $1.3T nonresidential and public; special inspection and materials testing at 0.5-1% (assumption) is $6.5-13B. Terracon alone reports about $1.1B in revenue (including geotech and environmental). $100M is 0.8-1.5% of the pool.

**Who does it today.** Terracon, ECS, Intertek/PSI, Kleinfelder, Braun Intertec and regional labs. I found no VC-backed digital-native firm.

**Why they lose.** Productivity cuts their own billable hours. They are local-office organizations; fixed pricing means taking schedule risk; they do not own software.

**Moat.** Counter-Positioning plus Process Power, later Switching Costs from embedded closeout data.

**Earned secret.** Occupancy dates slip on paper (missing sign-offs and reports), not construction. GCs pay for hours but value the date.

**First 90 days.** Get approved as a special inspection agency in 5 target AHJs; run a fixed-fee pilot on 3 warehouse shells; measure closeout days.

**Outside-the-box score: 5.**

## 6. Permit-Clean: Verified Permit Status and Closeout at the Closing Table (dilemma: title insurers and cities cannot)

**One-liner.** A rail that tells title agents, lenders and insurers whether a property has open, expired or unpermitted work, and clears it out of the seller's proceeds.

**Customer.** Title and escrow agents, mortgage lenders, and listing agents in Florida first, then Texas, Arizona, California and New York.

**Payer and signer.** Seller at closing via escrow for the cure; the title agency pays for the report. Signer: agency operations head or lender QC head.

**Problem.** Title insurance does not insure open or expired permits, and a standard title search will not show them; a full municipal lien search does (Florida attorney sources). Lenders will not fund until resolved. Sellers learn 10-14 days before closing, and one New York case cost about $25,000. I found no national incidence statistic.

**Solution.** (a) A report and API on permit lifecycle and closure across jurisdictions; (b) a closeout service using private providers or engineers (Florida law offers engineer/architect affidavits for concealed work); (c) a title underwriter partnership.

**Why now.** Florida private-provider final inspections and the 5-day single-trade clock; title agents are under margin pressure.

**Wedge.** Tampa Bay and South Florida title agencies, pitched on closings saved.

**Path to $100M.** About 4M existing-home sales a year (background knowledge). My assumption: 6-10% have a permit issue in permit-active metros at $1,500-3,000 to clear, a $0.4-1.2B cure pool, plus report fees of $30-60 per order. $100M could be 30,000 closeouts at $2,000 plus 1M reports at $40. All incidence and price inputs are unsourced assumptions.

**Who does it today.** PermitVista (South Florida; model not verified), PropertyKey IMAPP, municipal lien search vendors, Shovels (permit data; about $6.5M raised; third-party ARR estimate about $3M), BuildFax (2015: 30M records, 1,600 jurisdictions), local expediters, and county desks such as Collier County's Building Permit Resolution Services.

**Why they lose.** Title insurers price recorded matters, use standard exceptions and are paid per policy. Cities earn nothing from clearing and are siloed. Aggregators sell issuance, not closure. Expediters are local.

**Moat.** Network Economies and data (closure outcomes per jurisdiction), Switching Costs in title workflows, and relationships in hundreds of building departments.

**Earned secret.** The problem surfaces late. Whoever sees permit status at listing controls the cure and the fee. Many open permits are paperwork gaps a final inspection can close.

**First 90 days.** Sign 10 Tampa/Miami title agents for a pilot report; pre-register a private provider to do finals; measure cases cured and days saved.

**Outside-the-box score: 6.**

## 7. The Shot-Clock Concession (shock: HB 803 and AB 253 put a hard-dollar penalty on the department)

**One-liner.** Take over plan review and inspection for large departments that now lose volume and fee revenue when they miss statutory clocks, paid a share of retained fees, with the clock guaranteed.

**Customer.** The top 250 or so city and county building departments in states with private-provider or deemed-approval triggers (FL, CA, TX, TN, UT, GA).

**Payer and signer.** City manager or building official, paid from the fee-funded enterprise fund on a revenue share (the Macon-Bibb proposal was a 60-40 split). Signer: city council or manager after an RFP.

**Problem.** In Florida, a missed 10-business-day clock means deemed approval, and when a private provider does the work the locality cannot charge plan-review or inspection fees (commercial fees cut 50%). Departments lose volume faster than they can hire, and civil-service hiring and procurement cannot deploy the money they have.

**Solution.** A concession with an embedded team, tele-inspection, AI first-pass checks (partnered) and queue management with an SLA. The vendor posts a clock guarantee, insured against cure costs if a missed clock causes a deemed approval. Pay: 60-70% of retained fee revenue plus a performance bonus.

**Why now.** HB 803 (effective July 1, 2026); California AB 253 (third-party review after 30 days); Austin selected SAFEbuilt in July 2026 for plan review and inspection, showing big cities are outsourcing.

**Wedge.** Ten mid-size Florida departments ($3-15M of fees) losing private-provider volume: a 90-day overflow contract with a clock guarantee that converts to a concession.

**Path to $100M.** At a 60% share, $100M of revenue needs about $167M of fee revenue under contract: 8 departments at $20M or 40 at $4M. The top-100 departments' fee revenue is unknown. SAFEbuilt (about 1,500 employees, 2,000+ customers) proves the scale exists. It needs a few percent of the roughly $10-11B inspector-wage pool, but many procurements.

**Who does it today.** SAFEbuilt (Northeast Inspection Consultants acquisition, Oct 2026), Bureau Veritas/CAP Government, Interwest, Willdan, Milrose, and software vendors (Tyler, Accela, OpenGov, Clariti) with AI plan review from GreenLite ($49.5M Series B), Archistar, CodeComply.AI/CivicPlus and CivCheck/Clariti.

**Why they lose.** Outsourcers staff in person and are paid by hours or fee share, not clock guarantees. Software vendors sell tools priced at a fraction of labor (Denver's about $4.6M over five years versus a department's payroll) and avoid indemnifying deemed approvals.

**Moat.** Switching Costs (embedded systems and staff) and Counter-Positioning against hourly consultants.

**Earned secret.** The department has the money (fee reserves) but cannot deploy it; sell outcomes, not hours.

**First 90 days.** Offer 3-5 Florida departments a 90-day overflow pilot with a guaranteed turnaround; report days saved and fee revenue retained.

**Outside-the-box score: 3.** This is the incumbent model with a guarantee.

## 8. Energize-on-Schedule: Inspection, Commissioning and AHJ Desk for Hyperscaler Build Programs (shock: AI capex is a new buyer with a huge cost of delay)

**One-liner.** A program-level service that guarantees the inspection, special-inspection, commissioning and certificate-of-occupancy path across a hyperscaler's pipeline, priced against the idle-capital cost it removes.

**Customer.** About 15 hyperscaler and colocation delivery heads.

**Payer and signer.** VP of construction or head of delivery, from the capex soft-cost line.

**Problem.** Every site has a different building department, special inspection agency and commissioning vendor. A one-month slip on a roughly 60 MW site was estimated at over $14M of lost value (secondary blog; about $0.47M a day by my arithmetic). Census data center construction was $50.7B annualized in April 2026 (+28.1%), and consultancies call zoning and permitting a primary bottleneck.

**Solution.** A standardized program with pre-registered private provider and special inspection agencies, one commissioning-data pipeline, a scheduling desk and a guaranteed temporary-certificate-of-occupancy date, insurance-backed.

**Why now.** Hyperscaler capex above $600B in 2026 by several estimates. Texas Governor Abbott paused TCEQ permits for data centers on Sept 21, 2026, showing how regulatory friction can bind (environmental, not building).

**Wedge.** Virginia, Texas and Arizona colocation developers; one or two sites, using existing third-party programs such as Loudoun's optional third-party inspection program.

**Path to $100M.** Commissioning is 0.75-2% of construction (1.5-3.5%+ for critical facilities) and special inspection plus AHJ liaison add maybe 0.5% (assumption). On about $60B of construction a year (between the $50.7B April rate and an unverified $75B July pace), the pool is about $0.75-1.5B, so $100M is 7-13%, or 10 buyers at $10M each. Heroic.

**Who does it today.** Milrose, Terracon, Intertek, Bureau Veritas, SAFEbuilt in some jurisdictions, owner's reps (JLL, Cushman, Jacobs, AECOM), commissioning specialists, and the hyperscalers' own teams.

**Why they lose.** Local fragmentation; owner's reps bill by the hour; labs bill time and materials; no one can guarantee dates.

**Moat.** Process Power and Switching Costs; a Cornered Resource in AHJ approvals.

**Earned secret.** The cost is the date, not the fee. Power and transformer lead times (36 months in one report) may bind first, in which case the product becomes commissioning and integrated-systems-test scheduling.

**First 90 days.** Sign one colocation developer's Northern Virginia or Texas site for a commissioning-plus-AHJ pilot; map every sign-off; guarantee one temporary-certificate-of-occupancy date.

**Outside-the-box score: 5.**

## Ideas considered and killed

- **Instant-permit rail for HVAC and water heaters (SolarAPP+ generalization).** SolarAPP+ Foundation charges installers $35 per solar permit ($25 storage add-on) and is free to jurisdictions, with 280+ jurisdictions and 77,000+ permits. Ava is funding its extension to heat pump water heaters. California SB 222 (passed the legislature; signing unconfirmed) would cap fees at $150/$200 and mandate instant automated permitting. A free standard plus a tiny per-permit price cannot reach $100M.
- **Opening-Day Desk (CO-date guarantee for multi-site operators).** Six chains (Chipotle 350-370, Dollar General about 450, Aldi 180+, Dutch Bros 185+, Planet Fitness 180-190, Starbucks US 150-175) open only about 1,500 sites a year in total; $100M would need near-total share of the top-100 operators, while PermitFlow and Milrose already serve them.
- **Aerial-change-detection recovery for assessors.** EagleView/Pictometry price flat; counties report $50k-$350k of added revenue each. Too small.
- **Permit and impact-fee recovery audits.** The Sheetz remand went against the developer (July 2025), a second petition is pending, and no refund wave appeared.
- **Workforce Pell inspector academy.** Only about 4% of existing short-term programs are projected to qualify in North Carolina; awards are about $1,260-4,310 per student; programs must have run 12 months.
- **AI plan review for cities.** Crowded (GreenLite, Clariti/CivCheck, Archistar, CodeComply.AI/CivicPlus) with contracts of $0.75-4.6M.
- **Deemed-approval certificate as a standalone per-event rail.** Tens of millions at best.
