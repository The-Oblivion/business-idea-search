# P123: US biofuel producers' purchases of fats, oils and grease feedstock (round 2 ideas)

Pool file says about $26B a year (EIA July 2026 run rate times an assumed $0.50-0.60 a lb). Research says it is bigger and still growing. OPIS projects biomass-based diesel feedstock demand of 34.2B lb in 2025, 53.8B lb in 2026 and 62.0B lb in 2027 (domestic 20.5 / 32.8 / 34.6B lb; imported 13.7 / 21.0 / 27.4B lb). At roughly $0.65 a lb blended (soybean oil forecast 70c; US Gulf yellow grease $1,150-1,250 a tonne, about 52-57c; Gulf tallow hit a record $1,995.81 a tonne, about 90c, on 3 June) that is about $35B in 2026 and about $40B in 2027 (my arithmetic).

"(my estimate)" means I could not source the number. Treat those as hypotheses for the first 90 days. I ran about 35 searches, more than the 8-15 asked, because the first round showed the pool file's "no funded startups found" was wrong.

## What the research changed

- **The paper on a lot is now worth about as much as the oil.** D4 RINs traded 210-236c in late August 2026. 45Z credits trade at 80-93c on the dollar (Crux: about $825M of 45Z traded in 2025, the first year). UCO earns roughly 120c a gallon in California LCFS credits versus about 83c for soybean oil. Diamond Green Diesel (Darling/Valero) made $2.23 a gallon of EBITDA on 348.8M gallons in Q2 2026. For one 45,000-lb UCO load (about 5,800 gallons) I get roughly $21,900 of RIN value (1.7 RINs a gallon at $2.20), about $2,900 of 45Z (at $0.50 a gallon) and about $7,000 of LCFS, against about $24,800 of oil at 55c (my arithmetic).
- **Plants are under-running, and the constraint is coordination, not demand.** Argus: biodiesel plants ran at about 77% and renewable diesel at about 78% in May, against the 90% EPA assumed. Producers generated 736M BBD RINs in a month that needed about 915M. EIA counts 45 biodiesel plants with 1.96B gallons of capacity (1 Jan 2026) and 189 ethanol plants. Industry groups say the bottleneck is coordinating feedstock supply, processing capacity and distribution logistics.
- **Rules in motion.** EPA's final RFS (announced 27 March, published 1 April): 25.82B RINs for 2026 and 25.98B for 2027. The 50% import RIN reduction was NOT finalized; EPA says it intends 2028 or shortly after, and no Canada/Mexico exemption is signalled. 45Z: proposed regs 4 Feb 2026, final targeted November 2026; fuel must use US/Mexico/Canada feedstock. USDA's FD-CIC final rule (29 June, effective 29 July) is voluntary, covers corn, soybeans, sorghum and spring canola, and needs mass-balance traceability. DOE's June GREET update removed indirect land-use change. On 31 August EPA exempted 1.76B RINs for 29 small refineries and promised a reallocation proposal by end of October.
- **Supply is squeezed from three sides.** UCO imports fell 38% year on year (China-origin down 67%, from 1,124 to 368 kt). Brazil's tallow exports fell 34.9% in Jan-Aug. The US cattle herd was 86.2M head in January, the smallest since 1951, with rebuilding not expected before 2027-28. Soybean oil going to biofuel: 14.6B lb (2025/26) to a forecast 17.8B lb (2026/27), 53.7% of use against 40% two years earlier. Oil is about 50% of crush value (CME oilshare 50.65% in March 2026 versus a historical average near 37%).
- **The pool file's "no funded startups" is wrong.** Veriflux (EPA SBIR funded; 1B lb traced; 850 companies in 20+ countries) and Verity (Gevo subsidiary; CIBO partnership 17 March 2026) sell traceability and 45Z records. Tagaddod (Egypt) raised a $26.3M Series A for waste-lipid aggregation and traceability. Greasezilla (bp finances up to 40 receiving stations at up to $5M each) is already recovering brown grease. Consolidators: DAR PRO (Darling) serves roughly 200,000 foodservice locations; Liquid Environmental Solutions went from Audax to Goldman Sachs Alternatives in 2025; Denali (TPG); Restaurant Technologies (RTI) has about 40,000 customers and recycled 363M lb of UCO in 2024; Neste owns Mahoney Environmental.
- **Premiums for waste fats have narrowed but not closed.** 2026 premiums over soybean oil: UCO +5.1c, tallow +3.7c, distillers corn oil +4.7c a lb (Fastmarkets), roughly $970M a year on EIA run-rate volumes (my arithmetic).
- **Fees alone rarely reach $100M here.** Verification, traceability and compliance pools in this chain are tens of millions each. The $100M paths below mostly need a merchant or asset spread on a large flow, or a majority share of a fee pool. Where that is true I say so.

## Ideas

### 1. Grease Elevator (insider: UCO route collector, 20 years)

**One-liner.** Neutral, heated, assayed drop-off terminals where independent grease collectors and small renderers deliver any load size, get paid the same day, and have their oil consolidated into full tankers sold on term contracts with origin paperwork attached: a grain elevator for waste lipids.

- **Customer.** About 3,000 independent UCO collectors and small renderers (sellers), and the roughly 60 independent biodiesel, renewable-diesel and co-processing buyers that Darling's DGD does not supply.
- **Payer and signer.** Sellers pay handling and storage (about 1-1.5c a lb) and accept index minus a spread; buyers pay a fixed spread over index on 3-12 month contracts. Signers: the collector's owner-operator; the buyer's VP Feedstock. Budget line: feedstock procurement (buyer), revenue (seller).
- **Problem.** A collector's economics are made or lost between the truck and the plant. One route fills 5,000-20,000 lb, a tanker takes 45,000 lb, and he has no heated storage, scale-lab or the stop-level paper EPA auditors now ask for. The only buyer within 100 miles is the integrated renderer that also competes for his restaurants. So he sells at a posted discount to index, paid in 30 days or more (insider claim, size unverified).
- **Solution.** About 40 leased terminal sites (grain elevators, tank farms near rail), each with 200-500k lb of heated tankage, a certified scale, an auto-sampler with rapid assay (FFA, moisture-impurities, phosphorus, chloride), a loading rack and a rail spur where possible. Collectors drop 1,000-45,000 lb, are weighed and sampled, and are paid the same day against an electronic warehouse receipt they can pledge for a working-capital advance. Stop-level pickup logs (GPS, time, photo) travel with each lot as the origin file. The terminal blends to spec, consolidates into full tankers or rail cars, and sells on term contracts. It also takes brown grease, DAF fat, small-packer fat and DCO from independent ethanol plants.
- **Why now.** Demand is up 57% in 2026 and 81% by 2027 against 2025 (OPIS); independents are running at 75-77% and restarting; EPA has audited UCO collection locations since July 2023; imported UCO is down 38%; paper is worth as much as oil.
- **Wedge.** Three terminals in Iowa/Minnesota/Illinois (soy-biodiesel restarts plus rail to the Gulf) and one in the Southeast. First ten customers: eight collectors within 100 miles of each site and two independent producers (the kind of buyer that restarted or is restarting: Minnesota Soybean Processors' Brewster plant, FutureFuel's Batesville plant).
- **Path to $100M.** 40 terminals x 50M lb = 2.0B lb a year x 5c all-in (1.5c handling, 2.5c merchandising spread, 1c blend and finance) = $100M of net margin on about $1.3B of gross sales. 2.0B lb is 3.7% of 2026 feedstock demand and about 30% of a "small-supplier" universe of 6-7B lb (my estimate: about 1.8B lb independent UCO [EIA yellow grease run rate 7.3B lb less about 3.3B lb imports, Darling and RTI]; about 2B lb small-renderer and packer fat; about 2B lb DCO from independent ethanol plants; about 0.5B lb brown grease). Capex $1-3M a site if leased, $40-120M in total, plus about $40M of working capital. Heroic share, and the 5c is an insider guess.
- **Who does it today.** Darling/Valley transfer stations (captive); Baker Commodities, Sanimax, Griffin and other independent renderers; brokers (Targray, ISCC-certified); Tagaddod (MENA); Neste's Mahoney; RTI's closed loop. Tank terminals (Vopak, Kinder Morgan) do not take small drops or assay them. No neutral multi-site drop-off network found (not exhaustively searched).
- **Why they lose.** Darling cannot be neutral: it competes with every collector for restaurant contracts and with every independent producer for supply, and sells to DGD first (DGD was about 20% of Darling's net sales in H1 2026, from a secondary source). Global traders need 5,000-tonne lots. Terminals do not do assays, stop-level paper or same-day pay on 3,000-lb drops.
- **Moat.** Scale economies (terminal density shortens hauls; lab and scale fixed cost spread over volume), counter-positioning against integrated renderers, switching costs (receipts, advances, paper), and a transaction-price dataset. This is the marketplace-owned logistics, payments and credit flywheel from the digest.
- **Earned secret.** Collectors do not lose money finding restaurants; they lose it on the last 100 miles and the 30-day pay. The small-lot discount, the slow pay and the missing paper are one problem because nobody neutral sits between truck and plant. (Insider claim; verify against 30 settlement sheets.)
- **First 90 days.** Collect 20 collector settlement sheets and match them to the index to measure the real discount; interview 8 buyers; lease one site and price tankage and scale; pre-sell a 6-month term to one producer; settle the electronic warehouse-receipt structure under UCC Article 7 and line up a lender for advances.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 5.

### 2. Bankable Supply (insider: refinery feedstock buyer, 15+ years)

**One-liner.** A pooled-supply company that signs 3-7 year feedstock supply agreements (volume bands, index plus a fixed fee, a carbon-intensity true-up, a delivery guarantee) with independent renewable-diesel, biodiesel, SAF and co-processing projects, and fills them back-to-back from hundreds of small collectors, renderers, ethanol plants and crushers.

- **Customer.** Independent producers and developers that cannot get a long-term contract today: restarting biodiesel plants, farmer-owned renewable-diesel and SAF projects, refinery co-processors. About 30-40 projects (my estimate).
- **Payer and signer.** CFO or project-finance lead plus VP Feedstock at the producer; the lender's technical advisor has to bless the terms. Budget: feedstock cost, and a financing condition that has no other owner.
- **Problem.** Project lenders need a 5-10 year feedstock agreement with a creditworthy counterparty. Independents get either a parent that supplies everything (XCF's New Rise Reno buys all non-food feedstock from Phillips 66 at spot plus fees, a single-counterparty risk its 10-K flags) or a trader (Trafigura supplies about 470,000 t a year of UCO to Brazil's Acelen behind a $1.5B financing). In the US the independent middle has neither. Darling will not supply competitors of DGD, crushers sell soybean oil on annual terms, and 3,000 collectors cannot sign ten-year take-or-pay. In January producers were reported to be hesitating on long-term contracts because 2026-27 CI scores were unsettled.
- **Solution.** Sign supply agreements on a standard form with a CI true-up clause (price adjusts annually to the final 45Z emissions rate), volume bands and a replacement-supply guarantee backed by reinsurance. Fill them from a book of 300+ supplier contracts, Grease Elevator terminals and ethanol-plant DCO. Hedge the flat price back-to-back; carry origin files for every lot.
- **Why now.** 2027 demand is 81% above 2025 (OPIS). 45Z final rules are due in November. The EPA reallocation proposal is due by end of October. Import-RIN reduction in 2028 makes domestic-origin certainty the product. Plants are restarting. The pending rules are exactly why nobody is signing, so the first standard form wins.
- **Wedge.** One 3-year, 250M lb a year agreement with an independent producer, filled from four Grease Elevator terminals, two ethanol-plant DCO contracts and one mid-size renderer. First ten suppliers signed with letters of intent before the producer signs.
- **Path to $100M.** 20 agreements x 250M lb = 5.0B lb x 2c net (0.8c logistics, 0.7c risk and guarantee, 0.5c CI and true-up) = $100M net on about $3.2B of gross sales. That is 9% of 2026 feedstock volume. Capital: about $150M of working capital, hedging lines and a reinsurance layer, from infrastructure or credit funds. Moderately heroic; the risk is that two or three counterparties are 60% of volume.
- **Who does it today.** Darling (captive to DGD), Chevron REG and Phillips 66 (captive), trader-backed supply abroad (Trafigura/Acelen), Targray (ISCC-certified trading), Tagaddod (MENA aggregation; $26.3M Series A), crushers (annual soybean oil contracts). No US independent pooled-supply aggregator found (not exhaustively searched).
- **Why they lose.** Darling is conflicted. Traders prefer 5,000-tonne lots and will not carry first-mile risk. Crushers sell one feedstock. Lenders will not accept spot.
- **Moat.** Cornered resource (a portfolio of supplier contracts and terminals), scale in procurement, switching costs (agreements pledged under project debt), counter-positioning against integrated renderers.
- **Earned secret.** A lender does not care that the oil is 3c cheaper; it cares that someone creditworthy owes delivery for ten years. A buyer spends half his year proving that (supplier audits, backup suppliers, force-majeure language) and still cannot sign small suppliers. The same pooled paper solves both.
- **First 90 days.** Get term sheets from two independent producers; interview 10 project-finance technical advisors for the exact FSA terms they require; sign letters of intent with 10 suppliers; line up a hedging counterparty and one reinsurer for the delivery guarantee; draft the CI true-up clause with a 45Z tax lawyer once the November rule lands.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 5.

### 3. Cleared Lot (pattern: grading chokepoint plus mandate-created rails)

**One-liner.** The PSA of waste lipids: a private grade and sealed-sample certificate for every load of UCO, tallow and corn oil, with a binding deduction schedule so the gate-lab dispute disappears, an origin file, and an eligibility warranty for RIN and 45Z validity sold through an independent insurer.

- **Why it hasn't happened here.** Inspectors (SGS, Intertek, Saybolt, Bureau Veritas and others) are paid per inspection by traders and have no reason to standardize. RFS auditors (QAP providers) sample after the fact. Traceability software (Veriflux, Verity) has no physical sample. Insurers have no lot-level data. Darling's paper is private to Darling.
- **Customer.** Producers and refiners that buy waste-derived and imported lipids (buyers); collectors and aggregators (sellers).
- **Payer and signer.** Sellers pay $60-90 a lot for sealed sampling, assay and the certificate. Buyers pay a warranty premium of about 0.3-0.6c a lb on high-risk lots. Signers: VP Feedstock, Head of Compliance (RIN) and CFO (45Z).
- **Problem.** On the arithmetic above a load carries about $32k of credit-stack value against about $25k of oil. Obligated parties that retire invalid RINs must replace them unless they hold verified RINs (my reading of the RFS quality-assurance rules; confirm with counsel). EPA has audited UCO supply chains since 2023, and bipartisan senators called its verification "meaningless" in 2024 letters. 45Z insurance exclusions (fraud, known risks, change in law) leave a gap. At the gate, buyers and sellers argue over free fatty acids, impurities and metals every week.
- **Solution.** (1) Tamper-evident auto-sampler at the loading rack, a rapid assay (FFA, moisture-impurities, XRF metals), and a risk-based isotope and fatty-acid fingerprint on a sample of lots. (2) A one-page lot certificate that buyers reference in contracts: it fixes the deduction schedule and attaches stop-level origin logs and weigh tickets. (3) A lot-level eligibility warranty written by a separate insurer or MGA (the verifier must stay independent of the warranty, as QAP rules expect). (4) Payment instruction and a transaction-based price index as by-products.
- **Why now.** D4 RIN above $2; 45Z being sold for the first time; an import-RIN haircut from 2028 that makes origin a per-lot fact; 62B lb of feedstock by 2027; EPA audits.
- **Wedge.** Seller side: the same 20 collectors that use Grease Elevator terminals. Buyer side: three independent producers that publicly restarted and have thin compliance teams. Start with imported and mixed-origin UCO lots, which carry the most disallowance risk.
- **Path to $100M.** About 25B lb of waste-derived lipids a year (EIA July run rate: UCO 7.3B lb, tallow 9.3B lb, corn oil 5.4B lb, other greases about 3B lb). About 450k lots x $75 = $34M of assay fees, plus warranty on 15B lb x 0.5c = $75M, total about $110M. That needs a majority of waste-derived lots, so the standard has to win; at 25% share it is about $28M. Heroic.
- **Who does it today.** SGS, Intertek, Bureau Veritas/Inspectorate, Saybolt, AmSpec, Alfred H Knight (inspection); EcoEngineers (an LRQA company) and other EPA-approved QAP providers; Veriflux, Verity/CIBO (software); Marsh and IMA (45Z tax-credit insurance); ISCC (a sustainability scheme, not a lot-level assay).
- **Why they lose.** Inspectors sell hours; QAP auditors sell periodic audits and cannot also be the insurer; software providers cannot touch the oil; none can bind buyer and seller to one deduction schedule.
- **Moat.** Network standard (contracts cite "Cleared Lot"), capacity-limited lab network (the PSA analogue), loss data for underwriting. Race-the-bundle risk: Darling or a large inspector could ship a certificate. Needs a second product (warranty or index) before that happens.
- **Earned secret.** At a refinery the lab at the gate is the real contract. Buyers pay a fear premium for suppliers they trust because untrusted lots cost them rejected loads, catalyst life and compliance risk. A trustworthy sealed sample is worth more than 1c a lb to a buyer even when the oil itself is identical. (Insider claim; verify in the first 90 days.)
- **First 90 days.** Get 12 months of reject and dispute history from three buyers; pilot sealed samplers at two terminals; interview two 45Z insurers and one QAP provider about independence and exclusions; confirm with RFS counsel who bears replacement liability; price-test $60 / $90 / $120 a lot.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 6.

### 4. Oil Yield Partner (pattern: stalled supply technology re-rated by a deep-pocketed buyer, financed like energy-as-a-service)

**One-liner.** Zero-capex, pay-from-the-oil upgrades of corn-oil recovery at ethanol plants: we install and run the extraction system, guarantee the yield, and take a share of the extra oil for five years; the plant keeps the baseline and then owns the equipment.

- **Why it hasn't happened here.** Corn-oil recovery technology sat at modest value for a decade. Oil was worth perhaps 25-30c a lb in 2019 (my estimate) and about 70c now, which turned a 3-4 year payback (my estimate) into under one year. ICM's FOT system is installed or being built at about 8 plants (Oct 2024); Fluid Quip, Valicor and enzyme vendors sell equipment and chemistry, and none was found offering a revenue-share or yield-guarantee contract.
- **Customer.** The 189 US ethanol plants, mostly farmer-owned LLCs with risk-averse boards.
- **Payer and signer.** Plant general manager and board; budget line: no capex, paid out of incremental oil revenue.
- **Problem.** Average US plants recover 0.75-0.90 lb of corn oil a bushel, against a theoretical 1.9 and a practical ceiling near 1.3 (Novozymes, IFF). Elite Octane reports 1.31 lb a bushel after ICM's FOT; IFF reports 0.89 to 1.03 from enzymes alone. On a 29M bushel plant the gap is about 10M lb a year, about $7M at 70c.
- **Solution.** Choose and install the best of the vendors' systems per plant, operate it with remote process control, guarantee yield (pay the shortfall), market the oil in pooled lots with origin and CI files, and take 30-35% of the incremental oil for five years.
- **Why now.** The deep-pocketed buyer is renewable diesel (62B lb by 2027); the new supply it needs without land, cattle or imports is the oil already inside the corn. 2B lb of extra corn oil is about 4% of 2026 demand (my arithmetic: 5.5B bushels x 0.35 lb).
- **Wedge.** Ten plants in one rail-served corridor with a Fluid Quip or Valicor install base, a guaranteed floor, and a pooled oil lot sold to one producer. First customers: plants that have not committed to FOT and have lender covenants that allow a revenue share.
- **Path to $100M.** 40 plants x 10.2M lb extra a year x 70c x 35% share = about $100M. That is 21% of plants, and capex of about $240M ($6M a plant). Honest check: a plant that can self-fund pays back in about 0.85 year, so only the plants that will not self-fund (perhaps 40% of plants) are addressable, which makes this over half of the addressable set. Heroic, and the window is probably 18-24 months. Lender consent (CoBank, AgStar and others often restrict liens and revenue sharing) is a gate.
- **Who does it today.** ICM (FOT, 8 sites), Fluid Quip Technologies (DCO systems), Valicor (a $4.4M modular unit), Novonesis and IFF (enzymes), plant in-house teams. No performance-contract provider found.
- **Why they lose.** Vendors sell equipment once and are not paid for yield; they will not guarantee the oil.
- **Moat.** Process power from a yield dataset across plants and switching costs from the guarantee; weak, because OEMs can add a finance arm.
- **Earned secret.** A plant manager's problem is not the 5% payback, it is who gets blamed if the new skid misses its number. A yield guarantee moves that risk off his board.
- **First 90 days.** Pull yield data from 15 plants; get a vendor price on a modular system; ask two lenders whether a revenue share is permitted; price the guarantee; sign one pilot at a no-capex trial.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 4.

### 5. Second Stream (dilemma: haulers cannot cannibalize their own pump-outs)

**One-liner.** Put an automatic grease-removal unit at fryer-heavy restaurants and collect the skimmed grease on the same stop as the used cooking oil: the restaurant pays a flat fee that is less than it saves on pumping, and we sell the grease as feedstock.

- **Customer.** About 700,000 US restaurants have grease traps or interceptors (my estimate). Target: fryer-heavy, high-volume sites (quick service, chicken, burger, hotels, casinos) that are pumped monthly to quarterly.
- **Payer and signer.** Restaurant owner or GM (budget line: grease-trap maintenance, about $350-500 a pump-out, 4-12 a year, plus fines of $200-5,000 an incident); for chains, the facilities director. City FOG inspectors are the push.
- **Problem.** EPA estimates 800-17,000 lb a year of fats, oils and grease per restaurant. An automatic removal unit typically takes 7-10 lb a day at a high-volume burger restaurant (2,500-3,650 lb a year), fresh and mostly water-free. Haulers pump the trap, dewater it and landfill or treat it, so most of it is wasted as feedstock, and the restaurant pays for it.
- **Solution.** Place units (leased, financed), install, and bundle a flat monthly service with a weekly skimmed-grease pickup on the existing used-oil route. Sell the grease as brown-grade feedstock with origin logs. Pump-outs drop to one or two a year for solids.
- **Why now.** Waste-lipid prices are at records, North American-origin waste is the 45Z-favoured feed, and brown grease is the lowest-carbon, least-contested pool of domestic fat. Greasezilla and bp have shown a buyer will fund recovery, but only at the treatment plant.
- **Wedge.** One metro with an active FOG ordinance that accepts certified units: 150 quick-service and hotel kitchens signed by a UCO collector partner, pump-out logs as the baseline for the savings guarantee.
- **Path to $100M.** 46,000 sites x $2,250 a year = about $103M ($1,200 service fee plus about 3,000 lb of skimmed grease at 35c, my estimate) is about 6.6% of the interceptor base. Gross margin about $650 a site after hardware amortization and marginal route cost (about 29%); capex about $230M ($5k a site). Check: LES-style haulers hold the pump-out revenue, and 46,000 is a fraction of the roughly 200,000 locations DAR PRO already visits.
- **Who does it today.** Liquid Environmental Solutions (Goldman Sachs Alternatives; trap pumping, treatment plants and UCO), Denali (TPG), DAR PRO; Greasezilla (at the treatment plant); unit makers Thermaco/Big Dipper, Highland Tank and others (hardware, not service). No source-capture service paying restaurants found.
- **Why they lose.** Haulers are paid per pump-out and own treatment plants that charge tipping fees; a unit that cuts pumping cuts their revenue. Unit makers sell through plumbing distributors and do not run routes.
- **Moat.** Route density plus switching costs (installed hardware, multi-year service contract) and counter-positioning.
- **Earned secret.** A UCO driver is already at the back door every week. The second stream costs a minute of stop time and is the restaurant's biggest hidden bill. Everybody who sells the unit leaves out the pickup; everybody who owns the pickup sells pump-outs.
- **First 90 days.** Interview 20 fast-food and hotel GMs on pump-out invoices; confirm the AGRU is accepted as an interceptor in three metros (ASME A112.14.3 or PDI-G101 certification); get a unit maker price; find a UCO collector willing to co-run; test the 35c grease price with two buyers.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 5.

### 6. Oil Check (dilemma: crushers profit from the missing price signal)

**One-liner.** Pay soybean farmers for the oil they deliver: a per-load oil, protein and moisture settlement at independent elevators and farmer-owned crushers, a variety-level "pounds of oil per acre" score for seed choice, and identity-preserved premium contracts, funded by the crusher's extra margin.

- **Customer.** Farmer-owned and independent crushers and their elevators; farmers (indirectly); seed companies for variety data.
- **Payer and signer.** Crusher's head of origination or CEO (cooperatives: the GM and board). Budget line: grain-origination premiums; paid as a share of the extra margin.
- **Problem.** Oil is about 50% of crush value (CME oilshare 50.65% in March 2026), against a long-run 37%. Soybeans are still bought by the bushel at one price. A crusher measures oil and protein at the gate and keeps the variation. A Purdue/Iowa State estimate put the gain from sorting at 12c a bushel on average and 25c at most (older data). One processor already pays elevators on protein and oil (an estimated processing value, with a quarterly check for the top 25%), but the practice is not national. NOPA oil yield ranges 11.3-11.8 lb a bushel. A one-point difference in oil is about 0.5 lb a bushel, about $0.35 at 70c, less roughly $0.10-0.15 of meal lost to the oil-protein trade-off (my arithmetic).
- **Solution.** Per-load NIR oil and moisture at the pit, a settlement engine that pays a premium on the processing value, variety scores from yield times oil, and contracts that tell farmers which varieties to plant. Priced as a share of the crusher's extra margin.
- **Why now.** The oil share flipped in 2025-26, meal is at its lowest since 2016 (DTN), and the biofuel pull makes oil per acre the right target. Moisture is a further lever (lower-moisture beans favour buyers).
- **Wedge.** Two farmer-owned processors and 30 elevators in one state, 200,000 acres, one crop year of data, and a premium paid on the top quartile.
- **Path to $100M.** Program fee 4c a bushel x 1.5B bushels (60% of a crush of about 2.5B bushels, my estimate) = $60M; settlement software 0.5c a bushel across 2.5B = $12.5M; variety data and seed-company licensing $10M; adding corn for ethanol plants (oil and starch) at 1.5c a bushel on 1.0B bushels is $15M. Total about $97M. That needs well over half of US crush, so it is heroic, and the three crushers that do most of the crushing profit from the status quo.
- **Who does it today.** One processor's protein-and-oil premium program (source did not name it); seed companies (Corteva, Bayer and others) market yield, not oil per acre; grain software such as Bushel settles by grade. Nobody found doing national composition-priced settlement.
- **Why they lose.** ADM, Bunge and Cargill earn extra margin from receiving high-oil beans at a flat price; paying for oil gives it away. Elevators bear the cost of measuring and capture none of the gain.
- **Moat.** Data on variety-level oil per acre plus a payment rail; switching costs once farmers plant by it; counter-positioning against the large crushers.
- **Earned secret.** Dairy moved from volume to components pricing decades ago and yields climbed; soybeans have not, because the party that sees the composition (the crusher) is also the party that benefits from hiding it.
- **First 90 days.** Pull gate NIR data from two farmer-owned crushers; build the oil-per-acre score from public variety trials; interview 15 elevator managers; price a premium schedule that leaves the crusher 60% of the gain.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 7.

### 7. Co-Pro Desk (shock: refiners become the new buyer)

**One-liner.** Sell petroleum refiners hydrotreater-ready lipid feed, receiving skids, 14C sampling and RIN accounting as one service on a RIN-linked formula, so they can generate their own D4 RINs instead of buying them at $2.20.

- **Customer.** About 120 US refineries with distillate hydrotreaters, starting with obligated merchant refiners that are RIN-short. Not the integrated renewable-diesel majors.
- **Payer and signer.** VP Supply and Trading or the refinery manager; budget line: RIN purchases, the obligated party's largest compliance cost.
- **Problem.** EPA set record obligations, D4 RINs rose from 163c in February to 210-236c in August, moving between about 191c and 236c in the last week of August as exemption decisions landed. Topsoe says up to 10% renewable feed can go into a diesel hydrotreater with limited modifications; bp's Cherry Point refinery reports more than 7,000 barrels a day of co-processing capability. Few refiners have receiving tanks, a feed spec or a way to measure the renewable fraction.
- **Solution.** Pre-treated lipids (low phosphorus and metals) delivered in tank trucks or rail cars; heated receiving skid; catalyst and unit support from the licensors; ASTM D6866 carbon-14 testing per batch; EMTS and RIN reporting; formula pricing tied to RIN and diesel.
- **Why now.** RINs up about 45% this year, RVO up about 60%, plants under-running at 75-78%, and no more imports to lean on. Refiners are the one buyer class that is short the credit.
- **Wedge.** Three merchant refiners with a diesel hydrotreater and rail, 3-5% co-processing trial, a single-lot guarantee on feed spec.
- **Path to $100M.** 25 refineries x 150M lb a year = 3.75B lb x 2.5c net = about $94M on about $2.4B of gross sales (7% of 2026 demand). Per refinery, 150M lb is about 19.5M gallons: about 33M RINs ($73M at $2.20) plus $49M of displaced diesel against about $98M of feed and about $10M of processing, roughly $14M a year, without 45Z or LCFS (my arithmetic). Thin, but it beats buying RINs at $2.20 (self-generated RINs cost about $1.76 each).
- **Legal premise to confirm.** RINs for the renewable fraction of co-processed fuel by 14C (ASTM D6866) under EPA's approach; whether the final 45Z regs treat co-processed fuel as eligible (unverified); unit constraints (corrosion, cold flow, hydrogen).
- **Who does it today.** bp Cherry Point, a few others (not verified); licensors Topsoe, Honeywell UOP, Axens (technology); catalyst makers; Beta Analytic-type 14C labs; DGD and the full-conversion plants (not co-processing). Nobody found selling feed, skids, testing and RIN accounting as a package.
- **Why they lose.** Licensors sell technology, not molecules; Darling will not supply refiners that compete with DGD; traders cannot pre-treat; labs only test.
- **Moat.** Process power (feed spec learned unit by unit), switching costs (the unit is tuned to our feed), data.
- **Earned secret.** A refinery does not need a renewable-diesel plant to make RINs; it needs a 3-5% blend that its operators trust. The blocker is a feed they can specify, a tank they can heat and a lab number they can defend, not RIN economics.
- **First 90 days.** Interview 20 refinery supply managers and 5 hydrotreater licensors; get EPA counsel's read on 14C co-processing registration and 45Z treatment; price a 3% pilot on a 40 kb/d unit; line up pre-treated feed from one terminal.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 6.

### 8. Premium Ledger (shock: carbon intensity becomes a per-lot price)

**One-liner.** Measure each lot's carbon intensity from plant-level actuals (rendering and crush-plant energy, transport mode, allocation, farm practices) and clear the premium between supplier and producer by formula, taking a fee on the premium and a share of the credit that default scores leave unclaimed.

- **Customer.** About 90 US biomass-based diesel producers and their roughly 1,500 first-tier suppliers (renderers, crushers, UCO aggregators, ethanol corn-oil plants).
- **Payer and signer.** Producer's CFO or VP Tax; VP Feedstock. Budget line: the CI premium they already pay (about $970M a year, my arithmetic) and the credit they are not claiming.
- **Problem.** Under 45Z a gallon earns up to $1.00 times (50 minus CI) / 50 when wage rules are met (my reading of the statute; the search summary quoted only the $0.20 base), so each CI point is worth about $0.02 a gallon. Most producers file with GREET defaults because measured numbers need data from five to eight parties in the chain. USDA's FD-CIC final rule and the June GREET update reward measured farm and plant inputs but give no rail to collect and verify them. Credits trade at 5-11% discounts partly because buyers need diligence.
- **Solution.** A supplier-data network with meter and bill ingestion, mass-balance across the chain, and lot-level CI packages for the producer's independent verifier (the platform does not verify). Premium paid by formula: dollars a lb = f(measured CI points, current credit value per point). Settlement through the same ledger as Cleared Lot.
- **Why now.** FD-CIC effective 29 July; final 45Z rules in November; first returns in 2027; buyers of credits diligence at a discount.
- **Wedge.** UCO, tallow and corn-oil chains (three hops, ten buyers), then soy crush plants.
- **Path to $100M.** Premium flows of about $970M a year x 8% clearing fee = $78M, plus 20% of unlocked uplift (3 CI points x $0.02 x about 3B waste-based gallons = $180M, so $36M) = $114M, but only at near-total share of the waste-lipid chain. Adding soybean and canola farm-level CI (5-8 points on about 2.2B gallons of soy-based fuel = $220-350M) or corn for ethanol (5.8c a bushel per CI point, from a trade article) is what makes the share plausible. Heroic; closest to dead.
- **Who does it today.** Veriflux (1B lb traced since 2020; EPA/45Z/LCFS records), Verity/Gevo with CIBO (field-level data), EcoEngineers and other consultants and verifiers, Truterra, Indigo, Bushel, Regrow and the crushers' own programs on the farm side.
- **Why they lose.** Software firms do not touch the oil and cannot bind a price formula; consultants bill hours; crushers will not give suppliers a share of credit they keep.
- **Moat.** A supplier-data network and a formula that becomes the contract reference; weak, and it should be treated as a feature of Cleared Lot unless a producer pays for it alone.
- **Earned secret.** Auditors know where the points hide: transport miles and mode (rail versus truck), steam fuel at rendering plants, co-product allocation, and farm practices. Most are available only if someone collects primary data from a plant manager who has no reason to answer a producer's email.
- **First 90 days.** Pull default and actual CI inputs for 10 UCO and tallow lots; measure the average gap; ask three producers what they would pay per CI point; stop if the gap is under 2 points.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 4.

## Killed during research

- **Mexican UCO collection network.** Market-research estimates put Mexico's whole UCO market near $88M a year, and the 2028 import-RIN reduction (no Canada or Mexico exemption signalled) shortens the window to 14 months.
- **Backhaul on foodservice distributors' trucks.** US Foodservice tried it in 2011 and HAVI collects from all 252 Dutch McDonald's, but DAR PRO already serves about 200,000 US locations, and a $100M path needs about 250,000 restaurants.
- **Grease route roll-up with software** (pool file's first hint). LES (Goldman Sachs Alternatives), Denali (TPG) and DAR PRO are already consolidating pumping plus UCO; multiples are 6-12x EBITDA.
- **Fee-only 45Z/farm CI platform for farmers.** Verity/CIBO, Truterra, Indigo, Bushel, Regrow and the crushers are in; the biofuel-side pool is tens to low hundreds of millions.
- **Pennycress, camelina, carinata offtake.** Bunge and Chevron back CoverCress with a new oilseed plant; 2026 acreage unconfirmed; ILUC removal erased part of the advantage.
- **Oil-rights streaming company.** Reaching 3B lb needs rights to about 330,000 restaurants.
- **Pay-first lending to collectors.** About $80M at best on a $450M balance sheet, and exposed to a price crash.
- **Municipal FOG concession.** Public procurement cycles and about $90M at 100% share.
- **Transaction-based lipid price index.** Futures-style economics on this physical volume are single-digit millions.
