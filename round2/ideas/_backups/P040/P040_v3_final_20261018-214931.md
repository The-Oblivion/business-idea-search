## Pool facts used, and four corrections to the pool file

- Pool size: about $19.5B a year across Fiserv, FIS and Jack Henry segment revenue (pool file). The US share is unverified. My own cut of the community slice (banks and CUs under about $10B) is $8-10B [A], built from: call-report data processing is 16.5-22% of small lenders' budgets vs 10-14% for the biggest [S, Banker & Tradesman]; median bank technology is about 9% of non-interest expense [S, Bank Director]; Webster Five's data-processing line went from $1.23M (Sep 2020) to $2.49M (2025) [S].
- Share: banks - Fiserv 42%, Jack Henry 21%, FIS 9%, CSI 7%, COCC 3%, Finastra 3% (ABA 2022 via Kansas City Fed) [S]. Credit unions - Fiserv 1,155 CUs (25.9%, down from 27.1%), Jack Henry 535 (12.0%), Corelation 3.5% and gaining [S, Credit Union Times]. NCUA counted 4,214 CUs at Q2 2026 (4,411 a year earlier in Q1 2025), $2.50T of assets, average CU $568M, 748 CUs over $500M, so about 3,466 under $500M [S, NCUA; last number derived].
- Conversion frequency: an average FI converts its core about every 8.5 years (FIntegrate claim, so about 1,020 a year across about 8,660 FIs) [S/A]. ICBA says 36% of community banks had run their core five years or less (13% in 2016), which implies about 7% a year or about 320 bank switches [S/A]. I use 500-1,000 core conversions a year. ABA survey cited in a FIntegrate release: 19% of banks likely to convert at next renewal, 47% dissatisfied with their core [S, vendor release].
- Lock-in mechanics: deconversion fees are usually not quoted in the contract and default to the vendor's price list; one consultant saw $200,000 quoted for a 10,000-account deconversion; CU early-termination fees run into seven figures; a new 5-year term can start if the FI upgrades servers, merges another institution in, or upgrades an ancillary product [S, Cornerstone and Barclay Damon].
- Conversion damage: average branch deposit attrition from legal day one to three months after conversion is 11.1%, the worst quartile about 17%, the best under 5% (Curinos); about two-thirds of customers who leave do so in the first month (Deloitte); Community Trust Bank took 18,750 calls in two weeks from a 48,000-customer base after its conversion [S].
- Fiserv (Q2 2026): revenue -3%, adjusted EPS -21%, 2026 guide cut to -1% to flat organic, banking services -4%; new CEO Takis Georgakopoulos since mid-June (Mike Lyons left for Truist); he is "significantly expanding" a review of the mix of businesses; Lyons earlier admitted lost share "especially on the smaller credit union side" [S, American Banker, Payments Dive, Digital Transactions]. Pruning already done: ATM Managed Services, Cash & Logistics and MoneyPass into a JV with Bridgeport Partners (Fiserv keeps 49%); student-loan servicing sold to Infinite Computer Solutions [S, PYMNTS, Payments Dive].
- NEW and not in the pool file: in July 2026 the WSJ and Reuters reported preliminary talks between several large US banks (JPMorgan, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, PNC named) and Fiserv about buying one of its two PIN-debit networks (STAR, Accel). The CEO confirms a sale is under consideration; Cantor analysts expect Fiserv to keep STAR and shed Accel; Raymond James flags regulatory scrutiny. No signed deal [S, secondary summaries].
- Debit prize: Fed 2023 data (published Dec 2025): 100.7B debit and prepaid transactions; total interchange $34.12B; exempt (under-$10B issuer) non-prepaid transactions 29.84B, $1.32T, interchange $14.87B ($0.50 a transaction, 1.12%); network fees to all parties $12.95B ($0.129 a transaction) [S, Federal Reserve, read from the PDF].
- Correction 1, the M&A shock is weaker than the pool file says. 2025 had 188-200 bank deals (Seaport, S&P) but H1 2026 had only 83 and Q2 2026 only 47 deals; Mar-Jun deal value was $2.3B after the Iran war began on 28 Feb 2026 [S, American Banker, Seaport]. The FDIC board proposed faster merger review on 17 Sep 2026 (rapid processing, de minimis class, credit unions in the competitive analysis), comments due about 23 Nov; the Fed, OCC and DOJ have not followed [S, Skadden, Reed Smith]. One secondary source says the 10-year Treasury hit 5.35% on 7 Oct 2026, a 24-year high, with banks' bond losses back in the news [S, single source, unverified].
- Correction 2, no mandate to build on: the CFPB's 1033 open-banking rule is not in force, the CFPB told the court it is unlawful, and the reopened rulemaking is about whether banks may charge fees for data access [S]. Nacha's ACH fraud-monitoring rule is real (phase 1 on 20 Mar 2026, phase 2 on 22 Jun 2026 for everyone else) but shifts no liability and has no enforcement record, so it fails the whiplash filter as forced spend [S].
- Correction 3, the funded-startup list is stale: AI-native cores now exist in the US. Maximum ($30M seed, Emprise Bank leaving FIS, first traffic by end-2026, phased parallel run) [S]; 10x Banking raised GBP40M in Aug 2026, is EBITDA-positive, and ships an "AI Migrate" legacy-mapping tool [S]; Thought Machine reportedly raised about $41M in July 2026 (aggregator, unverified) [S].
- Correction 4, the "contract benchmarking and group buying" wedge is not empty: the Golden Contract Coalition (Paladin fs, Austin, with Pillsbury) launched in June 2016 with a standard master agreement, member fees, close to 50 members and over $1B of contract revenue at the start; it "does not negotiate price". I found no 2024-25 membership data, so ten years on it looks like a boutique [S]. A 2013-14 BPI Network study (using Paladin data, so biased) said CFIs paid about 24% above fair market [S].
- Charter wave: OCC got 14-18 de novo charter applications in 2025, mostly uninsured trust banks, and 28 fintech/crypto filings are tracked [S, KPMG, tracker]. That is dozens of buyers, not thousands, so I did not build on it.

How the eight relate: ideas 1, 4, 5 and 6 are parts of one "exit and conversion stack" (parity engine, vendor-side migration, neutral ledger, customer-graph migration). A founder would not build four companies; the table at the end says which to start with.

## 1. Parity (lens: insider, a core-conversion project manager with 15+ years of go-lives)

- **One-liner:** A fixed-price, date-certain conversion assurance and migration engine that rebuilds how an institution actually runs (from configs, exam findings, tickets, call recordings and staff interviews), proves account-by-account parity before cutover, and keeps every source-to-target mapping as a reusable library.
- **Customer:** The acquirer's COO or Chief Integration Officer when the target's core differs from its own; a $0.5-10B bank or CU switching cores; and the challenger cores (Maximum, 10x, Nymbus, Corelation, Mambu) that need to onboard banks faster.
- **Payer and signer:** The "conversion and integration costs" line of a deal budget or the technology budget, signed by the COO or CIO with board and CEO sign-off. On the challenger side, the VP of Implementation pays per live bank out of cost of sales.
- **Problem:** A conversion is 12-22 months of work (Cornerstone cites a 22-month project), the parity testing runs in Excel by two or three staff who also run the bank, the old vendor delivers flat files with an incomplete data dictionary on its own schedule and price list, and the defects that hurt customers come from rules nobody wrote down.
- **Solution:** (1) Ingest the old core's parameter files, extracts, policies, exam reports, support tickets and recorded staff walk-throughs; agents write a "behaviour spec" (product rules, fee and interest logic, GL mapping, custom reports, exceptions). (2) Generate target-core setup files and data mapping. (3) Run repeated mock conversions with an automated parity harness (balances, accrued interest, holds, fees, statements, tax forms) and produce a signed parity report the board and examiners can read. (4) Price per account with a go-live date, backed by an insurer-funded warranty (design, not yet agreed).
- **Why now:** Deals are still 83-117 a half-year (H1 2026 and H2 2025), the FDIC is about to speed approvals, Fiserv is shedding share and reviewing its portfolio, and AI-native cores (Maximum at Emprise) prove challengers can run phased migrations, which makes conversion capacity the bottleneck. Models can now read legacy configs and transcripts. This is not the "COBOL to Java" cliché: the product is behaviour parity between two cores, not code translation.
- **Wedge and first 10 customers:** Independent parity assurance for acquirers in announced deals where the cores differ. The vendor still does the load; Parity is the neutral verifier, so it does not need to displace anyone. Ten acquirers of $0.5-3B targets, found from deal filings that name the target's core and conversion date, at about $250k each. Add 3-4 CU mergers.
- **Path to $100M:** Core conversions are 500-1,000 a year [A, two sources above]. Parity revenue per core conversion about $350k (assurance $150k plus engine $200k, about 20-25% of an all-in cost of $1.5-2M [A]) and about $100k for each digital, card or lending conversion (about 0.8 per core conversion [A, unsourced]). At 25% of all conversions: 125-250 core x $350k = $44-88M, plus ancillary $8-16M, so $52-104M. **This needs about 25% of all conversions, which is heroic.** Three things lower the share needed: cheaper, safer conversions raise the switch rate (ABA says 19% of banks plan to convert at renewal); idea 4 adds vendor-paid volume; challengers pay per live bank. Standalone on the bank side I see $200-400M of market, so the idea only clears the bar combined with idea 4.
- **Who does it today:** FIntegrate (Birmingham; FusionLRS launched July 2024 for legacy images and data research, claims 70% cheaper data conversion, DCI partnership Aug 2025, says it has served 4,800 FIs) covers archives, not rules or parity. Cornerstone Advisors (32 conversions in 2023, 31 engagements in 2024) is project management. Plante Moran, Crowe and Wipfli sell PMO hours [M]. Fiserv, FIS and Jack Henry run their own conversion teams. 10x's AI Migrate maps onto 10x only. Mechanical Orchard does mainframe code. No independent AI parity product found.
- **Why they lose:** The vendors earn from lock-in and deconversion fees and cannot certify their own conversion. Consultants bill by the hour. FIntegrate moves data but does not recover rules. A challenger's tool is a sales aid for its own core.
- **Moat (7 Powers):** Cornered resource (the source-by-target mapping library grows with every conversion and no one else sees as many pairs) and process power (a verified parity pipeline), with counter-positioning against vendors who cannot cheapen conversion without hurting lock-in.
- **Earned secret:** Conversions do not fail on data extraction; they fail on rules nobody wrote down (how a 1998 savings product accrues interest, which custom report the CFO uses) and on the two staff who know them. The schedule is set by those people's hours.
- **Legal premise check:** Vendor licences may restrict sharing proprietary documentation and data dictionaries with third parties. The bank owns its data and configs, and interagency third-party guidance expects an exit plan [M], so I expect the bank's consent to carry it, but this needs counsel.
- **First 90 days:** Build the list of 2025-26 deals that name both cores and a conversion date (S-4 and 8-K text). Get two institutions that finished a conversion in the last year to let you replay their pre- and post-conversion extracts through the harness, and publish the defect classes found. Hire two ex-vendor conversion analysts (Fiserv's restructuring is a talent supply). Quote the first three live deals at a fixed price per account band.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 6.

## 2. Tenancy (lens: insider, a credit-union CEO with 15+ years)

- **One-liner:** Run the whole back office of several hundred small credit unions on one multi-tenant stack with shared specialists, so each keeps its own board, brand and charter and pays per member per month instead of per institution plus six salaries.
- **Customer:** CEO and board of a $50-500M credit union (about 3,466 CUs are under $500M [A]).
- **Payer and signer:** The CU's operating budget: staff plus vendor lines, about 3% of assets in total operating cost [A, not sourced this run]. The CEO signs, the board approves.
- **Problem:** The CU count is falling about 3.7% a year (4,411 to 4,250 from Q1 2025 to Q1 2026) because a sub-scale CU must carry a BSA officer, compliance, accounting, IT, lending operations, a core, a digital vendor and a card processor. Merger is the standard answer, and it ends independence. The big three price per institution and run single-tenant instances, so three CUs cannot share one.
- **Solution:** A for-profit operator, structured as a CUSO owned by anchor CUs, that runs a modern multi-tenant core and digital stack, card and lending operations, BSA monitoring, accounting and compliance with AI-run workflows and a pooled team of human specialists. Pricing is per member per month ($3-4 all-in [A]) with outcome terms on cost-to-income and exam findings.
- **Why now:** Fiserv admits lost share in smaller CUs and "pricing competition"; the CU count is dropping fast; rate stress squeezes CU margins; the FDIC proposal puts CUs inside merger competition analysis; agents make shared-service workflows cheap enough that the specialists, not the tools, are the constraint.
- **Wedge and first 10 customers:** Start with the roles small CUs cannot staff well: BSA/AML monitoring, accounting close and exam preparation, for 25 CUs under $150M, ideally those with a vacancy in the BSA or compliance seat. Add core and digital at the next contract end, using Parity to convert.
- **Path to $100M:** 200 CUs x about $500k a year ($3.5 x 12,000 members x 12) = $100M, which is 5.8% of the 3,466 CUs under $500M. The unit is credible; the pace is the risk, because CU boards decide slowly and each core exit needs a conversion. Expect 5-6 years.
- **Who does it today (hidden incumbents):** CU-owned CUSOs prove the demand: CU*SOUTH runs core platforms for member CUs, Xtend has 69-75 CU owners, Aux (55 CU owners) does accounting, compliance and analytics [S, CU Insight], and CU*Answers is a long-running CU-owned shared core [M]. Cores built for CUs (Corelation, FLEX, Sharetec, FedComp, CompuSource) sell software, not a shared back office. I found no CUSO past a few dozen owners.
- **Why they lose:** Owner-governed CUSOs move at the speed of their slowest owner and have no capital for a large build; each decision is a vote. Fiserv and Jack Henry would be selling a CU a cheaper operation that cancels their own per-institution licences, and an outsourced seat competes with their own BPO.
- **Moat:** Scale economies (one stack, one specialist pool), switching costs (the staff are gone, so leaving means rehiring), network economies (pooled fraud and credit signals across hundreds of CUs).
- **Earned secret:** CU CEOs do not resist mergers over technology. They resist losing the board, the brand and the name on the building. Nobody sells the third option: keep the charter and share everything else.
- **Legal premise check:** NCUA CUSO rules (12 CFR 712) allow service CUSOs, and NCUA's vendor-oversight authority is limited [M]; a BSA officer must be named by the board and stays accountable even if the work is shared [M]; this needs counsel before any pricing promise.
- **First 90 days:** Sign 10 design partners at cost for BSA monitoring and accounting; recruit three anchor CUs as CUSO owners; write the services agreement and the exam-readiness pack with a CU-experienced law firm; price on a per-member band.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 6.

## 3. Quorum (lens: pattern, "free tool for a profession, paid by the suppliers who want its attention", with group purchasing as phase two)

- **One-liner:** A free price-and-peer engine for bank and CU executives (what did a $2B bank on DNA actually pay and get?) built from banks' own invoices and contract abstracts, paid for by vendors who buy disclosed, qualified shortlist slots, and later by committed-volume blocks sold to challengers.
- **Customer:** CFO, COO and CEO at the 8,660 FIs, free of charge; the buyer of the product is the vendor's CRO or VP of Marketing.
- **Payer and signer:** The vendor's customer-acquisition budget (6% of first-year contract value is my assumption [A]); banks pay nothing.
- **Problem:** Prices are opaque, contracts carry confidentiality clauses, a bank cannot generate a credible competing bid on its own, and notice windows are missed. The 2013-14 BPI Network study put the premium at about 24% (biased data). Fiserv is now in retention mode, so a concession window is open.
- **Solution:** Banks upload invoices and contracts, the system abstracts them (AI), normalises per account and per transaction, and returns a benchmark, a notice-window calendar and an RFP draft. The answer engine names three vendors with a visible "sponsored" label. Phase two pools FIs into blocks (100+ institutions committing to a challenger's price card), with a disclosed admin fee paid by the vendor.
- **Why now:** Invoice abstraction became cheap, Fiserv needs retention, and AI-native challengers need cheap access to buyers.
- **Wedge and first 10 customers:** 30 FIs inside their notice window, found through the FIs themselves, get a free renewal-leverage report; the first 3 paying vendors are challengers (Nymbus, Corelation, Maximum-type) buying qualified shortlist slots.
- **Path to $100M:** Competitive core, digital and card decisions are 500-1,000 a year at an assumed $1.2M of annual value [A], so $0.6-1.2B of value; 6% is $36-72M even at 100% capture. Group-purchase admin fees at 3% on 15% of the $9B community big-three spend add about $40M. **This is the weakest idea on size; it needs most of all competitive decisions plus a big ancillary pool I could not size from sources. I judge it likely dead as a standalone company and better as the distribution layer for ideas 1, 4 and 6.**
- **Who does it today:** Golden Contract Coalition (Paladin fs, 2016; member-paid, boutique), Cornerstone Advisors and other core-selection consultants ($75-250k an RFP [A]), the ICBA and ABA endorsed-vendor programmes (royalty, no pooled volume) [M], Bank Director's FinXTech [M], and G2/Gartner for generic software [M]. No vendor-paid, invoice-based price engine found.
- **Why they lose:** Consultants and the Coalition are paid by banks per project and cannot become free. Vendors cannot publish their own prices. Trade media sell attention, not price data.
- **Moat:** Cornered resource (a contract and price database no one else can assemble) and network economies (more banks, better benchmarks, more vendors), weakened by easy copying once the data exists.
- **Earned secret:** The leverage is not the price list. It is a credible alternative bid and the notice date. A single bank cannot produce either; pooled and anonymised data can.
- **Legal premise check:** Contract confidentiality clauses may bar sharing pricing, so the product must use only data the bank itself holds and show aggregates. The vendor-pays model touches the Bank Bribery Act (18 USC 215) if any value reaches a bank employee or agent [M]. Fees must go to the platform only, be disclosed, and never flow to the bank's staff. Needs counsel.
- **First 90 days:** Build the invoice-abstraction pipeline on 20 real bank invoices (ask the Coalition's alumni and your own network); publish a free Fiserv-renewal leverage guide; sell three shortlist slots to challengers.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 4.

## 4. Sunset (lens: pattern, outcome-priced replacement of a high-volume labour function)

- **One-liner:** Migration as a service for the incumbents themselves: a fixed price per account moved from a platform the vendor wants to retire onto its strategic platform, with a no-churn guarantee, so Fiserv, FIS or Jack Henry can finally prune the second and third cores they carry.
- **Customer:** The president of a vendor's financial-institutions business, or its head of client delivery.
- **Payer and signer:** The vendor's cost of sales for implementation and conversions (and a restructuring reserve), signed by the divisional COO. Price per account moved ($15-40 an account [A]).
- **Problem:** Vendors carry many overlapping cores for decades because a forced migration is a free RFP for the customer. A bank told to move off its platform treats it as the moment to shop. Fiserv now says "we need to simplify", is "reviewing the mix of businesses", and has sold or spun off ATMs, student-loan servicing and may sell a debit network.
- **Solution:** The same engine as Parity, sold on the vendor's side: behaviour recovery, mapping, parity proofs, plus Day One (idea 6) so customers do not re-enrol. Contracts are per account moved, with a guarantee that parity differences are fixed at our cost and a retention clause shared with the vendor.
- **Why now:** Fiserv's new CEO and a -1% to flat guide make a platform rationalisation more likely than at any point in years. That is an inference: **I found no announced core sunset**, and the American Banker headline says only that product changes may follow [S]. Watch end-of-life notices.
- **Wedge and first 10 customers:** Start with whichever vendor announces a retirement; failing that, with the small-CU platforms where Fiserv admits losses. First customer is one vendor, so concentration is the built-in risk; the first ten are ten platform cohorts at that vendor.
- **Path to $100M:** If Fiserv retires platforms covering 30% of its roughly 3,000 bank and CU clients [A: 42% of 4,450 banks plus 1,155 CUs], that is about 900 FIs x $350k of vendor-borne conversion cost = $315M over four years, about $80M a year. FIS and Jack Henry rationalisations and Maximum/10x onboarding add the rest. **It rests on vendor decisions I could not confirm, and on one buyer; I rate probability of the core scenario at roughly one in three.**
- **Who does it today:** The vendors' own conversion teams plus systems integrators (Cognizant, Tata, Infosys and similar [M]); FIntegrate on archives; Cornerstone as PMO. No one sells outcome-priced, parity-proven migration to vendors.
- **Why they lose:** The vendors' in-house teams are paid per project and their incentive is the customer's lock-in; integrators bill time. An outside party with a churn guarantee carries risk the vendor cannot price internally.
- **Moat:** Cornered resource (the mapping library) and switching costs (once a vendor routes its retirements through the pipeline it is hard to unwind).
- **Earned secret:** Vendors do not avoid sunsets because they love old code. They avoid them because customers leave when forced to move. Remove the pain and the sunset becomes affordable.
- **Legal premise check:** Vendor licence terms and client consents for moving data to the vendor's other platform are already inside the vendor's own contracts, so the legal risk is low; the real check is customer-concentration and revenue-recognition terms.
- **First 90 days:** Build the EOL watchlist (Fiserv, FIS, Jack Henry release notes and customer forums); pitch the head of delivery at each vendor with a proof run on a retired-platform client; offer the first migration at cost.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 5.

## 5. Books of Record (lens: dilemma, legacy systems plus business-model conflict; voice of a community-bank COO/CIO)

- **One-liner:** A vendor-neutral, always-on copy of the bank's ledger and customer records, built from the core's own journals and owned by the bank. First it gives 24/7 balance truth and stand-in processing for instant payments; then it feeds data and AI; then it becomes the control that lets any new core take accounts in cohorts.
- **Customer:** COO or CIO of a $1-10B bank or a $1B+ CU. About 748 CUs are over $500M [S]; I put banks at $1-10B at 700-800 [A, unsourced].
- **Payer and signer:** The payments-modernisation, resilience and data-platform budget, $80-250k a year [A], signed by the COO or CIO. The board reads it as the exit plan.
- **Problem:** The bank does not hold its own books. The core is a nightly batch system; every examiner request and every analytics project starts with a vendor extract; 24/7 payments need a real-time balance, hold and sanctions check inside seconds (a Federal Reserve comment letter flags real-time OFAC and AML screening inside FedNow's five-second window as a major challenge for smaller institutions [S]); a vendor outage leaves the bank blind; and leaving means an 18-month data project. Data-processing costs have doubled at Webster Five in five years and 47% of banks say they are dissatisfied with their core [S].
- **Solution:** Event or journal adapters per core (Fiserv Premier, DNA and Portico; FIS Horizon and IBS; Jack Henry SilverLake and Symitar; CSI NuPoint [M, platform names]) feed a canonical, immutable double-entry ledger. On top: a real-time balance and hold API; stand-in authorisation for FedNow, RTP and cards while the core is in batch or down; continuous reconciliation; examiner-ready reports; a warehouse for AI; a one-click exit-plan export. Later, dual-run routing so a new core (Maximum, 10x, Thought Machine, Nymbus) can take cohorts of accounts with the mirror as the control.
- **Why now:** FedNow and RTP make 24/7 mandatory for anyone who joins; Fiserv's distress makes boards ask about vendor-failure plans; AI-native cores (Maximum at Emprise, "phased, with existing systems running in parallel") need a safe bridge; and the interagency third-party guidance of June 2023 expects exit planning [M].
- **Wedge and first 10 customers:** Real-time balance and stand-in for FedNow/RTP at ten banks whose cores post in batch, at about $100k. A second door is the challengers themselves: Maximum, 10x and Nymbus each need a parallel-run bridge to close their next ten banks, and can pay per live bank.
- **Path to $100M:** Land 400 FIs at $150k = $60M, then move 40 of them to ledger-of-record at about $1M = $40M, over six to seven years. That is about 27% of roughly 1,500 target FIs, which is heroic. The real route is the expansion: once the mirror is the book of record the price jumps toward core level ($1-3M for a $2B bank [A]), so about 70 banks gets to $100M.
- **Who does it today:** Fiserv's Finxact (an adjacent next-generation core, tied to Fiserv); Thought Machine, 10x and Mambu (cores with coexistence modes); Volante (raised $66M; payments hub for mid-tier banks; date unconfirmed) [S]; Finzly and ACI (payments hubs) [M]; Jack Henry's FedNow service [M]; Sheltered Harbor-style data vaults [M]. Two searches found no neutral mirror for community banks.
- **Why they lose:** A batch core cannot be sold as optional, and vendors charge for extracts. A neutral mirror weakens lock-in, so no incumbent will build it. Finxact serves Fiserv's strategy. Challenger cores want to replace, not mirror. Payments hubs do not own the ledger.
- **Moat:** Switching costs (it becomes the second and then the first book of record), counter-positioning against vendors, and process power (the adapters accumulate per core version).
- **Earned secret:** The board and the examiner believe the bank has its books. In fact every number comes from a vendor extract, and the real reconciliation runs in Excel.
- **Legal premise check:** The bank must hold a contractual right to the core's journals or events in near real time. Vendors may restrict or price this, and I do not know which cores expose event streams at all (open question, first thing to test). Stand-in authorisation must follow network rules, OFAC and FedNow participation terms.
- **First 90 days:** Map journal and event access on Premier, DNA, SilverLake, Symitar and one more; get two banks to sign data-access letters; build the canonical ledger with 12 months of history for one bank; run a Friday-5pm demo of balance truth against the core.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 7.

## 6. Day One (lens: dilemma, because no one is paid to move the customer's plumbing)

- **One-liner:** Move each customer's financial graph (bill-pay payees and schedules, direct deposits, card-on-file tokens, external-account links, alerts, Zelle and P2P enrolment) before conversion day, so there is no re-enrolment cliff and first-month attrition collapses.
- **Customer:** The acquirer's head of retail banking or customer experience (M&A), and the switching bank's COO. Second market: fintechs and neobanks that want primary accounts.
- **Payer and signer:** The merger-integration budget or a deposit-retention reserve. Value: at a 4% marginal funding cost [A], keeping 6 points of deposits on a $1.5B target ($90M) is worth about $3.6M a year. Price $250-400k a deal; signed by the head of retail with the COO.
- **Problem:** Average deposit attrition after conversion is 11.1%, worst quartile about 17%, best under 5% (Curinos); two-thirds of leavers go in month one (Deloitte). Typical pain: every customer must re-enrol for online banking, Bill Pay and Zelle are down for the weekend, 18,750 calls in two weeks at one bank [S]. The old vendor has no reason to help customers leave, the new vendor cannot see the old credentials until cutover, and the bank sits between.
- **Solution:** With customer consent, capture the graph before cutover (through permissioned aggregator access, payroll connections, and the card networks' account-updater programmes [M]); pre-provision the new app so day-one login works; carry payees and schedules across; flag at-risk customers (low balance, high fee, inactive) for targeted outreach in week one.
- **Why now:** Deposits are expensive (the reported 5.35% ten-year yield, unverified), approvals are getting faster, and AI-native cores want a clean cutover.
- **Wedge and first 10 customers:** Sold with Parity into deals where the target's digital vendor changes. Ten acquirers from filings, at one pilot cohort each.
- **Path to $100M:** Events needing graph migration: about 190 bank deals plus about 150 CU mergers (the CU count fell by 161 in the year to Q1 2026, which includes liquidations [S/A]) = 340, plus 400-800 voluntary core switches and about 300 digital-only conversions [A] = roughly 1,000-1,400 a year. At $250k a deal, 25% share = 250-350 events = $62-88M. A second leg, the same rail used for ordinary primary-account switching at $12 a switch for 3M switches, adds $36M. So $100M needs about 25% of conversion events plus the second leg. **That is heroic, and the standalone M&A-only version is $20-40M.**
- **Who does it today:** The FI's own communications team plus the new vendor's conversion staff (Fiserv, Jack Henry, Alkami, Q2 [M]); Visa and Mastercard account-updater programmes for card-on-file (partial) [M]; payroll-connectivity firms (Pinwheel, Atomic, Argyle [M]) for direct-deposit switching, sold mainly to neobanks; Curinos for benchmarks and advisory. No one packages the whole graph for conversions.
- **Why they lose:** The old vendor is leaving, the new vendor starts at cutover, consultants sell advice, and payroll-switch firms do one leg.
- **Moat:** Cornered resource (a payee, biller and token map that grows with every conversion) and process power; it also benefits from network effects as payroll and biller connections accumulate.
- **Earned secret:** Attrition is not a communications problem. It is the dozen invisible connections (payees, direct deposit, tokens) that silently break. The causal story is my inference; the size comes from Curinos and Deloitte.
- **Legal premise check:** Credential handling and consent under GLBA. With 1033 not in force there is no right to pull a customer's data from the old bank, so it must run on the bank's own authority plus customer permission. Direct-deposit changes need the employee's authorisation. Needs counsel.
- **First 90 days:** Take two recent conversions and decompose attrition (accounts that lost payees or deposits versus the rest) from the banks' own data; build payee and token capture on one cooperating bank's customers; pilot at 60 days before cutover on an announced deal.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 5.

## 7. Switchboard (lens: shock, the Fiserv debit-network sale talks; open-book, network-neutral card processing)

- **One-liner:** Open-book, cost-plus debit and card processing for banks and CUs under $10B, with pre-certified multi-network routing so no community institution depends on one network owner, and a cooperative-equity layer for the FIs that want to own the plumbing.
- **Customer:** The head of cards or payments, and the COO or CFO, at a bank or CU under $10B; CU CEOs for smaller shops.
- **Payer and signer:** The card-processing line, paid out of interchange (the exempt non-prepaid pool is $14.87B a year [S]). The COO or CFO signs.
- **Shock:** In July 2026 several large banks held preliminary talks to buy one of Fiserv's PIN-debit networks (STAR or Accel), the CEO says a sale is being considered, Cantor expects Accel to be shed, and Raymond James expects regulatory scrutiny [S]. Community FIs enable these networks on their cards. If the buyer is a megabank, the network owner becomes a competitor of its users.
- **Problem:** Processing is bundled into the core contract, pass-through fees and network incentives are opaque, and the processor often owns or is paid by a network. The Fed shows network fees rising to $12.95B ($0.129 a transaction) while the exempt average interchange is flat at $0.52 [S], so the squeeze falls on the issuer and the processor. A community FI cannot see what its card programme really earns.
- **Solution:** (1) A free two-week "debit P&L audit" from the FI's own statements: unit cost, network fees, incentives, routing. (2) A multi-network contingency pack so the FI can enable alternates (Pulse, NYCE, Visa Interlink and Plus, Mastercard Maestro and Cirrus, SHAZAM, CO-OP, Culiance [M, network names]) if an owner changes. (3) Open-book processing at disclosed cost plus a fixed fee. (4) Optional: a cooperative route for FIs that want to take equity in a routing layer.
- **Why now:** The talks are live, Fiserv is pruning, and processing contracts that come up for renewal in the next 24 months will be signed against that uncertainty.
- **Wedge and first 10 customers:** The free audit sold to 30 FIs whose card contracts are up for renewal; ten convert to processing for their next contract.
- **Path to $100M:** 29.84B exempt non-prepaid transactions a year [S]. At $0.035 of net processing and routing margin a transaction [A], $100M is 2.86B transactions, 9.6% of the exempt pool; adding a small per-card fee brings it to about 6-8%. If the cooperative layer takes an Accel-sized routing business instead (single-message networks carried 28.84B transactions across all issuers [S]; at a 5-10% share and $0.03-0.05 net a transaction that is $43-145M [A, share unverified]), the path is shorter but needs a seller and capital. **About 8% share is heroic but not absurd.**
- **Who does it today:** Fiserv (owns STAR and Accel), FIS (NYCE) and Jack Henry [M], Elan and CO-OP and SHAZAM (networks and processors) [M], Global Payments/TSYS issuer processing [M]; fintech-grade processors (Marqeta, Lithic, Highnote, Galileo, i2c, Thredd [M]) serve fintechs, not community FIs.
- **Why they lose:** Each incumbent is conflicted: it owns or earns from a network, bundles processing into the core, and profits from opacity. Fintech-grade processors lack the core integrations, ATM and PIN networks, dispute workflows and the sales motion for FIs. The potential megabank owner competes with the FIs it would serve.
- **Moat:** Counter-positioning (open book), scale economies in processing, and cooperative equity that binds owner-FIs.
- **Earned secret:** A network's real asset is not its software. It is the set of issuers that have enabled it on their cards. Issuers choose which networks are enabled, so together they hold the option.
- **Legal premise check:** Regulation II requires at least two unaffiliated networks for each authentication type and bars issuer restrictions on routing [M]. A joint decision by FIs to disable a network could look like a group boycott, so each FI must decide for itself and the cooperative must be a product, not an agreement. Raymond James already expects regulatory review of the sale itself [S].
- **First 90 days:** Offer the audit to 30 FIs; map which networks each is enabled for; build the alternate-routing playbook per processor; open talks with CO-OP, SHAZAM and Culiance as partners; track the sale news weekly.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 6.

## 8. Bureau (lens: shock, Fiserv's pruning and a wave of retiring owners; pattern: buy the services firm)

- **One-liner:** Buy the independent bank and CU processors, and the pieces Fiserv wants to shed, whose 7-10-year contracts and high retention are the asset; consolidate them on one AI-run stack; run them at the incumbents' margin.
- **Customer:** The processors' owners (sellers), and the 100-300 FI clients each serves.
- **Payer and signer:** The FIs already pay; the buyer-side signer is the sponsor's investment committee, with a lender. Sellers are founders, families or small boards.
- **Problem and insight:** The big three earn high margins from lock-in (Fiserv's financial-solutions segment is $9.7B of revenue). Independents lack scale and, I assume, run lower margins (10-20% [A]). The usual strategic buyers are distracted (Fiserv and FIS) or constrained. Owners are ageing. And Fiserv is now selling: the ATM and cash businesses went into a JV with Bridgeport Partners, student-loan servicing went to Infinite Computer Solutions, and "other deals could follow" [S].
- **Solution:** Acquire four to six processors over three years. Migrate each to a single multi-tenant stack using the idea 1 engine. Automate support and regulatory updates, cross-sell digital banking, cards (idea 7) and fraud tools, and use Fiserv carve-outs as a supply of assets.
- **Why now:** Supply of sellers is rising, and AI lowers cost-to-serve. Against it: if the 10-year yield really is 5.35% [S, unverified], leverage is expensive.
- **Wedge and first targets:** From the Kansas City Fed data: CSI (7% of banks), COCC (3%), Finastra's US business (3%); among CUs FedComp (about 10% in 2020), CompuSource, FLEX and Sharetec [S]; DCI (a core processor for community banks, FIntegrate's partner) [S]. Begin with the smallest (under $30M revenue) where an owner wants out.
- **Path to $100M:** Independents' pool: about 13% of 4,450 banks (580 x $0.7M) plus about 20% of 4,214 CUs (840 x $0.3M) = about $0.4B + $0.25B = $0.65B [A: shares from Kansas City Fed and Credit Union Times, revenue per FI assumed]. $100M of revenue is about 15% of that pool: four to six acquisitions of $15-40M revenue each. At 2-3x revenue the equity and debt needed is $200-300M [A]. **Share is heroic, but it is bought rather than won, so the constraint is capital and integration skill, not a model lead.**
- **Who does it today:** PE-owned CSI (taken private in 2022 [M]); Candescent (ex-NCR digital banking, Veritas [M]); Bridgeport Partners (took the MoneyPass JV) and Infinite Computer Solutions (took student-loan servicing) [S]; Jack Henry and Fiserv as bolt-on buyers; software consolidators in the Constellation style [M].
- **Why they lose:** PE owners usually buy for quick margin through price rises and cost cuts, which triggers the churn that contract lock-in holds back. Strategics are distracted and face antitrust and exam scrutiny. Co-operative owners cannot raise acquisition capital.
- **Moat:** Scale economies (shared data centre, support and regulatory-update engineering), switching costs (the contracts), and a cornered resource (deal flow from retiring founders and Fiserv carve-outs).
- **Earned secret:** The lock-in that makes Fiserv rich also sits inside a small processor's contracts, which is why these businesses are financeable. They are cheap because they are sub-scale, not because the revenue is weak.
- **Legal premise check:** Change-of-control and assignment consents in client contracts; client banks' third-party due diligence on a new owner; regulators' examination of technology service providers [M]. None looks blocking, but the consent mechanics decide timing and price.
- **First 90 days:** Build the target list from the Kansas City Fed and ABA data; approach five owners with under $40M of revenue; line up a sponsor and a lender; draft the 100-day consolidation plan on the Parity engine.
- **Outside-the-box score:** 5.
