# P129: US enterprises' spend on offshore-delivered IT services (~$100B a year)

Status: DRAFT v1 (ideas 1-4 of 8 written; ideas 5-8 follow). Backups of every version live in `ideas/_backups/`.

Date of research: 2026-10-18. Searches run: ~25 (WebSearch standard mode). Tags used below: **[src]** = found in a source listed at the bottom; **[my estimate]** = my arithmetic or assumption; **[bg]** = background knowledge, not verified this session; **[vendor claim]** = a seller's own number.

## Read this first: three facts that change how the pool's shock should be used

1. **The $100,000 H-1B fee is not a safe premise.** A federal court in Massachusetts vacated it on 2026-06-08, the First Circuit refused to stay that on 2026-07-24, the proclamation's 12-month term ran to 2026-09-20 (reports of a renewal and of a 2026-09-30 N.D. Cal. order vacating the implementing policies are single-sourced), and a D.D.C. court went the other way [src: Fragomen, Littler, Barnes & Thornburg, EY alerts]. The behavioral damage is already done (FY2027 lottery entries fell 91% at Infosys, 95% at TCS, and Cognizant filed none [src: Bloomberg Law]), but no idea below depends on the fee surviving.
2. **The durable policy shock is tax, not visas.** OBBBA's new Section 174A lets US companies expense domestic software R&E immediately, but foreign research still amortizes over 15 years, and software development counts as R&E [src: Rehmann, Surgent, beancount.io; CPA blogs, not IRS text]. My arithmetic: for a 21% taxpayer discounting at 8%, the lost timing value is about 8.5% of every offshore development dollar (7.0% at 6%, 9.8% at 10%) [my estimate]. Foreign work also cannot earn the Section 41 credit [bg]. The HIRE Act (25% excise on foreign outsourcing) is stalled with no cosponsors and a 0% GovTrack forecast [src], so treat it as an option, not a plan.
3. **The money is being re-priced, not removed.** About 15-20% of outsourcing contracts used to be renegotiated each year; Cognizant's CFO expected about 25% [src: HFS, InformationWeek]. Persistent's CEO says clients want the same work for 25-30% less [src]. TCS says about 80% of its business-services contracts are outcome-based [src: Reuters via HFS]. Cognizant's Daimler Truck deal splits AI savings [src]. Nobody can yet measure those savings neutrally. Most ideas below sit on that gap.

Four candidate ideas were killed while generating this set (so nobody re-does the work):
- **Captive/GCC-as-a-service for the US mid-market.** Already happening without a new entrant: roughly 480 mid-market firms stood up about 680 Indian GCCs in FY2025 [src: ANSR blog, promotional], ANSR sells build-operate-transfer, and a GCC gets the same 15-year Section 174A treatment as a vendor. L-1 transfers do not rescue it (qualifying-relationship and prior-employment rules).
- **Mainframe "peak-peeling" for IBM licence savings.** IBM's Tailored Fit Pricing removes the rolling-four-hour-average peak for many customers, and the only savings evidence (10-25%) comes from licence advisers selling the service [src: Redress Compliance, BMC].
- **Distressed roll-up of H-1B-dependent consultancies.** I found no reporting of distress at small firms [src search]; the fee is vacated; and "AI roll-up of a legacy services firm" is on the cliche list.
- **A software-labor price index sold as a standalone business.** Even with generous assumptions the licence pool is a few hundred million dollars and $100M needs 20-30% of it. Its data is folded into idea 1 as a module.

## IDEA 1 (insider): The Meter

**One-liner:** A vendor-neutral system, owned by the client, that reads the logs the client already holds (logins, tickets, commits, AI-gateway calls, invoices) to prove what offshore engineers actually delivered, clause by clause, and is paid mostly out of the savings it proves.

**Persona:** Client-side vendor management office (VMO) lead, 15+ years, who has sat across from TCS, Infosys and Cognizant delivery heads at 40 renewals.

**Customer:** US enterprises spending $20M+ a year on outsourced application, testing and infrastructure services (about 1,500 firms, roughly $70B of the $100B pool [my estimate]); start with banks, insurers and payers that have a contract renewing within 12 months.

**Payer and signer:** Chief Procurement Officer or VP IT Sourcing signs. CIO and CISO approve read-only telemetry access. Budget lines: (a) a contingent fee of 15-25% of verified recoveries and price reductions, which needs no new budget; (b) a $150-400k a year subscription from the vendor-governance line (ISG just signed a governance contract worth up to $17M with one manufacturer [src], so the line exists).

**Problem:** The baseline in nearly every master services agreement is "FTEs" or "resource units", not work. The vendor holds the only evidence of how much was done by people versus AI. The advisers who benchmark (ISG at about $250M a year of revenue, Gartner, Everest) are paid when a deal is signed and also sell to vendors [src: ISG Q1/Q2 2026 results]. So CIOs negotiate the biggest repricing in the industry's history blind. Service-level credits are a second blind spot: Forrester notes providers often never pay credits because earn-back clauses cancel them [src].

**Solution:** Read-only connectors to systems the client owns where vendor staff work: identity provider, VPN and virtual-desktop logs (who actually logged in), Git and CI (pull requests), Jira and ServiceNow (ticket volume, ageing, reopen and repeat rates), the client's own LLM gateway (share of work AI-assisted), and the accounts-payable and statement-of-work data. Outputs: (1) invoice-to-activity reconciliation (billed FTEs versus active named people, shared resources billed twice); (2) unit cost per ticket, pull request and change request with an AI-adjusted should-cost; (3) a clause engine (productivity glide paths, benchmarking rights, escalator caps, service-credit earn-backs, AI pass-through formulas); (4) a renegotiation or rebid pack with evidence; (5) a signed "AI-gain ledger" that both sides accept, which turns the Meter into the neutral calculation agent for gain-share deals. Forward-deployed staff are ex-vendor delivery heads (the pool of mid and senior people is growing: the top five Indian firms' headcount fell by 7,389 in FY26 after adding 12,718 in FY25 [src: IANS], TCS cut about 12,000 mostly mid and senior roles [src]).

**Why now:** the 25%-a-year renegotiation rate; AI clauses in new deals; many client AI policies ban vendor GenAI use without consent and clients cannot see compliance [src: Morgan Lewis]; the Section 174A location-of-work question gives a VP of Tax a reason to want location-attested activity logs too (a second signer, not a second product).

**Wedge and first 10 customers:** A 90-day "ghost-FTE and unexercised-rights" audit on one contract of $30M+ a year that renews within 12 months, sold on contingency. Ten Fortune-1000 banks, insurers and payers whose CPO has already hired an adviser for a rebid, reached through ex-ISG/Everest consultants and PE operating partners.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up):** About 1,500 target enterprises at roughly $47M average offshore spend [my estimate: $70B / 1,500]. Verified savings plus recoveries of 4-6% a year (floor evidence: an outsourcing consultant says a line-item review yields at least 1% of contract value for every unchecked year [src: CIO.com/Strichman]; clients are asking for 25-30% cuts [src]; BlueOptima markets "up to 20% of software budgets" [vendor claim]). At 4% savings and a 20% fee plus a $200k subscription, revenue per client is about $573k, so 174 clients (11.6% of the 1,500). At 6% and 25%, it is about $900k, so 111 clients (7.4%). No step needs more than 12% share.

**Who does it today:** Advisers: ISG, Gartner, Everest, HFS (human, per-deal). Software that measures vendor output: BlueOptima (code-effort benchmarking since about 2009; a directory lists about 109 staff [src, unverified]). Internal-team analytics that do not target vendor proof: Jellyfish, Faros, DX (Atlassian is buying DX for about $1B [src]). Generic recovery audit: PRGX (has an "IT contract labor" case study) and Vaudit [src]. Contingent-labor systems: SAP Fieldglass, Beeline [bg]. Secure third-party access: Island ($6.4B valuation, about $200M ARR [src]). In-house VMOs with spreadsheets.

**Why they lose:** Advisers cannot sell continuous measurement without cutting their own deal-fee model. Engineering analytics sell to engineering leaders about their own teams, not to procurement with clause logic. BlueOptima has measured code for 15 years without becoming a procurement standard because the buyer never had a forcing event; renegotiation plus AI clauses now supply one. Vendors cannot audit themselves.

**Moat (7 Powers):** Counter-positioning against advisers and vendors whose revenue depends on opacity; a cornered resource in the cross-client database of ticket-level and pull-request-level unit prices that vendors never publish; switching costs once the Meter is the named calculation agent inside the contract.

**Earned secret:** The client owns the system of record where vendor staff work, while the vendor owns the invoice, and nobody joins the two. Most master agreements carry a 3-5% annual productivity glide path that has never been audited because the baseline was frozen in FTEs, not work units. [bg, from sourcing practice; the first deliverable tests it]

**First 90 days:** Sign three design partners with renewals inside 12 months. Build four connectors (Entra/Okta plus VPN logs, Jira/ServiceNow, GitHub/GitLab/Azure DevOps, AP and statement-of-work exports). Run one ghost-FTE reconciliation and one "contract rights not exercised" report. Hire two ex-vendor delivery heads. Legal checks: audit and telemetry rights in the master agreement, India DPDP and employee-monitoring consent, and a vendor flow-down notice.

**Outside-the-box score:** 6

## IDEA 2 (insider): Penny-Exact

**One-liner:** An independent referee that proves, to the penny, that an AI-translated mainframe or legacy system behaves exactly like the old one, and issues the evidence an auditor, a regulator and a board will accept.

**Persona:** Mainframe/COBOL architect at a bank or insurer, 15+ years, who has been through two failed cutovers.

**Customer:** Banks, insurers, payers and government agencies that run material COBOL, PL/I, DB2 and batch estates and start a modernization program within 12 months; then any enterprise running an AI-assisted migration (Oracle Forms, PL/SQL to Postgres, Informatica, Teradata, Java and .NET upgrades, SAP custom code).

**Payer and signer:** The program's CIO or COO signs; the Chief Audit Executive (and, in banks, the Chief Risk Officer) must accept the evidence. Budget line: the testing and acceptance share of the modernization program (a modernization vendor models UAT sign-off, parallel running, regulatory approval and business acceptance as the roughly 62% of effort that AI leaves largely untouched [src: Mechanical Orchard, vendor model]). Modernization vendors are a second payer, because acceptance gates their final invoice.

**Problem:** Translation is becoming free. AWS Transform converts COBOL to Java in months [src]; Anthropic's 2026-02-23 post claimed Claude Code can automate COBOL analysis and knocked IBM down about 13% that day (its worst day since 2000), even as IBM Z revenue grew 48% in the prior quarter [src]. The cost that remains is proving the new system matches the old on 30 years of edge cases: rounding, packed decimals, EBCDIC collation, date windowing, sort stability, abend and restart behavior, batch ordering. Whoever sells the translation also sells its own proof.

**Solution:** Read-only taps on production inputs and outputs (batch files, DB2 snapshots, CICS and MQ traces) produce golden datasets. Old and new systems run in shadow on the same inputs. An AI classifier sorts differences into expected (intended change) and defects. The product is an Equivalence Certificate per application and cutover wave, signed by the referee and backed by the stored golden record, which stays as the long-term test oracle after the vendor leaves. Priced as a fixed fee per wave, a platform licence, and a retained monitoring fee for the first 12 months after cutover.

**Why now:** The translation shock just happened (May 2025 AWS launch; Feb 2026 Anthropic post). Boards that watched IBM fall are asking CIOs for exit cases, and the sign-off step now sits on the critical path. [src for dates]

**Wedge and first 10 customers:** Ten insurers and regional banks ($5-50B assets or premiums) with COBOL batch suites, a modernization starting inside 12 months, and no single systems integrator locked in. Second channel: AWS Transform and other translation partners that want a neutral acceptance layer to unlock their final milestone payment.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up):** (a) Mainframe: about 35 new programs a year in scope at roughly $1.2M of certification and monitoring each (12-15% of a roughly $9M testing-and-acceptance line in a $25M program) is about $42M [my estimate: program count assumes about 5% a year of several hundred mid and large mainframe owners]. (b) Other AI-assisted migrations: about 150 programs a year at roughly $400k each is about $60M [my estimate]. Total about $100M at roughly 185 programs a year. This is borderline: it needs about 10-15% of AI-assisted migration programs in mid and large enterprises, and I could not source the program count.

**Who does it today:** Mechanical Orchard (Series A $24M; its Imogen platform captures production behavior and claims continuous equivalence, but inside its own migration) [src]; AWS Transform; IBM (watsonx tools) and the large integrators (Accenture, TCS, Infosys, Cognizant), each validating its own work; Hypercubic ($5.3M seed, August 2026) and Kodesage ($6.6M seed) on understanding and translating legacy code [src, pool file]; Datafold for SQL and warehouse migrations [src]; Tricentis and Parasoft for test automation [bg]; Big 4 independent verification and validation on large government programs [bg].

**Why they lose:** Translators grade their own homework, and an audit committee knows it. Test tools generate tests but do not issue accepted evidence. Integrators bill time and materials for the acceptance phase and have no reason to shorten it.

**Moat:** A neutral position (counter-positioning against every translator) that works like a rating agency or auditor, which only becomes valuable once examiners and Big 4 audit partners accept the format (cornered resource); a library of "gotcha" test packs per language and platform; process power from repeated cutovers.

**Earned secret:** Every cutover that fails, fails on a behavior nobody wrote down (a sort tie-break, a rounding rule applied only in month-end batch, a restart that reruns a half-posted file). Production data, not specifications, is the only complete spec, and it is available only from the system being replaced. [bg]

**First 90 days:** Build a replay harness around an emulator for z/OS batch and a DB2 snapshot differ. Partner with one integrator running AWS Transform and one mid-size insurer; run an equivalence report on one batch suite; show the certificate format to the insurer's internal auditor and a Big 4 audit partner and ask what would make it acceptable. Legal check: I found no US rule that mandates independent equivalence certification, so demand is for defensible evidence, not a mandate; keep independence rules clean (never paid by the translator for the same program).

**Outside-the-box score:** 6

## IDEA 3 (pattern): The Middle

**One-liner:** A private-equity sponsor signs one framework agreement, and every portfolio company gets fixed-unit-price, AI-native application maintenance and development from a shared pod, below the minimum ticket of the big Indian firms and clean to carve out at exit.

**Pattern transferred:** Concentrated-operator vertical platform (EliseAI): win a few very large operators who control thousands of repeated sites, then widen. Here the "operators" are PE sponsors and the "sites" are portfolio companies.

**Customer:** PE-owned US companies with $50M-1B of revenue. S&P Global counts 82,927 US private companies owned by PE as of August 2025 [src]; I assume about 8,000 fall in this size band [my estimate].

**Payer and signer:** The sponsor's operating partner or head of portfolio operations signs the framework (value-creation-plan cost take-out is the budget line); each portfolio CIO or CFO issues work orders.

**Problem:** A $200M-revenue portfolio company spends roughly 3-5% of revenue on IT [my estimate], a fifth of it on offshore-deliverable maintenance and development, bought from tier-2 vendors or local boutiques one company at a time with no leverage and no standard security pack. The majors will not take sub-$5M tickets [pool file]. Few sponsors have built shared outsourcing, historically because it complicates exits [src: Private Funds CFO, 2012]. Now more portfolio teams are warming to low-touch shared services [src: AlixPartners], and PE increasingly centralizes vendor management [src: Zinnov].

**Solution:** A shared delivery utility with unit prices ("per ticket resolved", "per accepted story", "per application-year"), 40% below current tier-2 spend on the common stacks (Salesforce, NetSuite, Dynamics, Workday, .NET and Java), a pre-approved security and data-processing pack at sponsor level (companies onboard in 30 days), 90-day exit rights, and a documented, portable runbook so a buyer at exit receives a clean data room. Delivery: AI-native pods in India with a US account lead. Everything runs inside the Meter's measurement.

**Why now:** Sponsors are under cost-take-out pressure with AI deflation visible (clients are asking for 25-30% cuts [src]) and the H-1B/onsite model is unreliable. Delivery agents (Devin-class tools) are now good enough to run common-stack maintenance with human oversight; Infosys itself announced a Devin-based "hybrid pod" model in January 2026 [src].

**Wedge and first 10 customers:** One mid-market sponsor with 25+ companies on overlapping stacks; first 10 customers are ten of its portfolio companies. Sponsor-level pilots begin with the cheapest, most repeated work (Salesforce and NetSuite administration and enhancements, tier-2 application support).

**Path to $100M (bottom-up):** About 8,000 companies at roughly $1.2M of offshore-deliverable spend each is a pool of about $9.6B [my estimate]. $100M of revenue is 1.0% of that pool, or about 83 companies, for example six sponsors with 14 companies each. The revenue is delivery revenue (principal), not a fee on spend.

**Who does it today:** Tier-2 Indian and US vendors and boutiques (Endava, Perficient, Slalom, Globant, EPAM at the upper end [bg]); PE-focused IT support firms [src: itpartners.com]; EY and Zinnov selling shared-service platforms as consulting, and EY claims 50-60% five-year savings from labor arbitrage plus 10-15% from centralization and 12-18% from automation [vendor claim]; AI-native entrants focused on single platforms (Tessera Labs, $60M Series A, SAP first [src]; AppFactor, $4M seed, autonomous maintenance [src]).

**Why they lose:** Tier-2 vendors have no sponsor-level standard and no incentive to make exits easy. Consultancies sell a design, not the running service. AI-native startups sell one platform and one buyer at a time with long enterprise sales cycles; the sponsor channel compresses that.

**Moat:** Scale economies on a shared stack of runbooks and prompts across portfolios; switching costs inside each company; an exclusive framework with the sponsor (cornered distribution).

**Earned secret:** A sponsor's operating partner can mandate a vendor across the whole portfolio within the first 100 days after a close, and reports cost take-outs to investors; what stops them today is the fear of a tangled carve-out. Sell clean carve-out, not savings. [bg]

**First 90 days:** Sign one sponsor and three pilot companies. Stand up a 40-person pod. Get SOC 2 Type 1. Publish the unit price card and the exit-transfer runbook. Legal check: portfolio-company data-protection agreements, and ensure each company contracts separately so the sponsor takes no liability.

**Outside-the-box score:** 6

## IDEA 4 (pattern): Clawback

**One-liner:** A recovery-audit factory for IT-services contracts: find money that vendors owe under rate cards, escalator caps, volume tiers, service-credit clauses and AI pass-through terms that nobody enforces, take 20-30% of what comes back, and advance cash against signed credit memos.

**Pattern transferred:** Windfall claims-recovery rail (tariff refunds, freight and telecom audit, healthcare payment integrity). The reusable asset is a claims-processing factory plus a financing partner.

**Customer:** The same 1,500 enterprises as the Meter, plus mid-market firms with $2-20M contracts that cannot afford a measurement project.

**Payer and signer:** No budget line is needed: the fee is contingent on recoveries, so the CFO or Controller signs (the CPO co-signs). That removes the "no budget" failure from round 1.

**Problem:** Contracts leak after signature. An outsourcing consultant says a line-item review produces at least 1% of contract value per unchecked year, and that about 5% of line items cannot be explained at all [src: CIO.com]. A recovery vendor claims 3-7% [vendor claim]. Forrester reports that earn-back clauses mean many providers never pay service credits [src]. Contingent recovery audit is a proven model: fees run 13.5-40% of recoveries (a 20% flat bid from PRG-Schultz in a 2004 state procurement) [src].

**Solution:** A rules library written for IT-services contracts (named-resource billing, FTE-to-ticket mismatches, volume-tier discounts not applied, escalators above caps, benchmarking rights never exercised, service-credit earn-back traps, "regulatory change" pass-through surcharges, duplicate and shared-resource billing). Look-back audits on 24-36 months of invoices; recoveries land as credit memos against future invoices; Clawback advances 50-70% of agreed credit memos to the client from a credit facility and keeps the spread. A subscription for continuous monitoring is the bridge into the Meter.

**Why now:** Vendors are fighting to hold revenue while renegotiating (about 25% of contracts a year [src]), which makes them more willing to settle legacy claims as part of a new deal; AI-clause deals create new rights with no enforcement mechanism; after the H-1B fee saga, any visa surcharge billed to clients while the fee was in force and then vacated is a refund claim (a hypothesis to test, not a fact I found).

**Wedge and first 10 customers:** Mid-market and large-enterprise contracts that were renewed in the last 36 months and carry an escalator; look-backs on ten clients sourced through procurement-association members and PE operating partners.

**Path to $100M (bottom-up):** If leakage is 3% of the $100B pool and the fee is 25%, the annual fee pool is $750M and $100M needs 13% of it. At 1% leakage the pool is $250M and the idea needs 40%, which fails. At 5% it is $1.25B and 8%. So the idea works only if the first ten audits show at least 3% recoverable. I found no independent leakage study; the first ten audits are the test. [my arithmetic]

**Who does it today:** PRGX (recovery audit; has an IT-contract-labor case study) [src]; Vaudit/VendorAudit (claims $1B+ of vendor spend audited) [src]; Cotiviti in healthcare [bg]; telecom-expense and freight-audit firms in neighboring categories; ISG and law firms at renewal; internal audit.

**Why they lose:** Generic AP auditors lack IT-contract logic (FTE pools, resource units, earn-backs); advisers do not work on contingency for old invoices; neither can match a ticket to a login. Specialization is the only wedge, and it is thin.

**Moat:** Process power (a rules library that improves with every audit) and, if financing is added, a balance-sheet edge. Honest verdict: the weakest moat of the eight; its main value is as the no-budget entry that funds the Meter.

**Earned secret:** Earn-back clauses are written so that hitting a target for two consecutive months cancels the credit, so credits go unpaid even when quality is bad [src: legal commentary and Forrester]. The unpaid-credit pool is largest in contracts older than 24 months.

**First 90 days:** Run five look-backs on contingency to measure the actual recovery rate; build the rules library from them; set up a $10M receivables facility with a specialty lender; decide at day 90 whether the measured rate clears 3%.

**Outside-the-box score:** 4
