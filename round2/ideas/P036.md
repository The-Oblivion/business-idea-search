# P036: Gas-turbine fleet services and aftermarket (8 ideas, 2 per lens)

Generated 2026-10-14. Pool: about $30B a year globally (GMI: $27.1B in 2025, $30.2B in 2026, US $4.5B; other paid reports say $10-25B). GE Vernova's whole Power segment (gas, steam, nuclear, hydro services) did $13.07B of services revenue in 2025.

## How to read the numbers (honesty notes)

Sourced facts are linked at the bottom. These are my own derivations or assumptions and should be checked with 20 outage managers before anyone believes them:

- US large-frame gas turbine units: about 1,500-2,000. Derived from EIA's 491 GW gas fleet (280 GW combined cycle, mostly 2x1). No unit count was found.
- US hot-section outages (hot gas path or major inspection): about 300-400 a year. Derived from OEM intervals (hot gas path at 16,000-24,000 equivalent operating hours) and a mix of baseload and peaking units.
- Cost of one hot-section outage: $5-12M. Trade sources conflict; a vendor blog says a major overhaul is $4-12M.
- US spend per large unit: about $2.4M a year, which is GMI's $4.5B divided by about 1,900 units. F-class baseload units run $3-5M.
- Share of units outside an OEM long-term service agreement (LTSA): unknown. One repair executive said about 70% were under OEM agreements at some point in the past. I assume 40-60% of units are outside one today.
- Where a number would need heroic share (over 20% of a pool), the idea says so.
- Overlap warning: ideas 1, 2, 5 and 6 are layers of one independent-aftermarket stack. They are separate businesses with different payers, wedges and capital needs. I would start with 5, 1 or 2.

Ranking by my own view of big x non-obvious x defensible: 5 (independent LTSA), 1 (spare-set pool), 2 (core exchange), 8 (BTM wrapper), 6 (repair factory), 7 (capacity-as-a-service), 3 (certified unfired), 4 (outage bench). Ideas 3, 4 and 7 are the weakest and are labelled that way.

## 1. [INSIDER: plant O&M director / outage manager] Spare-Set Pool: hot-section outages with a guaranteed return-to-service date

**One-liner.** A neutral pool of pre-inspected, pre-fit hot-gas-path sets (rotor, nozzles, shrouds, combustion hardware) for the common F- and E-class frames. Owners with one to four units rent a set per outage and send their retired set into the pool. The contract carries a return-to-service date with liquidated damages.

**Customer.** Owners of 1-4 large-frame units that cannot carry a spare set: G&T co-ops, municipal and joint-action agencies, small PE-backed IPPs, industrial cogens. Units outside OEM LTSA scope first.

**Payer and signer.** VP Generation or plant manager signs; the CFO co-signs because it replaces a $5-10M spare-capital purchase. Budget line: major-outage O&M and spares capital.

**Problem.** The length of an outage is set 12-14 months earlier, by whether a certified spare is on site when the unit opens. Multi-unit fleets already do this. GE says "many operators that have multiple F-class" units use a seed rotor. Dubai's DEWA built a rotor-swap method that Siemens says added up to 64 days of availability across eight uses, with one overhaul done in 29 working days. A single-unit owner cannot tie up $10M in a spare, so it waits on shop turnaround, and castings scarcity is stretching that wait.

**Solution.** Build or buy sets for the 6-8 biggest frame families. Priced like an aircraft-engine lease: rent while the owner's set is in the shop, plus usage-based maintenance reserves per equivalent operating hour that fund the repair, plus a guarantee fee. Sets are financed with infrastructure or equipment-finance capital. Returned sets go through partner repair shops and back into the pool.

**Why now.** New heavy-duty units are sold out into 2030-31 (GE Vernova backlog plus slot reservations 116 GW, Q2 2026), so no replacement capacity exists during an overrun. Wood Mackenzie says single-crystal blade production is the "most stubborn constraint" and GE Aerospace is paying $11.75B for casting maker CPP. PJM capacity cleared at the cap three auctions running ($333.44/MW-day for 2027/28, $325 for 2028/29), and 2027/28 was 6.6 GW short of the reliability requirement. Overrun days have never been dearer.

**Wedge and first 10 customers.** Two frame families in one region (for example 7FA.03 and 7EA in PJM/MISO). Ten co-ops and municipal agencies from the NRECA and APPA ecosystems and the combustion-turbine users' groups, each with a 2027-28 hot-section outage coming. The first set is discounted in exchange for the outage data.

**Path to $100M.** Revenue per swap outage is about $6M (rent plus usage reserves plus guarantee), against a total event cost of $5-12M. $100M is about 17 outages a year, or about 5% of the roughly 350 US hot-section events a year (derived), or about 12% of the events at few-unit owners. Capital: sets cannot be shared across frame families, so depth costs money. 16-25 sets at $5-10M is $110-200M of assets. Analog: Willis Lease earned $291.6M of lease rent and $232.0M of maintenance-reserve revenue on a roughly $2.8B engine portfolio in 2025. Canada, Mexico and the Middle East extend it.

**Who does it today.** Multi-unit owners hold their own spares. OEMs run exchange and rotor programs inside LTSAs (GE rotor-in major inspection, seed-rotor concept; Siemens rotor exchange for DEWA). ISPs offer repair-exchange (Chromalloy, Sulzer, EthosEnergy, Allied Power Group). For aeroderivatives, engine swaps are routine and ProEnergy sells repurposed jet cores. No neutral, multi-owner pooled lease of frame hot-section sets turned up in searches (the closest hit was a patent on a "component pool control system" and a parts-stocking agreement listing).

**Why they lose.** OEMs earn their margin on new parts and want LTSA lock-in, so they will not rent sets to non-LTSA owners. ISPs do not have the balance sheet to leave $100M+ idle. Owners cannot pool among themselves (frame mismatch, coordination). Our edge is neutrality plus cheap long-duration capital.

**Moat (7 Powers).** Scale economies (each added customer lowers spares needed per customer), cornered resource (certified sets with serial-level records), switching costs (outage plans and records live with us), mild network effects (more owners means higher odds a compatible set is free).

**Earned secret.** The critical path of an outage is not labor. It is the shop-return slip, set on the day you booked the shop. The most common reason an outage overruns is that the repaired parts are late or the as-found scope grew, and nobody holds a spare. Multi-unit fleets solve this privately with a seed rotor. The owners who need it most (one or two units) are exactly the ones who cannot afford it.

**Risks and legal.** Fit-up and interchangeability between units of the same model (validate with outage managers first). LTSA clauses give the OEM title to remaining parts life, so non-LTSA units come first. Pool depth versus fleet fragmentation (Power Engineering notes many models and running changes limit parts interchangeability).

**First 90 days.** Weeks 1-3: 30 calls with outage managers and users' groups; map 2027-28 outage calendars by frame family. Weeks 4-8: LOIs for three spring-2027 outages; sign one repair partner; talk to one infrastructure lender. Weeks 9-12: buy or lease one used certified set through a broker, get an independent inspection, and design the liquidated-damages wrap with an insurer.

**Outside-the-box score: 7**

## 2. [INSIDER: independent service provider shop owner] Hot-Section Core Exchange ("LKQ for turbines")

**One-liner.** Pay owners real money for their retired hot-gas-path parts, give every part a serial-level remaining-life record, route them through partner repair shops, and sell warrantied remanufactured parts at 55-70% of OEM price.

**Customer.** Non-LTSA owners buying parts per outage (merchant IPPs, co-ops, municipal agencies, cogens), and ISPs short of cores. Both sides of the exchange.

**Payer and signer.** Outage manager or director of supply chain. Budget line: the outage parts line, which Turbomachinery calls "the largest slice of the maintenance pie." We also pay the owner a core credit, so the owner sees a net cut.

**Problem.** There is no market price for retired cores. Owners get scrap value or a trade-in credit set by the party buying. A repair vendor says a set of components worth $7,600 as scrap was repaired for $1.3M, and advises owners not to sell "scrapped" hot-gas-path parts without a second look (promotional, but the mechanism is real). Meanwhile new blades and vanes are the scarcest thing in the industry.

**Solution.** A core bid desk: the owner uploads its inspection report, gets a bid in 48 hours, and is paid cash or credit. Each part gets a passport (serial, hours and starts, coatings, repair history, NDE results). Partner shops repair to our specification for a fee. We own the inventory and sell reman sets with a one-year warranty, backed by an insurer so lenders and insurers will accept them. Cores flow into idea 1's pool.

**Why now.** Casting scarcity makes every reusable airfoil precious (WoodMac, GE Aerospace-CPP). OEM service pricing is rising: GE Vernova says Q2 2026 growth was "higher volume and price" and transactional orders per unit keep rising by double digits. New units are 5-6 years out, so old frames run harder and longer.

**Wedge and first 10 customers.** Start with the 7FA.03 and 7EA families, where independents already repair and the patents are long expired. Ten owners outside OEM LTSAs, found through outage managers. Get their last three outages' disposition data to build the first price index.

**Path to $100M.** US cores: about 350 hot-section events a year (derived) times $2-4M of repairable value is about $0.7-1.4B of cores a year. $100M of reman sales is about 25 sets at about $4M, or about 7% of all US hot-section events (about 14% of the events outside an OEM LTSA if half are). Globally the fleet is about 6,000 large frames (Power Engineering) and this is under 2% of global events. Gross margin 25-35%, so $25-35M gross profit. Heroic-adjacent in the US alone, so international ISP wholesale is part of the plan.

**Who does it today.** ISPs buy cores for their own repair and exchange (Chromalloy, Sulzer, Liburdi, EthosEnergy under One Equity Partners, Allied Power Group under AEA Investors, MD&A). OEMs run exchange and trade-in programs. Brokers sell used parts (Turbologistics, World Wide Gas Turbine Products). Nobody found runs a neutral, priced core exchange with serial-level passports.

**Why they lose.** ISPs will not feed cores to rivals and want exclusivity. OEMs make their margin on new parts. Brokers lack the engineering and certification.

**Moat.** Cornered resource (the core supply and its records), scale economies (batching repair capacity), data (remaining life by serial), network effects (more owners improves matching).

**Earned secret.** The aftermarket's scarce input is cores, not repair skill. A repair shop can always find a welder; it cannot always find a repairable bucket. Owners do not know what theirs is worth because no one posts a price. Whoever posts the price collects the supply.

**Risks and legal.** LTSAs revert title to remaining parts life to the OEM, so stay with non-LTSA parts. OEM patents on newer designs (GE has stepped up legal action against copies), so stay with designs old enough to be out of patent and repairs rather than new manufacture. Insurer acceptance of repaired parts needs to be arranged up front.

**First 90 days.** 30 days: collect 20 anonymized retired-part dispositions from outage managers and publish a first core-value index. 60 days: two repair partners on rate cards. 90 days: buy the first five sets of cores and sell the first reman set to a co-op.

**Outside-the-box score: 7**

## 3. [PATTERN: collectibles liquidity stack] Certified Unfired: the grading and escrow layer for stranded turbines and slot reservations

**One-liner.** A grading standard, escrow and OEM-consent layer so unfired and low-hour turbines, and later slot reservations, can trade as a standardized asset. This is the PSA of turbines. It is the weakest of the 8: the revenue is easy but a defensible profit pool is uncertain.

**Customer.** Sellers: developers with stranded orders, utilities swapping units, reservation holders who cannot get power or permits. Buyers: data-center developers, utilities and industrials that cannot wait for 2030-31 OEM slots.

**Payer and signer.** Buyer's VP of power or head of energy procurement (CFO co-signs). Budget line: generation capex. Wood Mackenzie puts new turbines at about $600/kW by end-2027; Hallador paid about $760/kW for a 460 MW unfired Siemens package with generators and steam turbine.

**Problem.** The used-turbine market is fragmented, with "no mandatory disclosure standards, no universal inspection protocols" and sellers who take deposits and vanish (USP&E Global, a seller, describing its own market). Buyers cannot verify preservation or whether the OEM will still warrant the unit. Hallador routed its units through Siemens USA refurbishment precisely to keep OEM inspection and LTSA eligibility.

**Solution.** A five-grade standard from "unfired, OEM-refurbished, LTSA-eligible" down to "parts only", with an independent inspection protocol and preservation records. Escrow and deposit protection, lien and title search, export-control screening. Orchestration of the OEM refurbishment and consent. Matching of transferable reservations where the OEM allows it. A principal desk for fast flips.

**Why now.** GE Vernova backlog plus reservations reached 116 GW with 2031 slots on sale; Siemens Energy's CEO says European buyers now pay reservation fees; turbine prices are up 195% since 2019; lead times are about six years. Cleanview counts roughly 90 GW of behind-the-meter data-center power planned against about 2 GW operating, so many reservations and orders will fail to convert and need a buyer.

**Wedge and first 10 customers.** Hallador-style deals into US data-center developers: five stranded-unit sellers (the Energy World Corporation type) and five behind-the-meter buyers.

**Path to $100M.** Two models. Fee model: 3-4% of GMV means $2.5-3.3B of trades a year, about 3.3-4.3 GW at $760/kW. That is heroic: only Hallador (0.46 GW) and ProEnergy (21 turbines, over 1 GW) are on record, so the secondary market is unproven. Principal model: buy, refurbish and resell, and $100M of revenue is only about 130 MW a year, but it needs about $100M of inventory at 8-15% net margin ($8-15M). Honest verdict: easy revenue, thin and cyclical profit unless the grade becomes the gate that lenders and OEMs recognize.

**Who does it today.** Turbologistics, World Wide Gas Turbine Products, USP&E Global, Surplus Record listings, ProEnergy (repurposed CF6 cores), older online auction experiments (Enporion), OEM refurbishment arms (Siemens USA).

**Why they lose.** Brokers have no grading, escrow or engineering. The OEM cannot be a neutral broker because it sells the scarce new slots, but it must consent to transfers, which is also the key dependency: if OEMs refuse consent, this idea is dead.

**Moat.** A trusted standard accepted by lenders and insurers (branding), liquidity network effects, OEM-consent relationships (cornered resource). All three are weak until volume exists.

**Earned secret.** An unfired turbine's value is not its metal. It is whether the OEM will still warrant it and put it on an LTSA. Buyers cannot tell a properly preserved unit from one rusting in a crate, so the grade is the product.

**Risks and legal.** Boom-bust: a late-1990s speculative resale market in turbines on order existed and collapsed with the cycle. OEM consent, export controls, and lien/title. This is the one most exposed to an AI-capex pullback.

**First 90 days.** Write the grading spec with two independent engineers and one OEM refurbishment contact. List three stranded units. Term-sheet one Hallador-style deal. Sign an escrow agent.

**Outside-the-box score: 5**

## 4. [PATTERN: expert-labor marketplace plus spot labor in a shortage] Outage Bench: a credentialed bench of turbine specialists

**One-liner.** An employer-of-record bench of vetted ex-OEM technical advisors, Level-III inspectors, rotor and controls specialists and precision millwrights, with a published skills passport that insurers, owners and ISPs accept in place of an OEM badge, plus remote senior supervision. Weakest moat of the 8: it is a staffing business unless the credential becomes a standard.

**Customer.** Outage managers; ISPs and EPCs with more outage work than people; OEM field arms at peak (GE's FieldCore, Mitsubishi Power).

**Payer and signer.** Outage or plant manager; ISP VP of operations. Budget line: outage labor.

**Problem.** Combined Cycle Journal reports that "eighty to ninety percent" of the experienced GE field engineers who commissioned the early-2000s fleet have left; GE is now ramping to 30 GW a year of output. OEM technical advisors are allocated to new-unit commissioning. Outage work is seasonal (one report: 35% of annual activity in the spring quarter, 15% in Q3), so skilled craft idle between outages. OEM LTSAs typically need OEM-qualified supervision, so independent labor cannot easily touch covered scope.

**Solution.** Recruit and retain 600-1,000 specialists as W-2 employees with per-diem and insurance. Publish a skills passport (work samples, OEM training history, incident record). A remote expert desk lets one Level-III supervise three to five crews on video. Pool the bench across owners to flatten the seasonal peaks. Sell the credential to insurers and lenders.

**Why now.** OEM capacity is spoken for through 2031; outages cost more and last longer; workforce is aging and vocational pipelines are drying up (GasTurbineHub, Equipment News).

**Wedge and first 10 customers.** Spring-2027 outages at non-LTSA units and ISP overflow. Ten owners and two ISPs, found through the same outage-manager network as idea 1.

**Path to $100M.** US turbine-outage field labor is about $1.3B a year (derived: 350 hot-section events x $8M x 30% labor, plus about 1,000 minor events x $1.2M x 40%). $100M of gross revenue is about 8% of that, about 4% if behind-the-meter and new-build commissioning labor is counted. At about $140k billed per person-year that is about 700 people. Take rate 18-25%, so $18-25M gross profit. Gross revenue is not profit: this is the thinnest margin of the 8.

**Who does it today.** Tradesmen International (reports about 988,000 outage man-hours), Nesfircroft, OEM field units, ISPs' own field forces (EthosEnergy calls itself the largest independent provider of field services), general staffing firms.

**Why they lose.** OEMs staff only their own contracts and new-unit liquidated-damages work. Staffing firms have no credential and no supervision layer. ISPs hire only for themselves.

**Moat.** Weak. A network of people (cornered resource) plus a passport and remote supervision (process power). Switching costs are low. It only becomes defensible if insurers and OEM LTSAs accept the credential.

**Earned secret.** The binding constraint on third-party outage work is not bodies, it is a credential the OEM and insurer will accept. The knowledge sits with a few thousand ex-OEM freelancers who owners cannot find.

**Risks and legal.** Worker classification (use W-2, employer of record). LTSA clauses that require OEM personnel.

**First 90 days.** Interview 25 outage managers; sign 50 freelance advisors; place three for spring 2027; talk to two insurers about accepting the passport.

**Outside-the-box score: 4**

## 5. [DILEMMA: OEM pricing cannot follow condition] Progressive for Turbines: a condition-priced, insurer-backed independent LTSA

**One-liner.** A fixed-price per-hour maintenance contract for F- and E-class units, priced on measured wear and operating history rather than the OEM's averaged equivalent-hours formula. It carries a pooled spare inventory and a reinsurer-backed parts-cost cap. It targets the best-run units that the OEM formula overcharges.

**Customer.** Owners whose OEM LTSAs expire in 2026-2029, or who are paying transactional rates after expiry: co-ops, municipal agencies, PE-backed IPPs, industrials.

**Payer and signer.** CFO and VP Generation; the lender's independent engineer for project-financed units. Budget line: LTSA fees, about $2-5M per large unit a year (derived).

**Problem.** OEM LTSAs price on equivalent operating hours, with the OEM defining how starts and trips convert to hours. The factors are fleet averages. A clean-gas baseload unit with no trips subsidizes the start-heavy unit next door. LTSAs are close to non-terminable, and trade sources say title to the remaining life of parts bought under the contract reverts to the OEM. Older reporting says only OEMs offer LTSAs on F, G and H classes. Combined Cycle Journal notes that under an LTSA "you may have no other option" than hours-based overhauls. Scarcity has given OEMs pricing power (GE Vernova: "higher volume and price").

**Solution.** (1) A free forensic audit of the owner's LTSA (equivalent-hours math, true-up, title clauses). (2) A condition model per unit from borescope, NDE and operating data (the passport data from idea 2). (3) A fixed $/hour price 10-20% under the OEM's for top-quartile units, with an annual true-up to measured condition, hedged by excess-of-loss reinsurance and the pooled spares from ideas 1 and 2. (4) A delay and availability guarantee.

**Why now.** The 2000-2006 F-class fleet is rolling off contracts. OEM bandwidth is consumed by a 116 GW backlog. Owners cannot walk away to a replacement unit. Casting scarcity makes a pooled-parts balance sheet valuable. Plant M&A at about $1,100/kW (LS Power paying about $5B for about 4.4 GW) means buyers now underwrite remaining life and capex carefully.

**Wedge and first 10 customers.** Start with older-design frames where independents already have hardware (7FA.03, 501F, 7EA). Third parties are weakest on directionally solidified and single-crystal blades on the newest machines, and Chromalloy advises OEM parts for designs under about 10 years old. First 10: co-ops and municipal agencies plus one PE-backed IPP, opened by the free audit.

**Path to $100M.** Revenue is contract fees. At $3-4M per F-class unit a year, $100M is 25-35 units: 2-3% of the roughly 1,100 US baseload F-class units (derived), or under 2% of all large frames. Share is not the problem; credibility, bankability and balance sheet are. Gross margin 12-18% is my assumption; GE Vernova's Power segment ran an 18.8% EBITDA margin in Q2 2026 as a ceiling reference. Global expansion is available.

**Who does it today.** OEMs (GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, Mitsubishi Power). GE also runs "cross-fleet" service on rival 501F and SGT-800 machines, but through its own retrofit hardware as a conquest sale. Third-party term agreements exist: Wood Group has held a 7FA term service agreement with Associated Electric Cooperative, and EthosEnergy has an eight-year Texas 7EA major-maintenance contract. Allied Power Group and Sulzer do field service and repair. I found none offering condition-priced, reinsurer-backed F-class LTSAs.

**Why they lose.** The OEM cannot price on condition without repricing its whole book: the good risks would leave and it would be left with the bad ones (adverse selection). Its hours formula is also a profit lever. ISPs lack an insurance wrap and a balance sheet. Insurers lack engineering.

**Moat.** Counter-positioning (OEMs cannot copy it without cannibalizing their book), data scale (the condition database improves pricing), switching costs (10-year contracts), cornered resource (the pool).

**Earned secret.** The real buyer of an LTSA is the lender's independent engineer, not the plant manager, and the equivalent-hours formula is an averaged price that anyone with better data on wear can skim. The audit is the door; the takeout is the business.

**Risks and legal.** If the guarantee looks like insurance, state regulators may treat it as such. The round-1 finalist on pay-on-sale home-value guarantees died on exactly this. So use a licensed carrier and fronting arrangement. Early-termination costs on existing OEM contracts. Tail risk (a rotor failure) must sit with reinsurers, not our balance sheet.

**First 90 days.** Weeks 1-4: five free LTSA forensic reviews with co-ops and municipal agencies. Weeks 5-8: hire an ex-OEM contracts engineer and an ex-insurer engineering surveyor. Weeks 9-12: a term sheet from a specialty reinsurer for parts-cost excess-of-loss, and a bid on one LTSA expiring in 2027.

**Outside-the-box score: 8**

## 6. [DILEMMA: the OEM's repair-versus-replace conflict] Hot-Section Repair Factory: automated, multi-cycle repair that the OEM would rather sell you new parts than do

**One-liner.** Two automated, permitted repair-and-coat shops that make second and third repairs routine on E- and F-class buckets, nozzles and shrouds. Capacity is sold to owners, to ISPs, and to OEMs who are drowning in backlog.

**Customer.** Non-LTSA owners, ISPs short of shop capacity, OEMs subcontracting overflow, and the core exchange (idea 2).

**Payer and signer.** Outage manager or supply-chain director; for the OEM channel, the VP of service operations. Budget line: the repair line, which I estimate at about 22% of services, roughly $1B in the US (derived).

**Problem.** Repair capacity (welding, coating, heat treatment, NDE) is manual and capacity-limited. OEMs designate parts "life-limited" and sell new, and they put factory capacity on new units. With casting scarce, repair is the only supply of airfoils for many owners. Permitting a coating and heat-treat facility takes years.

**Solution.** Robotic laser cladding cells, scan-to-repair, adaptive machining, automated coating and NDE. Build a qualified library of repair procedures per part number, using the ORNL/EPRI additive-repair route for hard-to-weld alloys. Sell through the core exchange. Start by acquiring a small permitted ISP shop and automating it.

**Why now.** The CPP deal and WoodMac make casting the industry's binding constraint. OEM transactional prices are rising by double digits per unit. Old frames are being kept in service.

**Wedge and first 10 customers.** E-class and older F-class parts where designs are old enough to be out of patent and ISPs already repair. First 10: owners and one OEM overflow contract through the core exchange.

**Path to $100M.** Two shops at about $50M each. That is about 10% of a roughly $1B US repair market (derived), or under 2% of a roughly $6B global repair market (derived). Capital $40-60M per shop (assumption).

**Who does it today.** Chromalloy (repairs, coatings, castings; bought Trac Group), Sulzer, Liburdi (direct-energy-deposition repair), Allied Power Group (AEA Investors; bought EthosEnergy's generator division assets in April 2026), EthosEnergy (One Equity Partners; bought TSL in 2025), MD&A, and the OEM shops (GE has robotized arc-additive repair running in its service centers). Howmet, PCC and CPP make new blades.

**Why they lose.** The OEM earns more on replacement than repair and puts capacity on new units. The ISPs are PE-owned roll-ups with manual processes, spending on acquisitions rather than automation.

**Moat.** Process power (the qualified repair library and yields), cornered resource (permits and qualifications), scale.

**Earned secret.** Independents are limited by manual weld and re-qualification labor, not by metallurgy. Automation plus a scan-to-repair library can make repeat repairs routine, and that is exactly where OEM economics point the other way.

**Risks and legal.** GE has "stepped up legal action" against copied parts (Turbomachinery, 2017), so do repair and out-of-patent designs, not new manufacture of newer ones. Insurer acceptance. Capital intensity.

**First 90 days.** Identify two acquirable permitted shops; license a cladding cell; sign three owners and one ISP for first volumes; start the repair-procedure qualification on the top two part numbers.

**Outside-the-box score: 6**

## 7. [SHOCK: new-build scarcity plus capacity prices at the cap] Megawatts from existing iron: financed, multi-vendor uprates paid for from capacity revenue

**One-liner.** Packages of inlet cooling, controls and firing-temperature tuning, compressor and steam-path upgrades and supplemental firing, sold with financing and an accredited-MW guarantee. This is the weakest-evidenced of the 8: the uprate market has to grow roughly 3-5x for it to work.

**Customer.** IPPs and utilities with units in PJM, MISO, NYISO, ISO-NE and ERCOT.

**Payer and signer.** SVP Asset Management or Commercial, with the CFO. Budget line: growth capex and merchant capacity revenue.

**Problem.** At $325-333/MW-day, a megawatt of accredited capacity earns about $119-122k a year; a new turbine is 5-6 years out. Siemens Energy estimates up to about 6 GW from upgrading its own eligible US F-class units (a vendor estimate). Real examples: Avista's Coyote Springs +18 MW; Consumers Energy's Zeeland expansion +50 MW with a 2.5% efficiency gain. Yet PJM's 2028/29 auction cleared only 207.6 MW of uprates. OEMs sell upgrades as one-off hardware, tie them to LTSA renewals, and are short of engineering capacity.

**Solution.** A multi-vendor integrator that buys hardware from the OEM or an ISP, adds controls tuning, M&V and ISO accreditation filings, finances the capex against contracted capacity revenue, and guarantees the accredited MW. Components: turbine inlet cooling (a vendor claims about $100/kW), controls and tuning, compressor and steam-path upgrades, duct firing.

**Why now.** Capacity prices at the cap three auctions running, an RTO short of its reliability requirement, a 116 GW turbine backlog, owners with no alternative. The 2026 PowerGen program carries a session on retrofit upgrades, so interest is real.

**Wedge and first 10 customers.** Hot-day derate recovery via inlet cooling and tuning on non-LTSA F-class units in PJM and MISO, where the payback is one to two years. First 10: IPP fleets with summer capacity-performance exposure.

**Path to $100M.** About 400 MW a year of installed packages at about $250/kW (assumption) is $100M. That is about 2% a year of a roughly 20 GW US uprate potential (my extrapolation from Siemens' 6 GW for its own F-class). Revenue-share instead of turnkey is worse: 30% of $119k per MW-year needs about 2.8 GW installed. Heroic flag: PJM clears about 200 MW of uprates per auction today, so we would be a large share of today's market.

**Who does it today.** GE Vernova (T-Fire, Advanced Gas Path upgrades), Siemens Energy, Mitsubishi Power, ISPs such as Power Systems Mfg and EthosEnergy, inlet-cooling vendors, and EPC firms.

**Why they lose.** OEMs sell hardware up front and cannot take performance or financing risk across rival machines. Owners have capex hurdles and LTSA consent constraints.

**Moat.** Weak to medium. Counter-positioning (multi-vendor, performance-guaranteed), process power (M&V and accreditation expertise).

**Earned secret.** Every unit leaves megawatts on the table through hot-day lapse, conservative control margins and clearance degradation. With capacity at the cap, an uprate pays back in one to two years, but nobody takes the performance risk and the financing.

**Risks and legal.** Air-permit modification rules (an uprate that raises emissions can trigger major-source review), OEM approval under LTSAs, ISO accreditation rules, and the OEMs' own speed.

**First 90 days.** Pick 20 non-LTSA F-class units in PJM and MISO; do hot-day performance screens; offer two owners a guaranteed-MW inlet-cooling and tuning package; line up one capital partner.

**Outside-the-box score: 5**

## 8. [SHOCK: a new buyer, the AI-campus power developer] Uptime Wrapper for mixed behind-the-meter data-center fleets

**One-liner.** Cross-OEM operations, maintenance and spares for AI-campus gas fleets built from whatever turbines and engines could be found. One contract, one uptime number for the whole site, with a pooled spare-engine inventory and a remote operations center.

**Customer.** Behind-the-meter power developers and operators (neoclouds, developers like Crusoe-type companies, power-as-a-service owners, utilities-in-waiting) and the infrastructure funds financing them.

**Payer and signer.** Head of energy or COO of the developer; infrastructure-fund asset manager. Budget line: operating expense for energy supply, plus lender-required reserves.

**Problem.** Cleanview counts about 90 GW of behind-the-meter capacity planned and only about 2 GW operating, with xAI's Memphis sites (about 1.5 GW) most of it. Developers are using whatever equipment is available (aeroderivatives, Solar Titans, recip engines, refurbished industrial turbines). Crusoe's Abilene site mixes LM2500XPRESS units with Solar Titan 350s. Each OEM guarantees only its own box. Nobody guarantees the site.

**Solution.** A site-level availability contract: operate (remote plus on-site), maintain across OEMs, hold pooled spare engines and parts for the common models, and handle outage scheduling across the whole site. Priced per kW-year with availability credits.

**Why now.** 12 GW of behind-the-meter gas and fuel-cell capacity tied to data-center projects had been announced by mid-2026 and owners have no choice but to run old and mixed equipment. Equipment makers are bundling service (Baker Hughes' deal with Kodiak includes training, spares and potential long-term services), which shows the budget exists and leaves the mixed-fleet case unserved.

**Wedge and first 10 customers.** Two or three mixed sites of 200-400 MW coming online in 2027. First 10: the 10-15 developers and funds with the largest announced behind-the-meter pipelines.

**Path to $100M.** All-in $80-100 per kW-year (assumption: aeroderivative parts-and-repair of about $6-10/MWh x 8,000 hours, plus operations labor and a risk premium). $100M is about 1.0-1.25 GW. With 5-13 GW operating by end-2027 (Cleanview), that is roughly 10-25% of operating capacity, which is heroic, and developers like Crusoe and VoltaGrid prefer to operate themselves. A smaller path is principal ownership of the spare-engine pool plus services.

**Who does it today.** OEMs with bundled service (Baker Hughes, Caterpillar, Solar Turbines, GE Vernova aeroderivative service), developers' in-house teams (Crusoe, xAI), VoltaGrid with Halliburton on a design-build-operate offering, Duos with Fortress on operating mobile turbines, and ProEnergy on repurposed jet cores.

**Why they lose.** Each OEM guarantees only its own equipment and has no incentive to take on a rival's engines. In-house teams lack bench depth and spares pools.

**Moat.** Scale economies (pooled spares across customers), switching costs (embedded operations), cornered resource (cross-OEM runbooks and spare engines).

**Earned secret.** AI-campus power fleets are Frankenstein fleets, assembled from whatever could be bought. The customer's real KPI is cluster uptime, whose value per megawatt-hour is far above a utility's wholesale price. That makes site-level uptime the thing to sell, and nobody can guarantee it today.

**Risks and legal.** Air permitting is the owner's risk, not ours: EPA reportedly found xAI's "temporary" Memphis turbines not exempt, and that county permit expires 2027-01-02. In-sourcing by developers. Everything rides one capex cycle, and behind-the-meter power may be transitional once grid interconnection arrives.

**First 90 days.** Interview 15 developers on their mixed-fleet maintenance plans; draft a site-level availability contract with a reinsurer; line up a pooled-engine supplier; bid on one 2027 site.

**Outside-the-box score: 6**
