# P060: 340B Drug Pricing Program covered-entity purchases (round 2 ideas)

Pool: HRSA says covered entities bought $100B of outpatient drugs in CY2025 (up ~22% from $81.4B); disproportionate share (DSH) hospitals were $79.2B of it, and specialty drugs were 38.1% of units but 61.9% of dollars. IQVIA values the same purchases at about $200B at list prices, so the yearly discount is on the order of $100B (my arithmetic). The money a new company can actually contest is far smaller: contract-pharmacy and TPA fees (five chains/PBMs earned nearly $3B of gross profit from 340B in 2023; CVS's Wellpartner alone booked $382M of TPA fees in 2023), the hospital software layer (Craneware, which owns Sentry, had $206M of total revenue in FY26 and guides to about $185M), manufacturer platforms, and states' $58.4B Medicaid rebate stream.

Shocks stacked on 1 January 2027: (1) HRSA's revised rebate pilot (10 manufacturers approved 1 Oct 2026; AHA sued again 9 Oct); (2) CMS's CY2027 OPPS proposal to pay 340B-acquired Part B drugs at ASP minus 33.4% with JG/TB/XX modifiers and a faster remedy offset (final rule due within weeks); (3) state Medicaid carve-outs (Louisiana live, Indiana proposed, Illinois bill); (4) Medicare "maximum fair price" (MFP) colliding with 340B; (5) three competing bills in Congress; (6) federal PBM reform signed 3 Feb 2026; (7) a D.D.C. ruling on 31 Mar 2026 vacating HRSA's 2013 GPO-replenishment policy.

"(my estimate)" means I could not source it. Treat those numbers as hypotheses to test in the first 90 days.

## What the research changed

- **Hospital-side 340B software is a ~$100M-class pool, not a billion-dollar one.** Craneware's whole company is $206M of revenue and guided down for FY27; Sentry is said to serve about 40% of US hospitals; Pillr serves 300+ hospitals. A $100M company selling only to hospitals needs a large share of them. Ideas that stay hospital-side are flagged "heroic share".
- **Rebate financing is dead, and rebate operations alone is thin.** HRSA says the pilot's drugs are about 5.5% of 340B sales (~$5.5B at ceiling prices). Float on that for ~45 days at 8% is about $50M at 100% share (my arithmetic), wholesalers already extend trade credit, and the House (pause) and Senate (wind-down) bills would end the pilot. HRSA's own burden estimate is $537M a year; the AHA says over $1B. I folded rebate operations into one idea (#4) rather than leaving it standalone.
- **The big fee pools sit in the pharmacy channel and in Medicaid.** Chains/PBMs hold 77% of 239,905 contract-pharmacy relationships; Apexus (the HRSA Prime Vendor, a Vizient subsidiary) was on track for $227M of revenue in 2022 at margins above 80% (NYT via GV Wire, not read directly); Medicaid drug rebates were about $58.4B in FY2024 (KFF).
- **A statutory clearinghouse is a ~$50M contract, not a venture.** The Senate SUSTAIN 340B Act describes an independent third-party operator funded by a user fee with a $50M base from FY2031 (secondary summary; bill text not read).
- **CMS's own survey math flips the economics of Medicare Part B 340B drugs.** The survey found 340B drugs bought at 33.4% below ASP and non-340B drugs at 2.7% above; non-340B drugs at 340B hospitals stay at ASP+6%. Break-even discount is about 37%.
- **MFP and 340B already collide in practice.** Walgreens told 340B customers that from 1 Jan 2026 all Part D claims for the 2026 selected drugs go to MFP rebates, not the 340B pilot. NCPA says the NCPDP point-of-sale 340B flag is largely unusable. A Health Affairs piece (31 Jul 2026, read only via search summary) says the overlap could create billions of duplicate discounts.
- **The two manufacturer platforms are not neutral.** Kalderos sued HHS for the right to run rebates; Second Sight (340B ESP and the Beacon rebate platform) is a subsidiary of Berkeley Research Group, the firm behind PhRMA's $6.5B "340B pulled rebates from Medicaid" study.
- **Audit data say the errors are mostly administrative.** HRSA's FY25 audits: 115 entities, 49% with an adverse finding; of those, 75% had OPAIS record errors and 50% duplicate-discount/Medicaid Exclusion File findings, and 5 of 28 MEF findings were later found to be administrative only (Bluesight analysis).

## Ideas

### 1. Retro-Flag (insider: TPA compliance lead)

**One-liner.** A post-adjudication correction rail: take the 340B status that administrators assign days after a script is filled, push it back into the state Medicaid or MCO claim, reprice at acquisition cost, and invoice manufacturer rebates only on units that qualify. Paid out of the rebates recaptured.

- **Customer.** State Medicaid agencies (fee-for-service and managed care) and the MCOs/PBMs under them. First: Louisiana (live), Indiana (proposed), Illinois (bill introduced).
- **Payer and signer.** State Medicaid pharmacy program (rebate-administration and program-integrity budget), signed by the State Medicaid Director with the pharmacy program manager; on the MCO side the chief pharmacy officer. Fee about 10% of net incremental rebates and avoided disputes (my estimate), because the state gets cash recapture rather than a new line item.
- **Problem.** The statute bars a duplicate discount: a unit gets either a 340B price or a Medicaid rebate. States use a provider-level Medicaid Exclusion File (50% of FY25 adverse HRSA findings involved duplicates/MEF), claim flags that NCPA calls largely unusable, or blunt bans: Louisiana forbids contract pharmacies from billing Medicaid for 340B-priced drugs and, from 1 Jan 2026, seeks rebates claim by claim on physician-administered drugs under CMS-2434-F. Louisiana's six MCOs each run their own PBM from 1 Oct 2025; Aetna's is CVS Caremark, the owner of Wellpartner.
- **Solution.** (1) TPAs, covered entities and pharmacies post their retroactive 340B assignments by claim. (2) The service matches each to the payer's claim. (3) It issues NCPDP reversal/rebill or state-specific adjustment files. (4) It marks the claim rebate-eligible or excluded for state and MCO rebate invoicing. (5) It stores the evidence manufacturers will ask for in disputes. Covered entities get the same record as HRSA-audit proof (it clears false MEF findings). Legal premise: the duplicate-discount ban is statutory and Louisiana shows claim-level rules are workable, but hospital groups litigate and states are exempting FQHCs (Indiana), so sell a claim-level control, not a ban.
- **Why now.** Louisiana's rules took effect Oct 2025 and Jan 2026; CMS-2434-F makes physician-administered drug rebates mandatory; H.R.1 cuts federal Medicaid by $911B over 2025-34 (KFF, read in the pool file), so states want cash; Manatt surveyed all 50 states in July 2026.
- **Wedge.** Louisiana fee-for-service (vendor: Gainwell) plus one MCO, or Indiana before its policy starts. First 10: 3 states, 4 MCOs, 3 TPAs as data partners. Offer a recapture-only contract: no fee unless rebates are recaptured.
- **Path to $100M.** Take the PhRMA-funded BRG estimate of $6.5B a year of Medicaid rebates lost to 340B in 2024 ($4.2B federal, $2.3B state) as a ceiling, not a fact. If the 15 largest Medicaid-managed-care states (about 70% of spend, my estimate) each recapture 25% of their pro-rata share: 0.7 x $6.5B x 25% = about $1.14B, and a 10% fee = about $114M. That needs 15 states, over a third of the market. If the true number is a third of BRG's, the ceiling is about $40M and the idea is dead unless it extends to Part D MFP and commercial.
- **Who does it today.** Gainwell (Louisiana fee-for-service point-of-sale), MCO PBMs (CVS Caremark, Optum Rx, Express Scripts, Prime), HRSA's Medicaid Exclusion File, Kalderos (sells to manufacturers, so it disputes the state's invoices), state audit contractors, Apexus (free guidance).
- **Why they lose.** The MCO's PBM owns the 340B pharmacy and TPA profits it would have to police; Gainwell is paid per transaction and system build, not recapture; Kalderos works for the party being invoiced; no one holds the TPA-side feed.
- **Moat.** Network economies (every TPA integrated makes every state's data better, and each state's rule pulls in more TPAs) plus switching costs inside point-of-sale and MMIS integrations.
- **Earned secret.** In the replenishment (virtual-inventory) model nobody at the counter knows whether a script is a 340B script; the administrator decides days later, after the payer has paid and the state has invoiced the manufacturer. Every "flag it at the point of sale" policy is a fiction; only a correction loop works. Also, the 340B ceiling price is AMP minus the same unit rebate amount that sets the Medicaid rebate, so the manufacturer pays the same either way and the real fight is states against hospitals. (Insider claim; verify with 10 TPA staff.)
- **First 90 days.** Reconcile 12 months of Louisiana claims against a TPA's retro assignments for a free sample; sign data MOUs with two TPAs; hire an ex-state rebate analyst; map the NCPDP reversal flows with two PBM vendors; get a counsel memo on state authority; pitch Indiana and Illinois staff.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 7.

### 2. Route Desk (insider: hospital pharmacy director)

**One-liner.** A per-unit router that decides, for every administration and fill, whether the unit should be 340B, MFP or non-340B, sitting on the hospital's virtual inventory and paid a share of the margin it preserves. It is the first decision engine for the three policies that cap 340B value at once.

- **Customer.** DSH health systems (79% of 340B dollars), then the other 340B hospitals paid under OPPS (CMS counts 1,957 eligible 340B hospitals).
- **Payer and signer.** Hospital CFO co-signed with the chief pharmacy officer; pharmacy purchasing and revenue-cycle budgets. Success fee of 15-20% of preserved or added margin against a baseline (my estimate).
- **Problem.** If CMS finalizes its July proposal, 340B-acquired separately payable Part B drugs are paid ASP minus 33.4% from 1 Jan 2027 (JG modifier), exempt hospitals use TB, everything else XX at ASP+6%. CMS estimates $4.85B of lower drug spending (other firms say about $5.7B). Its survey found 340B drugs bought at 33.4% below ASP and non-340B at 2.7% above, so the 340B route earns (discount minus 33.4%) of ASP while the non-340B route earns about +3.3%. Break-even is a discount near 37%. Roughly half of NDC volume sits below that if discounts vary about 20 points around the mean (my assumption). MFP adds the "lower of" rule (Walgreens routes all 2026 selected-drug Part D scripts to MFP), and states are carving Medicaid out. Today these choices are made by administrators and software paid on 340B volume.
- **Solution.** An NDC x payer x site x pharmacy table (HRSA ceiling price, WAC, GPO price and ASP, MFP, state Medicaid policy, contract rate) that feeds assignments into the accumulator, ties JG/TB/XX claims back to purchase evidence for audit, and trues up monthly. Legal premise: choosing non-340B stock for a unit is lawful because 340B is elective, but DSH, children's and cancer hospitals are barred from GPO purchasing of outpatient drugs (the statute stands; a D.D.C. ruling on 31 Mar 2026 vacated HRSA's 2013 replenishment limits and HRSA can re-adopt them), and CMS's final rate and exemptions may change. The engine is parameterized so a different final rate just moves the break-even.
- **Why now.** Final OPPS rule is due about 1 Nov; the remedy offset rises from 0.5% to 3.0% of the conversion factor for pre-2018 hospitals (about $2.3B in 2027); the pilot starts the same day; the AHA suit is pending.
- **Wedge.** Medicare Part B for 10 DSH systems with heavy oncology and infusion. Give each a free "Day-One map" built from public ASP and ceiling data, in exchange for a three-year success-fee contract signed before 1 January.
- **Path to $100M.** The Part B 340B payment base is about $13B ($4.85B / 0.37, my derivation), or about $12B of ASP value. If NDC discounts are spread +/-20 points, routing is worth about 10% of ASP value (about $1.2B gross; about half realizable = $0.6B). Add routing for MFP drugs (about $0.4B, my estimate) and Medicaid carve-outs (about $0.2B). About $1.2B preserved x 20% = about $240M at 100% adoption, so $100M needs about 40% of the addressable margin. **Heroic share.** The credible route is an enterprise platform for the top ~100 systems at $0.5-1M a year plus the success fee.
- **Who does it today.** Sentry (Craneware; revenue flat, FY27 guided to about $185M), Pillr (300+ hospitals, split billing and rebate readiness), Wellpartner and Walgreens 340B Complete (administrators paid on spread), Bluesight (compliance), Apexus (free education), consultancies for one-off modeling, and EHR 340B modules (general knowledge).
- **Why they lose.** Administrators and software vendors are paid on 340B volume, so telling a hospital to stop claiming a unit cuts their own revenue; audit-firm consultancies cannot take contingent fees from audit clients (general rule; verify); EHRs cannot see wholesaler and pharmacy sides.
- **Moat.** Counter-positioning plus data scale (NDC-level realized discounts across hospitals no single hospital has).
- **Earned secret.** 340B is a per-unit election, not a status. CMS's own numbers make the Medicare 340B route worth less than WAC for about half of NDCs, and nobody on the hospital's payroll is paid to say so. (Insider claim; verify with 10 340B directors.)
- **First 90 days.** Build the break-even table by NDC from public files the day the final rule drops; run it for 10 systems; sign success-fee contracts; integrate with one administrator's accumulator; get a written HRSA/CMS counsel view on payer-specific accumulation; track the AHA suit.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 7.

### 3. Cass for 340B (pattern: float and spread on payment rails; freight audit-and-pay)

**One-liner.** Freight audit-and-pay for drugs: an independent hub that audits every 340B invoice, credit-and-rebill, pharmacy fee and remittance against ceiling prices and contracts, pays the clean ones, and advances cash against contract-pharmacy remittances.

- **Why it hasn't happened here.** The 340B chain has no payer in the middle: administrators are paid on claims, wholesalers are the counterparty, hospital accounts payable is automated, and chain administrators control remittance timing. Cass Information Systems shows the model works elsewhere: $190.8M of 2025 revenue ($66.1M processing fees, $40.4M financial fees, $81.2M net interest) on about $94B of annual freight payments, a yield of about 0.2%.
- **Customer.** Mid-size and rural covered entities and FQHCs first; large systems later.
- **Payer and signer.** CFO or treasurer (advances) and the director of pharmacy purchasing or VP revenue cycle (audit). Contingency of about 25% of recovered dollars plus an advance spread.
- **Problem.** Apexus notes credit-and-rebill options "have timeframe restrictions based on invoice date"; vendors describe invoice lines stuck at WAC, restored 340B prices that never appear, and late or wrong-quantity credits. Pharmacy and administrator fees are percentage-of-spread (Wellpartner: greater of $4 or 10% of reimbursement minus ceiling, rising to 14% by year three; GAO 2018 found $23 flat, $19 + 16%, and $8 + 20% of spread structures). No one reconciles price paid to ceiling price across time.
- **Solution.** Stage 1: contingency audit of 12-24 months of invoices (EDI feed only). Stage 2: audit of pharmacy and administrator fees against contract. Stage 3: a lockbox plus advances (about 90% of expected remittance on day 3). Obligors on remittances are investment-grade chains, so credit risk sits mostly with CVS, Walgreens, Cigna, UnitedHealth and Walmart.
- **Why now.** 340B purchases are growing about 20% a year; hospitals are about to lose Medicare margin and treasuries will be tight; the rebate pilot makes payment timing a live issue for the first time.
- **Wedge.** 10 critical-access hospitals and FQHC networks; audit for free for the first 90 days and keep 25% of recoveries.
- **Path to $100M.** At Cass's 0.2% yield, $100M needs about $50B of flows, half of all 340B purchases, so float cannot carry it. Three streams, each assumption flagged: (a) invoice/credit leakage of 0.4% of $100B = $400M, recover 60% = $240M, fee 25% = $60M; (b) pharmacy/administrator fee overbilling of 2% of a ~$4-5B fee pool = about $90M recovered, fee 25% = about $22M; (c) advances of ~$0.7B average balance at 9% = about $63M gross (needs ~$0.7B of capital). About $140M at 100% share. **Heroic share (70%+), and it only works if leakage is at least 1% of purchases.** Kill criterion: if the first 20 audits find under 0.4%, stop. Adding manufacturer-side chargeback validation (a second payer on the same data) would raise the ceiling.
- **Who does it today.** Apexus (free price verification guidance), Bluesight (inventory and invoice reconciliation), Sentry, Pillr, Wellpartner and Walgreens (their own reconciliation), wholesalers' 340B support desks, trade-press-described invoice mismatch tools, manufacturers' chargeback validation (Vistex, Model N; general knowledge).
- **Why they lose.** Wholesalers cannot audit themselves; administrators are paid on claims, not price accuracy; chains hold the float; Big-4 firms audit the health systems and cannot take contingent fees from them.
- **Moat.** Process power (an audit rule set refined on every recovery) and switching costs once payables run through the hub.
- **Earned secret.** The leak is in retroactive reclassification: units are bought at WAC, then credited to 340B after accumulation, inside windows no one owns. The administrator reconciles to claims, the wholesaler to invoices, accounts payable to purchase orders. (Insider claim; the leakage rate is unpublished.)
- **First 90 days.** Audit invoices for 5 free pilots; measure leakage; get HRSA ceiling-price access as a covered-entity agent; sign a lender for the advance line; price against Wellpartner's fee schedule.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 4.

### 4. Switzerland (pattern: mandate-created compliance rails)

**One-liner.** A hospital-governed, manufacturer-funded 340B claims trust that holds the unit-level ledger behind a firewall and returns only pass/fail and payment. It is the rail the Senate bill calls an independent clearinghouse, built before the law and useful under all three political outcomes.

- **Why it hasn't happened here.** Both live platforms belong to one side. Kalderos (Truzo; "thousands" of covered entities signed; pays in about 15 days on average) litigated for the right to run rebates. Second Sight (340B ESP and the Beacon pilot platform; more than 1,000 entities submitting claims by 2022) is a BRG subsidiary, and BRG wrote PhRMA's Medicaid study. HRSA requires the manufacturer to bear platform costs, which makes the platform the manufacturer's agent by design.
- **Customer.** Manufacturers (platform and duplicate-prevention), covered entities (claims submission and rebate operations), states (Medicaid ID), and later HRSA or a Senate-designated operator.
- **Payer and signer.** Manufacturer VP Government Pricing or chief compliance officer pays per verdict and per rebate processed; covered entities pay outcome-priced operations (2-3% of rebates collected, my estimate); HRSA or CMS pays under any clearinghouse law.
- **Problem.** Everyone agrees claims-level data stops duplicates. The fight is over what the data is used for: hospitals say it will be used to deny rebates and trigger audits; manufacturers say without it they cannot stop duplicates. HHS estimates the pilot's burden at $537M a year, the AHA over $1B, and a rebate must be paid or formally denied within 10 days with claims submitted within 45.
- **Solution.** A charter owned by hospital associations and health-center networks, a data-use covenant barring audit use, a verdict API (eligible / duplicate / pay amount) for the manufacturer, and a rebate-operations desk for small entities. Start with Medicaid and MFP verdicts, which need no rebates. Legal premise: the pilot is enjoined once already (D. Maine, Dec 2025) and challenged again; the House bill would pause rebates and the Senate bill would end the pilot, so the product must work with no rebates.
- **Why now.** Pilot starts 1 Jan 2027 with 10 manufacturers (AbbVie, Amgen, Astellas, AstraZeneca, BMS, Boehringer, GSK, Merck, Pfizer, Teva); the Senate SUSTAIN bill wants an independent third-party clearinghouse contracted within a year of enactment; Cassidy's draft offers a claims-repository option.
- **Wedge.** Three state hospital associations or primary care associations as founding members, plus two manufacturers outside the approved ten.
- **Path to $100M.** (a) Top 50 manufacturers x $1-3M for verdicts and settlement = $50-150M (my estimate; no public price); (b) 2,000 hospitals x $25k + 12,000 other covered entities x $3k = about $86M; (c) a statutory user fee with a $50M base from FY2031. Total about $140-240M if it becomes the standard; needs about half the manufacturers and a third of covered entities. **Heroic share, winner-take-most.** If rebates alone: $5.5B x 2.5% = $140M at 100% share, so the pilot cannot carry it.
- **Who does it today.** Kalderos, Beacon and 340B ESP (Second Sight/BRG), Pillr, Sentry, Wellpartner, Walgreens, Apexus.
- **Why they lose.** Neutrality: neither manufacturer-funded platform will be handed claims by the AHA's members, and no hospital-side administrator will be trusted by manufacturers or paid by them.
- **Moat.** Network economies (two-sided: more covered entities means better verdicts for manufacturers) plus a cornered resource (the hospital-owned charter and any designation).
- **Earned secret.** The impasse is about data use, not data existence. A firewall that returns only a verdict and a payment satisfies both stated objections. (Hypothesis; test in 10 counsel interviews.)
- **First 90 days.** Draft the data-use covenant with AHA-side and manufacturer-side counsel; build the Medicaid and MFP verdict API on public and sample data; sign two state associations; open talks with two manufacturers not in the approved ten; monitor the AHA suit and Senate text.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 6.

### 5. Open TPA (dilemma: pharmacy-chain conflict)

**One-liner.** A 340B administrator with no pharmacy attached: flat per-claim pricing, a portable ledger so a hospital can leave CVS's or Walgreens' administrator without losing its positions, and an independent pharmacy network.

- **Customer.** Regional hospitals, FQHC networks and covered entities in states that protect contract pharmacies; independent pharmacies.
- **Payer and signer.** The covered entity's existing administrator fee line; signed by the CFO or director of pharmacy.
- **Problem.** Fees track the drug price: Wellpartner's rises from 10% to 14% of the spread by year three, and its 2023 TPA fees were $382M; Walgreens charges 8-20% of the contracted reimbursement. Five chains/PBMs (Cigna, CVS, UnitedHealth, Walgreens, Walmart) hold 77% of 239,905 contract-pharmacy relationships; CVS told entities in Dec 2017 that Wellpartner would be the exclusive administrator for its pharmacies, and Sentry and RxStrategies sued (the outcome is unverified). A Senate draft would require flat fees per unit of service, and Cassidy's draft would delink fees from price.
- **Solution.** Flat fee per claim, pharmacy-agnostic routing, neutral reporting, fee audit, and an exit service that moves virtual-inventory history. Legal premise: no law yet forces flat fees, so the product must win on price today.
- **Why now.** Contract-pharmacy locations have fallen four years in a row to 31,593 (Rite Aid is gone, chains are closing stores); 19 states enacted 22 340B bills in 2025; federal fee-delinking bills are live.
- **Wedge.** 5 FQHC networks and 5 regional hospitals in states with contract-pharmacy protection laws, with a 90-day money-back price guarantee.
- **Path to $100M.** $8 per claim (my estimate) x 12.5M claims = $100M. The national count of 340B pharmacy claims is not published (my estimate 100M +/- 50%), so that is 10-15% of claims, or roughly 25% of the chain-administrator fee pool. **Heroic share.**
- **Who does it today.** Wellpartner (CVS), Walgreens 340B Complete, Sentry (Craneware), Pillr (Water Street, bought Cooper Strategy in June 2026), RxStrategies, Apexus (free tools).
- **Why they lose.** Chain administrators earn a percentage of the spread and steer to their own stores; Sentry has no pharmacy leverage and is shrinking; Pillr is private-equity owned and still sells through hospitals.
- **Moat.** Counter-positioning. Note that a portable ledger lowers switching costs for everyone, including us.
- **Earned secret.** The lock-in is the pharmacy-to-administrator tie, not the software, and the percentage fee rises with the drug price with no extra work.
- **First 90 days.** Price against Wellpartner's published schedule; sign 3 independent-pharmacy groups; build the ledger export; run a free fee audit for 10 prospects.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 4.

### 6. JG-Day for Payers (dilemma: conflicted insurers)

**One-liner.** Give Medicare Advantage plans that do not own a 340B pharmacy business a contract-and-claims desk that uses CMS's new JG/TB/XX modifiers to pass the 340B cut through hospital contracts and audit modifier accuracy, paid a share of savings.

- **Customer.** MA plans and Medicaid MCOs outside the two conglomerates that sit in the five-chain list (UnitedHealth/Optum Rx and CVS/Aetna): Humana, Elevance, Centene, Molina, Blue plans, Kaiser (general knowledge on enrollment shares).
- **Payer and signer.** SVP provider contracting or chief payment-integrity officer; contingency of 12-15% of savings (my estimate).
- **Problem.** From 1 Jan 2027 every OPPS drug line carries JG, TB or XX, the first verifiable 340B flag. MA contracts that reference OPPS rates may or may not pass the cut through; MA plans may try to. Hospitals self-report the modifier, and a JG costs them about 37%, so there is a standing incentive to report XX. Plans have no hospital-level 340B knowledge.
- **Solution.** Classify each hospital contract's drug language; propose amendments requiring the modifiers and audit rights; edit claims; score each hospital's 340B likelihood from OPAIS child-site registration, DSH status and HCPCS mix; audit XX lines; recover. Legal premise: MA contracts are private and federal law preempts most state rules on MA payment, but state anti-discrimination laws on 340B pharmacy reimbursement (19 states enacted 22 340B bills in 2025) may reach hospital-administered drugs in commercial contracts; get counsel opinions state by state.
- **Why now.** The modifier regime starts 1 Jan 2027; plans' MA benchmark lags FFS cuts, so their margin is squeezed first.
- **Wedge.** Two mid-size MA plans, contract analysis for their top 20 340B hospitals, before the final rule.
- **Path to $100M.** FFS 340B Part B payments are about $13B (derived above), so MA at 54/46 of enrollment is about $15B (my estimate). Full pass-through at 37% is $5.6B; realized 30% in two years = $1.7B; a 12-15% fee = $200-250M. $100M needs 40-50% of that pool, i.e. winning roughly the four largest non-conflicted plans. **Heroic share.**
- **Who does it today.** Plan in-house contracting and payment-integrity vendors (Cotiviti, Zelis, Optum Payment Integrity; general knowledge, not searched). Molina, Moda and others already publish missing-modifier policies for 340B claims.
- **Why they lose.** Vendors owned by conglomerates will not police their parents' pharmacy profits; a modifier edit is a feature, not a service; none has a hospital-level 340B model.
- **Moat.** Counter-positioning plus a growing 340B-likelihood dataset.
- **Earned secret.** The rate matters less than the audit right: the modifier is hospital-reported, so whoever can check it controls the savings.
- **First 90 days.** Read 50 hospital-plan contracts for OPPS language; build the likelihood score from OPAIS and public cost reports; sign two plans on contingency; watch the final rule.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 5.

### 7. Specialty Door (shock: PBM reform and chain closures)

**One-liner.** A shared, accredited, PBM-credentialed specialty pharmacy that health systems use as their own front door (their patients, their 340B account, flat fee per script), timed to the Medicare any-willing-pharmacy rules arriving 2027-2029.

- **Customer.** The 40-60 health systems with the largest 340B specialty volume (specialty is 61.9% of purchase dollars).
- **Payer and signer.** Chief pharmacy officer plus CFO; the budget is the contract-pharmacy and administrator fees they pay today. In Drug Channels' illustrative $5,000 specialty script, CVS's fee is $624 and Wellpartner's $497.
- **Problem.** Over 70% of health systems say their specialty pharmacy is frozen out by payers; limited-distribution drugs block about 30% of fill opportunity; one insurer wants two years of dispensing history and dual accreditation. So 340B specialty scripts flow to PBM-owned pharmacies that also charge the fees.
- **Solution.** One national entity with its own NPI, dual accreditation, network contracts and limited-distribution access, operating under each system's brand against the system's 340B account. Legal premise: the federal PBM reform signed 3 Feb 2026 includes Part D delinking and any-willing-pharmacy rules (effective 2028-2029 per one summary) and CMS is writing Part D pharmacy contracting standards; no commercial ownership ban was found.
- **Why now.** Reform enacted; Rite Aid gone and chain store closures; manufacturers' single-pharmacy policies push entities toward in-house or designated pharmacies.
- **Wedge.** 3 systems with Medicare-heavy oncology and immunology volume; Part D first.
- **Path to $100M.** The five chains earned nearly $3B of gross profit from 340B in 2023 (likely $4B+ now, my extrapolation), about 5M high-value scripts at ~$900 each (my estimate). At $250 a script, 400,000 scripts = $100M, about 8% of chain volume or 10 systems x 40,000 scripts. **Needs PBM networks to open, which only Medicare Part D will do by 2029.**
- **Who does it today.** Trellis Rx (now CPS Solutions), Shields Health Solutions (Walgreens-owned), Pillr, PBM-owned CVS Specialty, Accredo and Optum Specialty, health-system in-house pharmacies.
- **Why they lose.** PBM pharmacies earn the fee they would have to give up; Shields belongs to a chain that is itself a contract pharmacy; Trellis builds one pharmacy per system and so never aggregates network credentials.
- **Moat.** Scale economies in network credentials plus a cornered resource (accreditation and dispensing history).
- **Earned secret.** "Frozen out" is a contracting-entity problem, not a pharmacy problem: a shared entity clears volume thresholds no single hospital can.
- **First 90 days.** Interview 20 chief pharmacy officers; map each system's script volume by payer; line up a licensed, accredited acquisition target; request CMS's pharmacy-contracting RFI responses.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 4.

### 8. Pigeonhole (shock: MFP collides with 340B)

**One-liner.** A duplicate-discount floor for the Inflation Reduction Act: using only data manufacturers already hold (340B chargebacks by ship-to pharmacy and the Medicare Transaction Facilitator feed), prove for each pharmacy and month the minimum number of units that received both MFP and 340B, and recover them.

- **Customer.** Manufacturers with selected drugs outside the approved rebate pilot (J&J, Novo Nordisk and Novartis are not in the 1 Oct list as reported by Becker's, which may not be exhaustive) and, from 2027, every manufacturer with a selected drug.
- **Payer and signer.** Chief pricing officer or VP government pricing and IRA operations; fee 10-15% of duplicates recovered or avoided (my estimate).
- **Problem.** The statute requires the lower of MFP or the 340B price but creates no process to stop both; CMS declined to prevent it; pharmacies cannot flag 340B claims; HRSA's pilot is the workaround but covers 10 manufacturers.
- **Solution.** For pharmacy P, drug N, month M, take units bought on 340B accounts (A), units bought on non-340B accounts (B, from wholesaler transaction data) and units dispensed to MFP-eligible Part D patients (U, from the MTF feed). At least max(0, U - B) of the MFP units must have come from 340B stock. Example: if 60% of a pharmacy's units are 340B and 65% of scripts are Part D, at least 25% of units are duplicates. Output an evidence packet, a pharmacy attestation request, and a recovery path. Legal premise: the MFP statute obliges manufacturers to pay refunds promptly, so unilateral withholding is risky; recover through consented offsets, CMS's dispute process, HRSA ADR or settlements, after a counsel opinion.
- **Why now.** MFP started 1 Jan 2026 (10 drugs), adds 15 in 2027, 15 in 2028 including Part B, and 20 a year after (statute; general knowledge).
- **Wedge.** One non-pilot manufacturer's 2026 selected drug, 12 months of its 340B chargebacks and MTF data.
- **Path to $100M.** Exposure: HRSA's ~5.5% of 340B sales is about $5.5B at ceiling prices; assume 40% goes to Part D patients, giving about $2.2B of duplicates for 2026, growing with the 2027 list to $4-5B (my estimates). Recover 50%, fee 10-15% = $100-375M at 100% of manufacturers. **Needs 30-40% share.**
- **Who does it today.** Kalderos (claims-level duplicate prevention; says Truzo can address MFP overlap), Beacon/340B ESP (BRG), the HRSA rebate pilot, CMS's MTF, manufacturers' in-house teams, IQVIA's 340B analytics (general knowledge).
- **Why they lose.** Claims-level tools need covered-entity data, consent and months of integration; a lower bound needs none of it. Chargeback operations (trade) and IRA operations (government pricing) sit in different departments, so no one joins the two files.
- **Moat.** Cornered resource (data-licensing from manufacturers and wholesalers) plus process power.
- **Earned secret.** Manufacturers already receive the two files that bound the problem; the pigeonhole argument needs no patient data. (Hypothesis; test on real files.)
- **First 90 days.** Get a counsel opinion on recovery paths; license one manufacturer's chargeback and MTF files; run 12 months of the bound; brief the manufacturer's government-pricing head; compare against Kalderos output.
- **Outside-the-box score.** 7.
