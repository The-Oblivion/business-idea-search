# Round 2 lessons: what killed round 1's finalists (read before generating, judging or gating)

Round 1 generated 1,340 ideas, then ran a 3-lens tournament, a light competitor check (6-12 searches), breeding, and deep validation of the top 12 (research dossier, 3 adversarial critics, a rewrite, a second critic and 3 judges). **None of the 12 scored above 3.3/10.** Five died, and seven came back wounded. They all failed in the same ways:

1. **The real market was small.**
   - Every refined idea's own bottom-up ceiling was $0.1-2M a year: a niche "desk" serving a few hundred buyers.
   - Pitch-level sizes were inflated. "$3-10M" became "$45-210k of fees" once someone counted the reachable buyers.
   - Upside scored 2-3/10 for all 12.
2. **There were hidden incumbents and free substitutes.** In 11 of 12, deep research found an existing competitor, in-house team or free alternative that the light check called a "gap":
   - GreenLancer (lender-paid solar completion)
   - Alvarez & Marsal "Value Assurance Programs" (home-value guarantees)
   - F&I Express (a lender-to-administrator refund exchange, built in 2017)
   - Allied RefundPlus and Express Recoveries
   - SSRS (verified survey panels)
   - OEM in-house labs (inverter model validation)
   - Novartis's in-house China due-diligence team
   - a free, NSF-funded university smoke-testing project
   - free state-provided tools
3. **Wrong payer, or no budget line.** The user of the product isn't the one who pays (for example, the insurance carrier, not the defense lawyer, controls the legal budget), or nothing forces the spend.
4. **Legal premise errors.**
   - Federal Power Act 202(c) shields generators run under DOE emergency orders.
   - Regulators (for example NY DFS) treat pay-on-sale home-value guarantees as insurance.
   - Selling a "privileged" number that defeats its own privilege.
5. **The price exceeded the value.** For example, a fee per loan that was higher than the expected losses it prevents.
6. **The why-now was already used up.** The bankruptcy wave had passed, the new rule exempted the target segment, or real demand wouldn't arrive until after 2028.
7. **Shrinking to survive.** Every time a critic hit an idea, the rewrite narrowed it, until it was defensible but tiny.

## Rules for round 2

- **Start from a big pool of money.** Every idea needs a credible **bottom-up path to at least $100M in annual revenue**: the number of reachable payers × a realistic annual spend per payer, with sources. A TAM hand-wave doesn't count. If the path needs heroic share (over 20% of a pool), say so.
- **Assume someone already does it until searching proves otherwise.** Before claiming a gap, name who does this today: venture-funded startups (Crunchbase, YC, TechCrunch), incumbents (read their product pages), in-house teams, and free or public substitutes.
- **The payer must already have a budget line**, or a specific mandate or shock must force new spend. Name the person who signs.
- **Check the legal premise** if the idea depends on a law, rule, privilege or regulatory classification.
- **Insider knowledge is still the best source of non-obvious insight**, since round 1's insider personas beat everything else. Use it as the **wedge into a big pool**, not as the whole business.
- **Services are fine as a wedge**, but there must be a path to something that scales: software, a network, a data asset, a balance sheet, or a standard.
- **Don't shrink to survive.** If an idea can't be both defensible and big, call it dead rather than narrowing it into a boutique.
- **Avoid round 1's clichés** (`cliche_list.md`): generic "AI for X" copilots, AI SDRs, note-takers, AI tutors, and the like.
