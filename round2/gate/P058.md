# Gate report: pool P058, No Surprises Act IDR awards (payers and self-funded employers)

Gate date: 2026-10-14. Stance: skeptical by default. 8 ideas checked with roughly 3-6 searches each plus fetches of the CMS readout, the CMS implementation timeline and law-firm summaries of the IDR Operations Rule. Result: 4 kill, 4 weak, 0 strong. **No picks.**

## What the pool research changed (applies to every idea)

1. **The buyer channel is already owned.** Zelis says it serves more than 750 payers and launched an AI "NSA Claim Advantage" in June 2026 covering intake, open negotiation and IDR briefs (https://www.techtarget.com/healthcarepayers/news/366645008/As-No-Surprises-Act-rules-tighten-Zelis-expands-AI-role-in-dispute-process, https://medcitynews.com/2026/06/zelis-unveils-solution-to-tackle-payer-idr-challenges/). Claritev claims to have handled nearly 50% of federal IDR claims in 2025, 270,000 arbitrations plus 400,000 post-payment disputes negotiated (company-reported, https://briefglance.com/companies/claritev-corporation/pulses/64121). Daffodil launched a payer/TPA dispute product on 2026-07-30 (https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/payers/daffodil-health-unveils-ai-powered-payer-platform-managing-nsa-disputes). Any idea that sells to TPAs and payers is selling into Zelis and Claritev's installed base.
2. **The pool is a policy flaw, and the flaw is under attack.** Pallone's Lower Premiums, Faster Payments Act would end IDR for services on or after 2028-01-01 and replace it with a recalculated QPA, with annual audits (https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/10/rep-pallone-proposes-replacement-of-no-surprises-act-arbitration). Holland & Knight rates near-term passage as limited (Pallone is in the minority) but says structural change is more likely in 2027. A 5-7 year revenue ceiling for any idea that lives off arbitration volume has to be haircut for this.
3. **Several "why now" items are not live yet.** The IDR Gateway features that matter (open-negotiation notices, entity selection, eligibility review) are expected from Spring 2027, and each applies 90 days after its notice (https://www.cms.gov/files/document/Federal-IDR-Operations-Implementation-Timeline.pdf). Sidley says the portal takes about 24 months to fully implement (https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2026/06/us-government-finalizes-no-surprises-act-independent-dispute-resolution-operations-rule). The admin fee did fall to $15 per party, effective 2026-06-11 (https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/alerts-practies-hltc-federal-independent-dispute-resolution-operations-rule-under-no-surprises-act).
4. **Awards cannot be enforced in court in at least two circuits.** The Fifth Circuit (cert denied 2026-01-12) and the Second Circuit (2026-09-17, East Coast Advanced Plastic Surgery v. Cigna) hold there is no private right of action to enforce IDR awards (https://www.hfma.org/payment-reimbursement-and-managed-care/no-surprises-act-idr-enforcement-uncertain-after-supreme-court-declines-review/, https://www.mondaq.com/no-surprises-here-second-circuit-holds-idr-awards-should-be-enforced-administratively-and-not-through-private-plaintiff-lawsuits/1850438).
5. **The size figures hold up.** About $15B of 2025 payouts, 2.5-2.6M disputes, and about $1.3B of entity fees in 2025 (https://www.healthcaredive.com/news/no-surprises-act-disputes-increase-arbiters-progress-speed-backlog-cms/826011/, https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/10/rep-pallone-proposes-replacement-of-no-surprises-act-arbitration). Three filers hold more than three-fourths of 2025 resolved lines: Radiology Partners 30%, HaloMD 27%, TeamHealth 20% (https://chir.georgetown.edu/spending-on-idr-process-pushes-no-surprises-act-costs-to-more-than-22-4-billion-over-just-four-years/). The money is real. It is held by filers and incumbents, not by a neutral middle layer.

## Evidence notes per idea

**1. Pre-Op Lock.** Competitors and substitutes: payer network teams doing single case agreements by hand; Turquoise Clear Contracts (SCA, retrospective OON agreements); Solum; Zelis and Claritev (own the payer channel); eviCore and Carelon UM. I found no vendor selling an ancillary-network check at authorization, which is consistent with the idea's claim, but the lever fails for the reason in the table, not because of a competitor. Legal premise unchecked at the CFR level: whether IONM is an NSA ancillary. Georgetown data showing neuro disputes at 5% of lines and $2.02B in awards implies the Departments treat it as covered. Plans cannot deny the in-network facility's surgery to force in-network ancillaries without hitting the NSA's protections for the patient; the lever that works is hospital contracting, which needs consulting, not a platform.

**2. Sponsor Desk.** Legal: delegating to a TPA does not shift fiduciary responsibility, and each self-funded plan gets its own IDR registry number once the registry is live (https://www.hubinternational.com/en-us/hub-resources/employee-benefits-compliance-bulletins/2026/08/no-surprises-act-final-rule-changes-the-idr-process/). The idea's "portal visibility" for sponsors is not confirmed: the rule requires payers to register, and I found no sponsor-facing portal access. HIPAA access runs through the plan document and the TPA contract, which the TPA controls. Free substitutes: CMS public use files, TPA IDR reports, broker analyses. The cost figures in the idea ($100-135 per self-funded life per year) are the idea's own arithmetic, not a sourced number.

**3. Lockstep.** TeamHealth says it prefers in-network at fair rates and has approached about 15 employers, which supports the existence of a small zone, but the only disclosed deal (Teachers Health Trust, 35,000 members, $500k saved in fees) has no rate terms. Antitrust (buyer coalition, messenger model) was not tested and is moot given the economics.

**4. Float.** Legal premise is the weakest in the set, as the idea admits: assignment of proceeds, true sale versus loan, usury, champerty, HIPAA. The enforcement rulings above make it worse. I found no evidence of anyone financing IDR awards and treat that as weak evidence of a gap, not proof.

**5. Cold Open.** Open-negotiation-first is already how payer vendors resolve volume. A neutral that both sides pay is novel, but the demand test (would a filer with an 85% win rate accept a neutral's range) has no evidence behind it.

**6. Guild.** Provider-side intermediaries are where the big revenue sits, but that is the segment under insurer suits, ineligibility crackdowns in the Operations Rule (five-business-day information requests, default closures), and the 2028 cutoff in Pallone's bill. The idea itself flags the reputational risk of helping providers win more.

**7. Plumb Line.** The TiC final rule is dated 2026-10-05 and moves to network-level reporting (https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/transparency-coverage-final-rules-cms-9882-f). I could not confirm the quarterly cadence and attestation claims from a primary source. TMA III held ghost rates and categorical exclusion of bonus and risk-share payments unlawful and upheld exclusion of single-case agreements. This is the idea I would revisit if the Departments issue a recompute deadline.

**8. Gavel.** Certification itself is open on a rolling basis with no stated cap (https://www.cms.gov/nosurprises/Help-resolve-payment-disputes/apply). It is not the barrier. CMS certified 13 entities in 2022 and the Oct 1 2026 CMS convening listed 17 active entities, so entry has happened (Dane Street was certified in January 2026). Published 2026 entity fee ranges are $200-$840 single and $268-$1,173 batched, with Dane Street at $595 and $695 (https://www.cbiz.com/insights/article/january-2026-regulatory-legislative-update, https://site.danestreet.com/federal/idr-fees-and-payment/); the idea's $425-$800 range does not match the CMS range. Fee pool arithmetic in the idea ($1.2B net of admin fees) is close to the reported $1.3B.

## Picks

**None.** Reasons, tied to the lessons file:

- **No bottom-up path to $100M survives.** The best ceiling is Plumb Line at about $35M, and its sizing comp (FAIR Health, about $37M of revenue) argues against it. Sponsor Desk needs Castlight-scale distribution at a $5 per life price.
- **Hidden incumbents again.** Zelis (750+ payers), Claritev (about half of claims, company-reported), Daffodil, HaloMD, Pivotal, Turquoise, PayerPrice and FAIR Health each cover a slice of the eight ideas.
- **Legal and why-now errors.** Entity selection (Gavel), award enforceability (Float), portal timing (Cold Open, Gavel), and the 2028 cutoff bill (all but Plumb Line) each break a premise.
- **Shrinking to survive.** The only versions that look defensible are narrower: a sponsor-audit service, a QPA recompute for independent TPAs, an IONM network-gap consultancy. Each is a sub-$30M boutique, which the lessons file says to call dead rather than narrow.

What would reopen the pool: (1) Congress restores a statutory benchmark without ending third-party validation, which would make Plumb Line a standard rather than a data vendor; (2) the Departments issue a dated QPA recompute rule, which creates a forced compliance spend for hundreds of TPAs; (3) evidence that an employer-side buyer will pay per member for IDR oversight outside a consultant's bundle.

## Gaps in this check

- WSJ, Becker's and Health Affairs pages returned 403 on direct fetch; figures from them come from search summaries and from Holland & Knight and Georgetown, which I could open.
- Claritev, Zelis and Pivotal numbers are company-reported.
- I did not find Zelis or Claritev pricing, entity-level market shares from the CMS public use files, or a CMS-published open-negotiation settlement rate.
- The CFR text of the IDR entity certification standards (45 CFR 149.510(e)) was not read.
