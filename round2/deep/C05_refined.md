# C05 Refined: Anchor-Funded Independent Foundry Platform (formerly "The Non-GE Foundry")

Written 2026-10-18 after three "fatal" critiques. "(V)" = I opened the source in this pass. "(C)" = taken from a critic's fetched evidence, not re-opened. "(S)" = search summary only. "(mine)" = my arithmetic or assumption.

## 1. Verdict

**Alive, but only as a different business, and not as a solo-founder startup.** The original pitch is dead: a greenfield plant, a consortium of rival OEMs, a 10-year take-or-pay, open-book cost-plus pricing and neutrality as the product. All three critics were right about it, and I concede every one of those pieces below.

What survives is an operator-led, OEM-anchored acquisition platform. It buys qualified independent superalloy casting capacity, then grows it with the customers' own reservation payments. It keeps a credible $100M+ revenue path because revenue is bought on day one rather than built over 5 years.

Three plain limits:
- It is a capital-allocation business with an operating edge, not an insight a founder tests for free. It needs roughly $100-175M of other people's equity and a proven casting operator.
- My rough odds of reaching $100M revenue within 7 years are about 5% without an operator and an equity partner in hand on day one, and about 12% with both (mine).
- If the two-week test below fails, the idea is dead and should be dropped, not narrowed.

## 3. The refined business

**One-liner.** Buy a qualified, sub-scale independent superalloy caster that several OEMs already depend on, and expand it on those OEMs' own reservation payments, so their capacity is contractually protected from conflicted owners.

**Why this answers the "product already exists" critique.** It does. Doncasters says it is "the only competitor ... able to provide OEMs with dedicated capacity" and that customers fund up to 80% of capital investment (V). Howmet says new capacity is largely committed for 2027-2029 (V), and its CFO says capex is backed by customer contracts (C). That proves buyers pay for dedicated capacity. It also shows the two suppliers doing it are full, so demand spills over. The refined business is a third and fourth place to put that same customer money. It is not a new product.

**Target profile (Plant A).**
- Qualified investment caster, $30-70M revenue, 150-350 staff, nickel and cobalt superalloy, equiaxed plus some DS.
- At least two non-captive buyers in its top five customers.
- Furnace-limited, so expansion capex converts straight into revenue.
- Owned by a seller who values a customer-endorsed buyer: a conglomerate carve-out, a family owner, or a PE exit.
- Comparable: GF sold Precicast (about 600 staff, 3 sites, CHF 220M cash proceeds, closing end-2026) to CPP in July 2026 (V). Sales were not disclosed. At $200-300k revenue per head that is about 1.4-2.4x revenue (mine, low confidence).

**Payer and signer.** The VP Supply Chain or Chief Procurement Officer signs the LTA. Blades-and-vanes sourcing or the supplier-capacity lead owns it. The CFO signs the reservation payment. The budget line is supplier capacity and tooling investment, which is the same line that funds Doncasters (V). Siemens Energy and GE Vernova both have named roles for this (C). A second payer is the equity provider: an infrastructure or PE fund, or an independent-sponsor partner.

**Customers (reachable payers, about 20-25).** Three groups, each with a different reason to pay:
- Non-big-3 IGT makers (Ansaldo, Baker Hughes, Solar) and dual-sourcing demand from Siemens Energy and GE Vernova. Siemens hires for "capacity expansion investments ... and dual sourcing" (C).
- Aeroengine OEMs short of capacity, including GE. The old "no-captive policy" is gone: an independent that refuses GE's money is a worse independent.
- Unmanned and missile propulsion: Kratos plans 3,000 Spartan engines in 2027 with a quoted capacity of 10,000 a year (V). Its casting source for advanced turbine parts is a development contract with Rangeview, value undisclosed (V). This buyer class has no legacy incumbent LTAs.

**First product.** The approved part numbers the acquired plant already ships, so revenue is real at closing. The first funded expansion is non-rotating hot-section hardware (vanes, nozzle segments, shrouds, small-engine turbine nozzles and wheels) in equiaxed and DS alloys. I assume this has a shorter qualification path than SX blades (mine, not verified). DS and SX capability is added later, with qualification funded by the customer, which Doncasters says is the norm (C).

**Contract an anchor signs (bilateral only).**
- 5-7 year LTA on named part families.
- Reservation payment funding 60-80% of the line's capex, credited against invoices over the term.
- Volume floor of 70-80% of reserved capacity, with a make-whole payment of the unamortized balance on early termination. This closes the "terminate for convenience" gap that Doncasters admits to (V).
- Metal, energy and labor pass-through on published indices, as in Doncasters' LTAs (V).
- Supply-assurance rights: ring-fenced line, tooling and process-data escrow, step-in rights if the supplier fails.
- Right of first offer or veto if the plant is sold to a competitor of the customer. This is the old "neutrality" idea turned into a contract term.
- Foreign OEM anchors (Siemens Energy, Mitsubishi, Ansaldo) are contract-only. No equity and no board seats, to avoid FOCI and CFIUS problems (mine, inference).

**Pricing.** Market pricing with index pass-through. No cost-plus, no neutrality discount, so scarcity pricing is kept. Target EBITDA 18-22%. Doncasters' adjusted margin was 16.5% in FY25, with segment margins of 18-22% (C). The anchor's payment buys priority and security of supply.

**Sequencing.**
1. Months 0-1: the two-week test (section 7) and recruiting the operator.
2. Months 1-4: screen targets, collect non-binding anchor term sheets with dollar ranges, soft-circle equity.
3. Months 4-12: close Plant A with anchor amendments signed at closing and conditional on it.
4. Months 12-30: first anchor-funded line. Brownfield shell lines take up to 18 months, so capacity lands about 2028-29, alongside incumbents rather than after them.
5. Years 2-5: bolt-ons and a core and shell capability, because Doncasters self-supplies alloy and cores for the same reason (C).
6. Greenfield only after two plants make cash, and only with a signed 7-year-plus reservation covering at least 70% of capex.

## 5. Bottom-up market and revenue

**Pools (verified or sourced).**
- IGT airfoils: about $2.6B (rough, from Howmet gas turbine sales of about $1.28B annualized at over 50% share (C)).
- Aeroengine hot-section castings: about $8-10B or more (C).
- BNEF projects OEM turbine output up more than 50% to about 102 GW by 2030 (C). On the IGT pool that implies about $1.3B a year of added airfoil demand (mine).
- Rystad: 2025 orders about 100 GW, 30% above what OEMs could deliver, "that gap hasn't closed", and one supplier holds over 80% of single-crystal blade supply (V). Hot-section parts are "widely reported" to be among the tightest constraints and order books run toward 2030 (V).

**Reachable payers times realistic spend.**
- Non-big-3 IGT lane: about 10-15% of the IGT pool, so $260-390M (mine). A realistic 10% share is $25-40M.
- Overflow and dual-sourcing from the big IGT OEMs: 3-4% of added demand is about $40-50M (mine).
- Aeroengine OEMs at about 1-1.5% of an $8-10B pool: $80-150M.
- Unmanned and missile propulsion: $15-60M. Casting content per engine is my assumption and is unverified.
- Six to eight anchors at $12-25M each give $100-160M. $100M of revenue is about 1% of the combined pool, so the obstacle is access and capital, not share.

**Median case: $55M of revenue in year 6, conditional on closing a first deal.** Plant A at $45M, plus about $10M from the first anchor-funded line, with no second acquisition. The unconditional median is $0, because most attempts never close a first deal.

**Upside case: about $200M in year 7.** Named assumptions:
1. Three acquisitions at 2x revenue or less: Plant A $45M, a bolt-on of $40M in year 3, and a $55M carve-out or divestiture in year 5.
2. At least five anchor deals averaging $15-20M each, with 65% customer-funded capex at about $1.0 of capex per $1 of added revenue (Howmet Wichita Falls works out near 1.0-1.1 (mine)).
3. A $15M unmanned or small-engine line.
4. The turbine order cycle holds through about 2030. BNEF warns of overcapacity if demand softens (C).
5. Capital: about $340M of uses, of which $52M is customer-funded, about $115M is debt at 4x EBITDA, and $130-175M is equity (mine).
6. Blended 18% EBITDA, roughly $36M. Exit or IPO at 10-12x EBITDA is assumed. Doncasters trades far above that, so this is conservative, and the exit still depends on the cycle.

**Honest odds (mine).** About a 35% chance to close a first deal within 24 months if the operator and equity partner are in hand. About a 35% chance of reaching $100M after that. That is roughly 12% overall, and about 5% without those two on day one.

## 6. Competitors, edge and moat

**Who does it today.**
- Howmet: capex backed by customer contracts, new capacity largely committed for 2027-2029 (V).
- Doncasters: four partnerships worth more than $200M of added revenue at full run rate, possibly by 2029 (V).
- Chromalloy: the Siemens-exclusive Tampa JV (C).
- PCC.
- CPP under GE, which is buying Precicast.
- Pursuit, Arcline and Fabri.
- Rangeview.
- Captives: Pratt Asheville, Safran Rennes, Rolls-Royce Rotherham, SpaceX Bastrop.

**Edge, stated honestly.** It is thin against Pursuit, Arcline and CPP, who have capital and process. The edge has three parts:
- Deal flow sourced through customers who would rather not see a conflicted owner.
- Customer-funded expansion, so less equity per dollar of revenue.
- Willingness to serve every buyer.

None of these is a technology edge.

**Moat (7 Powers).**
- Switching costs, from OEM approvals that take years. These arrive on day one by acquisition. This is the incumbents' moat, bought rather than invented.
- Cornered resource: approved part numbers, furnace slots, the operator bench and dedicated customer-funded lines. Years 2-3.
- Counter-positioning, narrowly: change-of-control rights make the plant safe from owners that compete with its customers. Real against OEM-owned captives only, weak against PE-owned peers.
- Scale economies in melt, cores and NDT: year 4 onward, at three or more plants.

## 7. Riskiest assumption and the two-week test

**Riskiest assumption.** At least one non-captive buyer will sign a dollar-denominated, multi-year capacity reservation, with make-whole, that funds a line at a qualified caster owned by a non-incumbent. Customer money follows the qualified asset and a protective contract, not the incumbent's name. No source I or any critic found shows this happening.

**Two-week test (cost: near zero to a few thousand dollars).**
1. Days 1-3: build a list of 12 qualified independent superalloy casters, excluding those owned by Howmet, PCC, CPP, Doncasters, Pursuit and Arcline. Note each one's likely top customers.
2. Days 3-12: reach 20 supplier-capacity, blades-and-vanes sourcing and procurement leads at non-captive buyers (Ansaldo, Baker Hughes, Solar, Siemens Energy and GE Vernova dual-sourcing teams, Honeywell, Kratos, Anduril, Williams) through ex-employee referrals or paid expert calls. Also reach 3-5 sell-side advisors who ran recent aerospace casting sales.
3. In each call, show a one-page term sheet (section 3) and ask three things. Do you have a part family whose only qualified source is at risk on capacity or ownership? If an independent owner with committed capital bought that source, what reservation payment and minimum term would you sign? Who signs, and how long does it take?

**Pass:** at least 3 of about 10 responding buyers name a dollar range of $10M or more and a term of 5 years or more, and at least 1 agrees to mark up a draft LOI. At least 2 advisors confirm 3 or more qualified mid-size targets could trade at 2.5x revenue or less within 24 months.

**Kill:** one buyer or fewer gives a number, or the consistent answer is "only through our existing LTA partners" or "we would buy it ourselves". Then drop the idea.

## 8. Pre-mortem (it is 2028 and this is dead)

1. **No sellable asset at a sane price.** The consolidation wave (Precicast to CPP, Aeromet to Pursuit, ACC to Arcline) removes the mid-size targets. Probably the most likely cause. Only partly fixable.
2. **Anchors buy rather than fund.** Siemens Energy and GE Vernova have shown they prefer to own supply (V, C). Fixable only if the multi-customer character of the target makes single-OEM ownership unattractive to the caster's other buyers.
3. **Equity partners want a proven operator CEO.** The founder ends up as deal originator with a small stake. Acceptable only if the founder values the structure over control.
