# C14 refined: Covenant Parks after three critiques (pool P017)

Dated 2026-10-19. Verdict: **dead as a $100M company.** The strongest honest version is a sound strategy for an experienced manufactured-home-community operator. It is not a defensible company a new founder can build to $100M, and I am not narrowing it into a boutique to keep it alive.

Tags: [S] read by me this run; [C1], [C2], [C3] taken from critic 1, 2 or 3's primary reads, which I did not re-open; [D] my arithmetic; [A] my assumption; [M] from memory.

## 1. What I checked myself

- **Model rebuild.** I rebuilt the dossier's levered-return model (per site, interest-only debt, 10-year hold, 2% buy and 1.5% sell costs). With 7.2% debt and capex included, a market owner (5.5% rent growth, 3.5% expense growth) earns 17-19%. A 4% cap earns 11.6-13.7%. A CPI-linked ~3% cap earns 3.1-9.6%, depending on expense growth. My numbers run about 1 point above critic 3's, so the conclusions below are not driven by pessimism [D].
- **NY MHAP term sheet (Apr 2025, read in full).** Eligible applicants are "not-for-profit and for-profit owners of existing MHCs, as well as mission-driven affordable housing organizations (whether not-for-profit or for-profit) seeking to acquire an MHC". Applicants need experience owning affordable units "or be able to hire/partner with an entity that has relevant expertise". Acquisition cost is capped at the lesser of price or as-is appraisal, and a regulatory agreement is required. This settles the dispute between the dossier and critic 1: for-profits are eligible, with a partner. Critic 1 is right that the program is small (about $23.8M in total, per critic 1's NYHA source [C1]) and that the appraisal cap stops it funding an overbid [S].
- **FG Communities, Inc. Form C-AR (read; FY2025 figures unaudited).** Formed July 2022; stated purpose is to "preserve and improve affordable housing" by owning manufactured-home communities. Over 80 communities and about 3,100 sites in NC, SC and VA (April 2026). Rental income was $2.27M in 2023, $5.0M in 2024 and $9.6M in 2025. At end-2025: total assets $200M, notes payable $84.8M (about 42% of assets), paid-in capital $123M. Its 2024 notes ran from 3.25% (credit union) to 8% (bank). The CEO has overseen about 95 communities and 3,750 sites since 2017, including as president and CFO of Manufactured Housing Properties Inc. Press releases put the listed parent at 84 properties and 3,500+ sites (April 2026), and critic 1 cites 103 communities and 4,000+ sites by October [C1]. **This is the exact niche, already occupied, with the mission label and no rent cap.** Pace: about 850-1,000 sites a year and $4-5M of new rental revenue a year. Implied rent is roughly $3.5-4.5k a site a year [D, yearly site counts not disclosed].
- **Havenpark (search summary).** More than 100 communities and 30,000+ sites; average community about 270 sites; 95% of targets are 100-500 sites. A 2026 Mesa, AZ deal was 493 sites for $44M, about $89k a site. The platforms that reached tens of thousands of sites did it with big parks.
- **NOAH/impact capital (search summary).** Minnesota's NOAH Impact Fund raised $32.5M and $33.5M in two pools since 2017. Minneapolis's NOAH loans are 0-1% deferred. A Filene analysis says impact funds accept lower returns, and most focus on new development, not preservation. I found nothing on manufactured housing. The "middle bucket" of 7-8% affordability capital has no scaled MHC example.
- **ICS.** My own search did not surface the ICS shutdown. I rely on critic 2's primary read of ImpactAlpha (Aug 2026): one 84-unit deal, a planned $30M fund, board shutdown in 2025 [C2]. The ROC USA site confirms ICS was created in 2023 to buy and break up portfolios.

## 2. The best restructure, and what it fixes

The original sold one heavy covenant to three audiences. The critics showed nobody pays for it. The restructure splits it:

| Element | Original | Refined |
|---|---|---|
| Covenant | 4% / CPI-linked cap, resident equity glide path, discounted buyout, for everyone | **Tier A (standard, free):** 5% annual ceiling on in-place residents, 90 days' notice, no junk fees, resident right to buy at a pre-agreed appraisal formula (separate recorded instrument, not in the lease). **Tier B (deep):** 3-4% cap plus equity credit, only where a payer funds it |
| Who pays | Sellers (discount), LPs (lower return) | Tier A: nobody (priced to parity). Tier B: state programs (MHAP, Minnesota-style grants), residents' associations, mission LP slices. Tier B capped at about 25% of sites |
| Wedge | 50-150 site parks, NY and Maine plus one uncapped state | Same states, plus NJ, WA, OR, MA where caps or purchase rights exist. 40-150 site parks, preferring municipal water and sewer |
| Debt | Agency tenant-protection loans | Community banks and credit unions (FG Communities' route), plus seller notes. Agency debt only after an operating partner satisfies Freddie's two-year sponsor rule |
| Equity | $290-320M from pensions and insurers | 1031/DST and retail preferred (FG Communities' route), family offices, deal-by-deal syndication. Seasoned operator as co-founder |
| First product | A fund | One park with a recorded Tier A covenant and an MHAP infrastructure loan, bought with a partner |
| Moat | Counter-positioning against REITs | Tier B eligibility and track record with state programs; resident-association relationships in purchase-right states. Both thin |

**Why the cap level matters (the central number).** The covenant's cost is almost entirely the cap. Max bid per site for a buyer targeting 10% gross levered (about 7.5-8% net after fees), 7.2% debt, capex on, exit +100 bp, expense growth 3.5% / 5.0% [D]:

| Covenant | Max bid | vs institutional buyer (12% hurdle, $79.4k) | vs private-equity buyer (15% hurdle, $71.5k) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3% cap | $61.5k / $55.9k | -22.5% / -29.5% | -14.0% / -21.8% |
| 4% cap | $70.3k / $64.7k | -11.4% / -18.5% | -1.7% / -9.5% |
| 5% ceiling | $79.8k / $74.2k | +0.6% / -6.5% | +11.6% / +3.8% |

Each point of cap is worth about 9-10% of price. A 5% ceiling costs about 0.5 point of growth against the 5.2-5.6% REITs report, so it is close to free. Tier A therefore achieves price parity by being nearly the market. A tenure-based cap (cap in-place residents, reset to market on home sale) adds only about 0.1-0.2 point of growth, so I dropped it as a fix [D].

**Capital-side payment closes only part of a deep cap.** At a 4% cap, putting 30% of the debt at 3% adds about 5 points of price. A $4k-a-site grant adds about 5.5. Both together close about 10 of the 11-18 point gap. The parity subsidy for a 4% cap is $4.6k-$14.7k a site depending on LP hurdle, or **$66M-$210M across 14,300 sites** [D]. Dedicated money that reaches for-profits: Minnesota awarded $43.9M since 2020, NY about $24M, and federal PRICE has $0 new money for FY2026 with elimination proposed for FY2027 [gate, dossier]. A deep covenant cannot be the product at scale; it can be a minority tier.

## 3. Answers to the critiques

**Critique 1 (outbid; mission lane taken; statute commoditizes; small parks, no agency debt).**
- Conceded: FG Communities wears the label for free; outbidding beats a covenant; where caps are statutory the covenant adds nothing (Tier A is about WA's 5%); Freddie's sponsor rule and stabilized-asset test exclude a new sponsor and the small, deferred-maintenance parks. Dealing with that, I moved the debt to community banks and credit unions.
- Corrected: MHAP does admit for-profit acquirers who partner for experience (section 1). That does not rescue scale, because the pool is about 400 pads.
- Unanswered, and fatal to the moat: nothing stops FG Communities, Sun or an Apollo fund from adopting Tier A. A standard covenant is a marketing line.

**Critique 2 (nobody pays; ICS died; no investment-committee slot; 5% of national sales).**
- Conceded on all four. Residents pay nothing extra, sellers pick certainty (Fox Run: $2.6M beat a $2.64M co-op offer; Cascade Acres: a 20% overbid), and the only documented budget line is the state. I split the covenant so the sellers and LPs are not asked to pay, and the state pays only for Tier B.
- New structural finding. **Scale lives in 100-500 site parks, where institutions set the price (Havenpark, Mesa at $89k a site). A capped buyer wins only 30-100 site parks, where $100M means 350-480 deals.** The covenant is relevant only where scale does not exist.
- The ICS result kills the interim-owner variant of the idea. I dropped it.

**Critique 3 (rates; no equity buyer; lender documents; operating liability).**
- Conceded: at 7.2% debt, the CPI-linked structures earn 3-10% and are unfinanceable. I removed CPI-linked caps. Freddie's no-purchase-option rule means the resident right to buy must sit outside the lease; with community banks that is negotiable, with agencies it is a document risk.
- Equity: the real small-park channel is retail, 1031/DST and preferred (FG Communities has $123M of paid-in capital at end-2025, with a listed parent behind it). It carries loads and is cyclical, but it exists. The pension and insurer bucket for a first-time sponsor has no MHC example.
- Operating liability: the $250 a site capex line is wrong for infrastructure-stressed parks. I restrict buys to parks on municipal water and sewer, or with a clean sanitary survey, and budget $1-2k a site for the first three years [A].

## 4. Scale paths

Equity is about $22k a site (65% LTV on a $58.4k median price, plus costs) [D from Northmarq median]. Revenue per site: $5.0-5.75k in small Northeast parks (US median about $5k in 2021, Maine $5,750); FG Communities' Carolinas parks imply about $3.5-4.5k [D, site counts by year not disclosed]; Sun's base is $9.2k.

| Case | Assumptions | Sites | Revenue | Equity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Median (conditional on a first fund closing) | Hired operator, deal-by-deal syndication plus a $25-30M first fund, about 250 sites a year after year 2, $6.2k a site | ~1,300 by year 7 | **~$8M** | ~$29M |
| Upside | Seasoned operator as co-founder; FG Communities' pace (~900 sites a year) sustained 8 years; 5% ceiling holds price parity; three equity raises; rents +4.5% a year to ~$8.2k | ~7,450 by year 11 | **~$60M** | ~$165M at $22k a site; ~$300M at FG Communities' actual ratio (~$40k paid-in per site, including cash on hand) |
| $100M | Pace of 1,500+ sites a year for 8 years, about 1.6x the only comparable in this niche, and $265M+ of equity | ~12,000 | $100M | ~$265M |

Sponsor economics: a $320M equity fund pays the sponsor roughly $5M a year in fees plus carry. The company's own revenue is a small fraction of consolidated rent. Joint probability of the $100M row (price parity, operator partner, anchor equity, 1.6x pace, two more fund closes, stable rates), by my estimate: low single digits [A].

## 5. Why it still dies

1. A heavy covenant has no payer at scale, and a light covenant has no moat. Both halves of the original pitch fail the lessons' tests (wrong payer; incumbents with free substitutes).
2. The covenant and scale sit in opposite park sizes (section 3).
3. The one comparable in this exact niche, with a listed parent, a CEO who has overseen about 95 communities, and the same mission language, is at roughly 3,100-4,000 sites after four years, with $9.6M of 2025 rental income and roughly $13M annualized. $100M is 7-8x that, and a new sponsor starts without the team, the parent or the track record.
4. It is a real-estate sponsor, not a scalable company. The founder's value is fees and carry on someone else's rent roll.

## 6. Riskiest assumption and the 2-week test

**Riskiest assumption.** A seller or broker with a market bid in hand will accept a Tier A covenant bid at no more than 3% below it. If not, the covenant has no payer even in its cheapest form, and nothing else matters.

**Test (14 days, no capital, no unfunded LOIs, no misrepresenting capacity).**
- Days 1-4: build a list of 15 live listings and 25 owner contacts for 40-150 site parks in NY, ME and one uncapped state, from the NY registry, Maine's license list and broker pages.
- Days 5-10: call 20 brokers or sellers. State plainly that you are forming a sponsor. Read three hypothetical bids: A, $100, no covenant, 60-day close; B, $97, Tier A covenant, resident-association waiver of purchase rights signed up front; C, $90, deep covenant. Record the choice and ask for the last two sales where the seller took a lower bid for non-price reasons (buyer, number of closes, discount).
- Days 8-14: ask 10 residents' associations (NY and Maine) whether they would sign a purchase-right waiver for Tier A. Ask 6 allocators and DST sponsors whether a fund with a 5% ceiling at 10% gross, about 7.5% net, gets a meeting. Request answers by email.
- **Pass:** at least 3 of 20 sellers or brokers pick B, or name two closed deals at a non-price discount to a for-profit; at least 3 of 10 associations offer to sign a waiver; at least 2 of 6 allocators ask for a deck. **Kill:** fewer than 3 of 20 pick B and no closed example.

## 7. What would resurrect it

Any of: (a) three written seller or broker commitments at no more than 3% below the best bid outside NY and Maine; (b) a $50M+ anchor commitment at 10% gross or lower from a non-concessionary allocator, plus a named operator co-founder who satisfies Freddie's two-year rule; (c) appropriated federal money for PRICE-type covenants or a Duty to Serve final rule that pays for rent caps; (d) a named takeout buyer for the resident buyouts at a scale well above ROC USA's roughly $26M a year. Without (a) and (b) in 60 days, stop.

## 8. What survives, and it is not this company

A 3-8 park subsidized program ($1-6M of rent) run with a seasoned operator, or a role as acquisitions and impact lead inside an existing operator or fund. Neither reaches $100M, and I do not present either as the business.

## Not verified
