# C02 refined: P061 "Qualified-challenger fiscal agent: buy the ticket in choice markets, bid the statewide auctions later"

Written 2026-10-18 after three serious critiques. Marks: (S) read in a source (fetched, or via search-result summary, noted); (A) my arithmetic or assumption; (U) unverified. New facts from this session are in section 12.

## 1. Verdict

**Alive, but narrowly. It is a gated roll-up option, not a clean startup.** Roughly 1-in-10 odds of the $100M case, median about $25M of fee revenue at 5-8% margin (A). The original pitch (30% under the incumbent, a payroll-certainty SLA as the price lever, Kentucky/Ohio as wedge, 10-15% margin) is dead: all three critics are right on it and I drop it. What survives is a different business that happens to share the same pool.

Three things I will not claim any more:
- That the SLA earns a price premium. No state pays for it; Wisconsin and Connecticut treat late payroll as a penalty clause.
- That technology is a moat. HHAX (Annkissam, Sandata, CarePay), DCI and CYMA sell the workflow to every competitor.
- That 10-15% margin exists at the competitive-bid price of $68-83. The only public cost data (Wisconsin, 2025) shows -12.5% across four operators and +2% for the best one.

What I keep: the money is mandated, a named government or plan signer pays it, there is no free substitute outside California, and the scarce asset in this market is not software but **qualification** (multi-state past performance, a clean payroll record, state certification, credit capacity). In New York reportedly only 4 of 130+ bidders qualified (S, dossier, secondary). The refined business is built to acquire that asset cheaply and then spend it.

If the two-week test in section 9 fails, the answer flips to dead.

## 2. The redesign in one paragraph

Do not start where the critics hit hardest (winner-take-all statewide RFPs against sponsor-backed incumbents). Start where entry is by **enrollment, rate card or plan choice**, buy one to three owner-operated or nonprofit fiscal agents there to obtain the qualification, run them on rented software with a measured exception-rate program, and only then bid mid-size statewide RFPs and plan-delegated contracts, using audited payroll accuracy as past performance instead of a priced guarantee. Never front payroll.

## 3. The refined business

- **Wedge customer.** Participant-employers and their service coordinators or support brokers in choice markets, paid for by the state or plan. Evidence the market is open:
  - Texas: HHSC takes FMSA applications year-round with no deadline; FMSAs contract with the MCOs; members pick one from a county choice list (S, search summary). Rate $114.40 (S, dossier, search summary).
  - Minnesota CFSS: 13 FMS providers listed on the DHS site (S, nav of a fetched page; not a verified enrollment count), fees set by each provider, $165-250 a month flat, tiered up to $400-1,000 for large budgets (S, search summary of DHS pages). Nearly 50,000 people use services that could become CFSS (S, DHS slide, extracted).
  - Colorado: members can change FMS contractor quarterly; rate $103.21 (S, vendor site; dossier).
  - New Jersey: each MCO picks its own fiscal intermediary (Horizon chose Palco, 2025) (S, search summary of state pages). Pennsylvania CHC: plans delegated to HHAX, which subcontracts Tempus (S, search summary).
- **Payer and signer.** The payer is the state Medicaid or developmental-disability agency (rate card) or the MCO (delegated). Nobody signs a bid in an enrollment market: the signers are the state or plan provider-enrollment unit and, for each acquisition, the seller-owner. The Medicaid director signs only later, for statewide RFPs. This is the main change from the original, which needed a director's signature in an 18-24 month cycle on day one.
- **First product.** An acquired operating fiscal agent (target 2-6k participants, 3+ years clean record, owner or nonprofit seller), not software. In parallel, enroll de novo in one open-enrollment state as a cheap read on referral-channel acquisition cost.
- **Pricing.** Take the rate the state or plan sets; do not discount to win.

| Channel | Price PMPM | Cost PMPM needed | Margin target | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Budget-authority / choice (MN, CA SDP, TX, CO, LA) | $103-165, up to $250 | $85-125 | 15-25% gross | price (S); cost (U) |
| Plan-delegated personal care (NJ, PA, TX) | $100-115 | $85-92 | 10-15% | price (S); cost (U) |
| Statewide RFP, only once qualified | $77-83 | at or under $72 | 5-12% | price (S); only Wisconsin cost data exists |
| New York slice (option, not planned) | $68.50 | unknown | 0-5% | option only |

- **Payroll rule (replaces the SLA).** Default is a prefunded model: the payer releases funds into the disbursement account before payroll, as Wisconsin's contract does (S, critic 3 read it). Where a plan pays in arrears, either require prefunding (New York's plans prefunded PPL when it said it lacked cash) or price the float as a line item. Cost of an unpriced 40-day float at 8% is about $26 PMPM on a $36k participant (A), a third of an $80 fee, so it cannot be absorbed. The only credit line is a small transition tail (about $5-10M, A) for the first 60 days of a new book. Any guarantee covers state-authorized hours only, with the payer owning authorization-file accuracy (Connecticut shows GT blaming DSS files; S, critic 2).
- **Sequencing.**
  1. Months 0-3: run the section 9 test; recruit a named FMS operator as co-founder and a state-procurement lead; line up a healthcare-services sponsor or family-office equity for the first buy.
  2. Year 1: close one acquisition in an enrollment state (asset deal, escrow, 3504 and wage-hour diligence). Enroll de novo in a second.
  3. Years 2-3: two more small buys in different states; 15-20k participants in 4-6 states; 24 months of audited payroll-accuracy metrics; one plan-delegated contract.
  4. Years 4-6: bid mid-size statewide RFPs (10-25k participants) as a qualified bidder. Kentucky (due Nov 6 2026) is not bid; it is a free read on scoring weights and incumbents.
  5. Years 5-7: statewide wins plus any multi-FI slot New York opens. Exit to a sponsor is the realistic liquidity event (H.I.G. and General Atlantic show buyers exist).

## 4. Why now (re-tested, honest)

- **Policy is swinging toward choice.** DOJ called New York's single-FI award a "sham procurement" (Jun 16 2026); Wisconsin audit co-chairs are questioning its PPL award; New York bills would restore multiple FIs; Alabama multi-awards with participant choice; Minnesota is moving to a multi-vendor model (S, critics and dossier). Each opens slots that are won by enrollment, not by prior statewide experience. I cannot call this reliable: none of the New York bills is confirmed enacted.
- **Self-direction keeps growing and is shifting to budget authority.** The 2023 inventory: 1,520,267 people, +23% since 2019, 44 states up and 7 down, six states more than doubled (AL, CT, DC, FL, MD, SD), and the biggest growth is in states offering budget authority (S, extracted from the NCI deck). Counter-signal: Pennsylvania's own workgroup reported a significant reduction in self-direction use (S, search summary).
- **Wardley: the software layer just became a commodity.** EVV-to-payroll is shipping at HHAX; an FI no longer needs to build it. What was left scarce is operations, qualification and capital. This is why the plan rents technology and spends on operators and acquisitions.
- **Not claimed as why-now:** the New York day-one failure (cuts against new entrants), Kentucky's RFP (cannot be assembled in 19 days), or the 80/20 rule.

## 5. Moat (7 Powers), honestly scored

- **Primary: cornered resource, the qualification stack.** Multi-state past performance, audited first-payroll accuracy, state certifications, bonding and credit capacity. This is what kept most New York bidders out. It is reached at roughly 5+ states, 15-20k participants and 24 months of audited metrics (year 3-4). It is held by contracts, not by technology, and it decays if contracts are lost.
- **Secondary: counter-positioning** against sole-vendor consolidators (PPL's pitch is one vendor, one price). It holds only while the choice and multi-vendor trend holds.
- **Scale economies: unproven.** Wisconsin's largest operator (about 14.8k) cost more per head than the best (about 8.5k); New York's $68.50 implies scale at 200k+ but is unaudited. The plan does not depend on scale lowering cost; it depends on an ops program that the first acquisition will measure.
- **Switching costs: weak at state level (states do switch), moderate at participant level in choice markets.** Not a moat claim.
- Net: **moderate at best**, and only after year 3. Anyone telling you this business has a deep moat is selling it.

## 6. Bottom-up market and cases

**Pool.** About 540,000 self-directing people outside California and New York, an upper bound that includes veteran-directed and non-FMS programs (A, dossier); New York about 209-250k. Fee pool about $0.7-0.9B a year (A, dossier). **$100M is 11-14% of the entire US fee pool**, which is the honest share needed. The premium/choice segment is **unsized**: I found no participant counts for Texas CDS, Colorado CDASS or Minnesota's budget model. If that segment is 150-250k people at about $150, it is a $270-450M pool and the $50M premium line below is 11-19% of it (A, labelled assumption).

**Upside case, year 7: about $103M on 79k participants, blended about $108 PMPM (A).**

| Segment | Participants | PMPM | Annual revenue | Reachable payers |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Budget-authority / choice | 28,000 | $150 | $50.4M | 8-12 state agencies and their MCOs |
| Plan-delegated personal care | 15,000 | $105 | $18.9M | 6-10 plans or plan families (NJ, PA, TX, others) |
| Two mid-size statewide RFP wins | 24,000 | $82 | $23.6M | 2 state agencies out of roughly 6-10 rebids in the window |
| New York or equivalent multi-FI slice | 12,000 | $68.50 | $9.9M | 1 state |
| **Total** | **79,000** | **$108** | **$102.8M** | |

Named assumptions: (1) premium fees hold at about $140+ and cost lets them earn at least 15%; (2) the company reaches 1.6x GT's current footprint (GT: "more than 50,000" in 18 states plus DC after about 21 years, S); (3) it wins two of the roughly one competitive statewide award per year nationally (critic 2's count) while qualified; (4) a New York slot opens (receivership, rebid or multi-FI statute); (5) acquisition capital of about $60-75M is available (A). Drop assumption 4 and the case is about $93M; drop assumption 3 and it is about $80M.

**Median case, year 7: about $25M at 5-8% EBITDA margin (A).** 9k choice participants at $140 ($15.1M), 4k plan-delegated at $100 ($4.8M), one 8k statewide win at $80 ($7.7M) = $27.6M, less about 10% for participant attrition from state cuts and integration slippage. EBITDA is about $1.3-2M. That is a real but small business and not a venture outcome by itself.

**Probabilities (A):** upside about 8-10%; median band about 45%; bear (cannot buy at a workable price, aborts at the gate, or stalls under $10M) about 40-45%. The dossier's own scenarios were 10% / 50% / 40%.

## 7. Unit economics and capital

- Fee is about 1.8-2% of spend (A; Wisconsin $76.50 x 12 vs about $51.7k). Everything is a thin-margin spread business.
- Regulatory capital is small: Wisconsin requires working capital of at least 2% of revenue plus a restricted reserve of 8% down to 1% by tier; about $4M at $100M revenue (S, dossier).
- Acquisition capital is the real need. If acquired revenue is $35-45M at no more than 1.0x revenue, that is $35-45M, plus about $4M reserves, about $1-2M per statewide bid, and the transition tail. Total about $60-75M of equity and term debt over 5-6 years (A). This is sponsor-style capital, not seed capital.
- Liability: Section 3504 agents are jointly and severally liable for each participant-employer's payroll taxes (S, IRS); Calderon settled for $162M at PPL. Mitigation: asset deals, indemnity escrow, representation-and-warranty insurance, Form 2678 and wage-hour diligence, and no co-employer (agency-with-choice) model in the first 24 months.
- If margin on the first acquisition is under 5% after a year of the ops program, the "buy and fix" thesis is wrong and the plan stops.

| Tempus | Nonprofit, MA and PA | Not a margin analog |
| Consumer Direct Care Network | 14 states, moved to an employee ownership trust | Not for sale |

**Honest edge.** There is no structural edge over GT/H.I.G. if they chase the same small operators. The edge is thin and human: (1) proprietary relationships with owner-operators who will not sell into a sponsor auction, which is why a seasoned FMS co-founder is a precondition; (2) no dependence on one state; (3) a measured exception-rate and onboarding program on every acquired book; (4) counter-positioning for the choice wave. Plan-delegated revenue is capped in my mix because HHAX can intermediate it.

## 9. Riskiest assumption and the two-week test

**Riskiest assumption.** Small, non-sponsor-owned fiscal agents exist in choice and rate-card markets that (a) earn at least 15-20% gross margin at $110+ PMPM and (b) will sell at a price their cash flow can carry (about 1.0x revenue, about 6x EBITDA, both A). Everything else (the ticket, the ladder to statewide RFPs, the upside) rides on being able to buy qualification cheaply. No FMS deal price has been found by me or by any critic.

**Test, two weeks, no capital.**
1. Days 1-2: build a list of 25 owner-operated or nonprofit FMS operators from the Minnesota DHS FMS provider pages, Texas HHSC FMSA choice lists and MCO member lists, and the Colorado, Michigan and Virginia lists. Exclude GT, Acumen/TEAM, PPL and Consumer Direct Care Network. Pull Form 990s for the nonprofits (revenue, program cost, staff).
2. Days 2-12: contact all 25; aim for 8-10 conversations plus 2 healthcare-services M&A advisers. Ask six fixed questions: participants and states; revenue PMPM; cost PMPM; EBITDA margin; any 3504 or wage-hour claims; whether they would entertain an offer and at what multiple they have been told.
3. Days 12-14: build the table and apply the thresholds written down in advance.
   - **Pass:** at least 3 operators with usable numbers show cost at or under $90 PMPM at $110+ revenue, and at least 2 would consider a sale at or under 1.0x revenue.
   - **Kill (alive becomes false):** median ask above 1.5x revenue, or reported margins under 5% at $110+ PMPM, or fewer than 5 of 25 will share any numbers.
   - Between the two: shrink the premium-segment assumption, rerun the cases, and decide.

Second gate at about month 6: a signed LOI with a seller at or under 1.0x revenue, plus a committed equity partner. Without both, stop.

## 10. Pre-mortem (it is 2028 and this is dead)

1. **Could not buy at a workable price.** Sellers anchored on the TEAM and GT headlines; the only sellable books were the money-losing ones. Fixable only by the section 9 test, which is why it comes first.
2. **Bought a book and cost-out failed.** Scale did not lower cost (Wisconsin shows it may not), margin stuck at 2-5%, and the capital could not be returned. Fixable by an earn-out tied to retained participants and a stop rule at 12 months.
3. **Policy shock.** Federal restrictions on paid family caregivers (a stated target; HHS Secretary remarks are search-summary only) or state cuts (Maryland, Missouri, Idaho, Indiana, Colorado proposals) shrink participants 20-30%. Not fixable, only diversified across states and populations. This also caps the median case.
4. A fourth cause, a funded competitor buying the same tail first, is the likeliest reason the buy-side edge disappears.

## 11. How each critique was addressed

**Critique 1 (entry ticket; technology not a moat; SLA is table stakes; incumbents answer on price; prize locked and small).**
- Entry ticket and 10x sponsors: conceded. Moved the buy to owner-operated and nonprofit operators in enrollment markets, with a hard price ceiling and a test that can kill the idea. Not buying Wisconsin's losers.
- Technology: conceded fully; software is rented and no moat is claimed.
- SLA: conceded; it is demoted from price lever to scoring evidence, and the guarantee is limited to authorized hours.
- Price response: moved off competitive-bid price wars into rate-card and choice channels where an incumbent cannot underbid, and entered auctions only when cost is proven.
- Locked prize: New York removed from the base case; the upside survives without it at about $93M.
- Not solved: acquisition price is still unknown; H.I.G. could take the same tail.

**Critique 2 (SLA unbackable; the sector loses money at commodity price; reachable volume and cycle time; targets not for sale; NY closed and demand under attack).**
- Unbackable SLA: conceded. Prefunded model by default, float priced, no steady-state credit line.
- Loss-making sector: conceded; the plan cannot work at $68-83 and does not rely on it. Margin in the base case is 5-8%, not 10-15%. The premium-price segment is the bet, and its cost side is unproven.
- Cycle time and Kentucky: conceded; Kentucky is not bid. Statewide RFPs move to years 4-6.
- Targets not for sale: partly conceded. PE-held and EOT-held targets are excluded; whether others sell is the test.
- Not solved: demand-side policy risk, the size of the premium segment, and Wisconsin's unresolved protest (no outcome found as of this session).

**Critique 3 (timing; SLA solves a problem the analog lacks; revocable license; consolidation backlash and liability; weak wedge and price anchor).**
- Timing: accepted; no near-term RFP is bid.
- SLA and payroll float: conceded; Wisconsin's prefunded structure is adopted.
- Revocable license: conceded. One-year, 90-day-termination contracts (Wisconsin) are not an asset. The asset is the qualification stack, which is why the plan sells the idea to no one on "switching costs".
- Consolidation backlash: turned into the counter-positioning leg, with the caveat that no New York bill is confirmed.
- Liability: addressed with deal structure and diligence, not eliminated.
- Wedge and price anchor: EVV-equals-timesheet is dropped as a wedge; $68.50 is treated as unreliable.

**What I did not accept.** The claim that no premium-fee market exists. Minnesota's provider-set $165-250 fees and the rate-card states show it does; whether it is big enough is the open question.
