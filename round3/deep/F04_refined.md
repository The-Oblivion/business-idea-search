# F04 Refined: Contract Renewal Watchdog (Florida condo/HOA)

**Verdict: alive = false.** Both critics are right on the points that decide it. The part of the service that makes money is the licensed part. The part that is unlicensed is free or $99 a month. No form of this business, as a service, a software product, or a data asset, gets to $10M a year without heroic share of a market where free and funded competitors already sit. I am not shrinking it into a boutique to keep it alive. The one thing that survives is a small, capped test, described below, and Asher's 10-15 hours a week are better spent selling the freight audit he has already built.

Date of this analysis: 25 Oct 2026.

---

## 1. What I checked myself this session (versus what I am taking from the critics)

Verified by me:

- **Fla. Stat. 468.431(2)** (flsenate.gov, 2025 text). Paid "community association management" applies when the association has more than 10 units or a budget over $100,000. The listed practices include "negotiating monetary or performance terms of a contract subject to approval by an association" and preparing budgets or other financial documents. The only exclusion is clerical/ministerial work under a licensed manager's direct supervision, or maintenance-only work with no part in the listed services. Nothing in the section names or exempts consultants. Both critics read this correctly. DBPR's own hot-topics and FAQ pages say nothing about consultants either, so the risk is untested, not cleared.
- **FirstService benchmarking guide, Vol. 3.0.** Copyright 2021, so FY2021 data. I parsed the PDF. For Miami high-rises (60 properties, 12,658 units, so about 211 units each and professionally managed), per unit per year: total budget $13,252; elevator $239; landscaping and pest $245; trash $176; cable, internet and phone $693; electricity $732 (not negotiable). The negotiable lines excluding electricity total $1,353 per unit, or 10.2% of budget. Without cable they total $660, or 5.0%.
- **Fannie/Freddie reserve floor.** The most recent coverage I found (21 Oct 2026) still shows 4 Jan 2027 as the effective date. FHFA has resisted delay requests. A fully funded professional reserve study can substitute for the 15% allocation. So the why-now is real, and it acts on reserves, not on vendor contracts.
- **Inframark** (a Florida management company) sells a "Vendor Contract Oversight" service whose listed tasks include tracking vendor timelines and renewal activity. The page gives no price or negotiation detail and does not say whether it is bundled. This is a new incumbent the dossier missed. It shows managers already sell the renewal-tracking half.

Taken from the critics' fetches and not re-read by me: 468.4335, 468.4334, 468.432 and 468.436; the 2015 Florida Supreme Court-approved unlicensed-practice opinion; the FirstService procurement page; CAMbrands, ProVendorConnect, FolSon; the budget-calendar rules. I treat these as likely right and say so where I rely on them.

## 2. Answers to the critiques

### Critic 1

**1. Commodity or licensed.** Conceded in full. I looked for a third path and the statute text does not offer one. The preamble requires "substantial specialized knowledge, judgment, and managerial skill," which supports an argument that a report-only product the board acts on itself is outside the definition. Only a Florida association attorney's written opinion or a DBPR declaratory statement (binds only the petitioner) can bless that, and that design is the commodity half: Renewly from $99 a month with a free tier, a spreadsheet with calendar alerts, a chatbot on a PDF, and free law-firm advice. The CAM license is also not the fix. It adds a firm license and the 468.4335 conflict presumption (agenda item, contract copies, two-thirds vote, multiple bids above $2,500). The pitch's "$650 license" was wrong, as the critic says.

**2. Revenue per client is $1-2k, not $4k.** Conceded with one adjustment. Using the verified table: a 55-unit building (CAI's Florida average is 1,528,611 units over 27,537 condo associations, about 55) at Miami high-rise per-unit costs has a budget near $729k and negotiable lines near $74k. A 10% saving is $7.4k and a 40% share is $3.0k. Without the cable line, those figures are $3.6k and $1.5k. Cable and internet is the largest negotiable line, and bulk telecom is typically sold through vendor-paid brokers, which is the arrangement the conflict-free pitch rejects. Miami high-rise per-unit costs are also the top of the range. Real answer: $1-3k a year, with $3k only if everything goes right. The 10% saving assumption itself still rests on vendor and practitioner anecdotes. The gate's $4k was not supported.

**3. The clients he can reach cannot pay.** Conceded as a plausible inference. The dossier's best segment (self-managed, 30-40% nationally per the FCAR review) has no size data, and practitioners say they skew small. The large buildings in the FirstService sample average about 211 units and are managed, so a manager stands in the way, and FirstService, Inframark and Associa already run vendor programs. Neither the mentor (audio-visual integration) nor the family has a documented association-market network, and the gate could not name a single building.

**4. Free board-facing bid tools already hold the wedge.** Conceded. ProVendorConnect is free to boards and vendor-funded. CAMbrands is free to boards and bundles a licensed CAM consult. Condoly, Condo Control and Condominium Associates also offer bid collection. A board compares "free" with "about 40% of savings." Being conflict-free is the pitch, and it loses on price.

**5. The only proof of the model shows a small ceiling.** Conceded. FolSon has run since 2014 in the densest co-op market in the US and claims "dozens of projects" and hundreds of thousands of dollars of savings in total. Its fee terms were last public in 2023. The Schooley Mitchell FDD works out to roughly $19M of secured revenue for the entire US network (dossier arithmetic). 2,500 to 10,000 associations would need on the order of 100 times FolSon's cumulative output.

**6. The payable metric is the weakest one.** Conceded. "Avoided increases" is a counterfactual with no defensible baseline, and a perpetual share contradicts a pitch that attacks auto-renewals. Volunteer boards turn over, and the next board inherits a fee it did not choose. The only defensible metric is rebid price versus signed price, and producing that means running the rebid, which is the licensed act. Any surviving fee has to be flat and fixed in advance.

**7. The data asset has no buyer.** Conceded. FirstService publishes benchmarks free from about 1,700 communities and 360,000 units. PayHOA ($27.5M Series A, invoice OCR), Vantaca, Condo Control and CINC already hold the invoices. A few hundred contracts from free reviews is not a competing asset. Group buying only works with vendor-paid fees, which break the conflict-free pitch and invite kickback scrutiny.

**8. Why-now cuts both ways.** Conceded. The reserve floor is real and still scheduled, but boards squeezed by reserves raise assessments or buy a reserve study; their biggest pain lines are insurance (off-limits to him, and easing) and reserves. The 2026 kickback arrests make "no vendor money" credible but also make every director wary of anyone paid by vendor choice. A 2024 conflict-of-interest law (HB 1021) adds a further hurdle for anyone paid a share of savings (search summary only, not read in full).

### Critic 2

**1. License catch-22.** Conceded, and the statute confirms it. Unlicensed, the paid service sits inside 468.431(2). Licensed, he hits 468.432 (firm license, designated CAM), 468.4335 (conflict presumption) and 468.4334 (agent duties, mandatory contract language, records returned in 20 business days at $1,000 a day in penalties). The only clean exemption is holding another Florida license (attorney, CPA, engineer). A lawyer partner does not solve it economically, because a lawyer generally cannot share legal fees with a nonlawyer (Florida Bar Rule 4-5.4, not verified this session; confirm with counsel). I also found no sign anyone has tested a contingent consultant against DBPR, so the safe assumption is that nobody is allowed to find out cheaply.

**2. Unlicensed practice of law and liability.** Conceded. Extracting a date is ministerial. Deciding whether a notice is timely, whether a contract auto-renewed, or drafting a non-renewal letter is legal analysis. If the AI or Asher misses a window, the association is locked into a multi-year contract (the dossier's example is a fire contract at $27k a year) against a fee of $1-3k. An E&O insurer is unlikely to cover unlicensed practice. The only legally clean form is a tool the board operates with no conclusions, which is the $99 box.

**3. Thin, back-loaded economics.** Conceded. At $1-3k a year per client, 8-15 hours of onboarding each (the critic's estimate; plausible), and an attorney opinion before the first invoice, year one is a few thousand dollars at best. At 10-15 hours a week, onboarding capacity is roughly 40 clients a year even if every hour went to it, so revenue is capped near $40-120k a year on the best day. The dossier's "under $1,000" test left out the attorney opinion.

**4. Fee model hard to defend to a volunteer board.** Conceded (see Critic 1, point 6). Add: members can inspect association contracts, so directors must justify a permanent fee on non-events at an owner meeting; verification needs continuous access to invoices and financials, which pulls toward "preparing financial documents."

**5. Timing claim mostly wrong.** Conceded. A calendar-year association must mail its proposed budget 14 days before the budget meeting and adopt it at least 14 days before the fiscal year, so by about 18 Dec 2026 (critic's reading of the Becker and Cornell pages; I did not read them in full). Intros, document collection and a review cannot move the 2027 budget. Real savings arrive contract by contract through 2027 as 60-90 day notice windows hit, so verified-savings revenue lands in 2028, and any fee on it is exactly what boards dispute.

**6. Gatekeepers and trust.** Conceded as inference, consistent with his stated weakness that regulated buyers will not take him seriously. Boards just read about the 2026 kickback arrests (Greynolds Park, Venetian Gardens, a $5.8M five-association case). Asking them to give an unlicensed 18-year-old every contract and invoice is a hard sell, and the association attorney is paid to be the skeptic.

**7. Competition closes the wedge faster than he can open it.** Conceded. Vantaca (HOAi Fleet, Vantaca Vendor), PayHOA, Condo Control, ProVendorConnect, Renewly and now Inframark's oversight service. No one I found documents contract-reading renewal alerts in an HOA suite, so the gap is real but narrow, and the incumbents hold the invoice data and the manager relationships. A suite can add this feature in a quarter.

**8. Incentive and safety risk.** Conceded. A share-of-savings fee rewards pushing the cheapest vendor on elevators, fire and life-safety, pools and security. He cannot insure against a lapse. Any surviving version excludes life-safety lines and uses flat fees.

**9. Opportunity cost.** Conceded, and this is the deciding point. The freight-invoice audit is built, has no license wall, and is waiting only on outreach calls. It uses the same diagnostic-then-contingency structure, with a $500 flat front-end and 20% of recovery. F04's best case is $1-3k a year per client after a legal opinion; spending hours here instead of on freight calls is a bad trade.

## 3. Every route to big, tested and killed

| Route | Bottom-up math | Why it dies |
|---|---|---|
| Contingency services, unlicensed | $10M needs 3,300 to 10,000 associations at $1-3k a year. Capacity at 10-15 hours a week is about 40 clients a year. | Inside the license definition. FolSon took 12 years to claim dozens of projects. The Schooley Mitchell network is about $19M nationally after decades. |
| Contingency services, licensed CAM firm | Same count, plus a firm license and a designated CAM. | The conflict presumption, two-thirds vote, multiple bids and agent duties apply to every vendor he proposes. He would compete with FirstService, Associa and Inframark as a one-person firm. |
| Report-only, flat fee, attorney-blessed | $750 flat at the top of the price anchors (PayHOA $49-54 a month, Renewly $99). 8 clients is about $6k. | Commodity. Free substitutes. No recurring revenue. A side income, not a business. |
| Board-operated software | $10M needs 8,300 payers at $100 a month (16% of Florida's 50,600 associations), or about 17,000 at $49. | Renewal alerts are a feature. PayHOA bundles payables at $49-54 a month. Suites can copy it. Boards are volunteers and churn. Realistic best case is about $1.5M ARR (1,250 associations at $100 a month, 2.5% of Florida). |
| Data asset, benchmarks, group buying | Needs thousands of contracts before it has value. | FirstService already publishes per-door benchmarks for free from about 1,700 communities. Vendor-paid group buying breaks the pitch. No payer found. |
| Sell the tool to attorneys, CPAs or engineers | Perhaps a few hundred association law firms in Florida (my estimate, unverified) at a few thousand a year gives $1-2M at most. | Small count, and none will buy an unlicensed-practice-adjacent tool from an unknown teen. |
| Sell to management companies | Large buyers. | They are the gatekeeper with their own programs, and they will not buy cold from an 18-year-old. |

## 4. What survives and what to salvage

- **Survives as a small side income:** a flat-fee, report-only contract review for self-managed Florida associations, structured with an association attorney, with no negotiation, no letters on the board's behalf, no vendor money, and no life-safety lines. Realistic outcome: $5-15k over two years. It does not meet the "path to big" bar, so it is not a business for this search.
- **Salvage into the freight audit:** the contract-and-tariff extraction pipeline, the diagnostic-first pricing, and the "no vendor money" positioning. None need Florida.
- **Do not salvage:** a data business, group buying, any perpetual share of savings, or the CAM license.

## 5. If Asher still wants to run it: a capped 30-day test (3 hours a week, 12 hours total)

Everything else stays on freight audit.

- **Days 1-7 (about 3 hours).**
  - Write a spreadsheet of at least 10 real Florida buildings with a named board contact, drawn from family, the mentor's network and friends' parents. Output: the list. If he cannot reach 10, the idea is dead.
  - Book two paid 30-minute consults with Florida association attorneys, budget $500-1,000 total. One question: can an unlicensed person charge a flat fee for a written report on a board's vendor contracts that the board uses itself, without 468.431(2) or unlicensed-practice exposure, and what structure and disclaimer would they require? Output: two written answers. Either "no" ends it.
- **Days 8-14 (about 3 hours).** Run three free report-only reviews of real contracts from the warmest boards, on an engagement letter the attorney has reviewed. Report: the five contracts that move the budget, notice windows that fall within the next 180 days, and a sourced estimate of overpayment against the FirstService benchmark. Output: three reports.
- **Days 15-21 (about 3 hours).** Deliver and interview the three treasurers. Ask: would you pay $500-750 flat, would your board sign a savings-share letter, who else on your board would need to approve? Output: three interview notes and any signed letters of intent.
- **Days 22-30 (about 3 hours).** One-page scorecard. Go only if all four hold: the attorney opinion is favorable, at least 3 of 5 contacted boards sign a paid letter of intent, median savings found is above $2k a year per association, and Asher can name 20 more buildings. Otherwise kill it and write the freight-audit call count for the month.

## 7. What would revive it

All of these, not any one:

- A written attorney or DBPR position that paid, report-only review outside 468.431(2) is allowed, with a structure that still lets the report be useful.
- Three or more Florida boards paying a flat fee of at least $750 without a manager's objection.
- A proven buyer for contract-level benchmarks (a lender, insurer, or management company paying real money).
- A recurring revenue model with churn under 15% a year that does not rely on a share of savings.
